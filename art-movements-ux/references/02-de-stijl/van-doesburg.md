# Theo van Doesburg — De Stijl

## Essence
Van Doesburg kept Mondrian's vocabulary of flat primaries, black bars and white ground, then rotated it 45° against the architecture it sat on. The result, a "new polarity," is always two systems in friction, never one at rest. Elementarism treats tension, time and the moving viewer as material in their own right.

## Key works examined
- **Geometric alphabet, 1919**: monoline capitals on a 5×5 square grid, stretched or condensed to justify. It teaches type as building blocks.
- **De Stijl magazine (1917 Huszár masthead; 1921 redesign)**: woodcut rectangles spelling the title over a fully justified bold block. In 1921 a horizontal bold sans was overprinted on "NB". It teaches layered, justified, rectangular lockups. *(retry pass, 1921 redesign visually checked: Stedelijk scans of the 1921 cover (vol. 4) and a 1924 issue.)* Seen: tall red bold condensed letters (red `#E5371F`, ≈9% of the front cover) with the black bold sans "DE STIJL" overprinted straight across their middle; the masthead sits in the upper third, left-weighted. Volume and year are a small bold line top-right, and the publication data runs as a narrow justified block rotated 90° up the right edge. The rest of the cover is bare paper (≈84%). In 1924 the same lockup returns with a huge red issue number at the lower right, so the lockup is a fixed system with one changing numeral.
- **Counter-Composition V, 1924**: nine colored rectangles tilted 45° that touch edge to edge with no black lines between them, plus one small grey patch. It teaches adjacency without dividers. *(visually checked: Stedelijk gallery photo and a book scan, both on Commons. Nine planes meet edge to edge at 45°; the red rhombus covers ≈48% and sits central-high (centroid 48% / 40%), cropped by the top edge; black ≈19%, blue ≈11%, yellow ≈5%, plus greys)*
- **Contra-compositie VII, 1924**: a large off-center plane ringed by smaller ones, built on a rhombus. Its black bars are so thick they read as planes. It teaches that line weight can turn a line into a plane. *(visually checked: Museum De Lakenhal collection image, S 5794)* Seen: a square canvas hung as a lozenge in a stepped white frame; inside, the grid is upright, not tilted: four thick black bars (≈8% of the lozenge's width, black ≈20% of the surface) ring one large pale grey-white square that fills ≈60% of the canvas, slightly off centre. The four corner triangles left over hold the colour: yellow at the top (≈3%), blue at the bottom (≈2%), a small red wedge at the right (≈1%), white at the left. So the 'counter' here is the canvas turned against an upright grid, and the colours are only the scraps the turn cuts off. *(Earlier substitute, not obtained then; checked instead: **Counter-Composition VIII, 1924**, AIC scan: a lozenge canvas holding an upright black L whose arm is ≈19% of the width, black 40%, grey 10%, white 50%, no colour)*
- **Counter-Composition VI, 1925**: three thick black diagonals laid over an orthogonal background grid, each as wide as the grid's squares. It teaches two grids sharing one module. *(visually checked: Tate 600 px image. The background is a hairline orthogonal grid of about 10 × 10 squares with both diagonals drawn in each square; the charcoal bars (26% of the surface) run at 45° and are as wide as one square's diagonal, so their edges land on grid diagonals. No colour at all)*
- **Café Aubette ciné-dancing, Strasbourg, 1926–28**: colored relief panels tilted 45°, clashing with the orthogonal doors, windows and booths. It teaches that a rotated layer can sit over a functional layer that stays upright. *(visually checked: 2014 photo of the reconstructed ciné-dancing, Commons. Booths, windows and radiators are upright; tilted wall and ceiling panels in grey, green, blue and red are separated by raised light relief bands about a tenth of a panel wide, not butted edge to edge; colours are lighting-dependent)*
- **Simultaneous Counter-Composition, 1929–30**: 50 × 50 cm, with orthogonal and diagonal systems superimposed in one square. It teaches simultaneity: two readings at once. *(visually checked: Commons 1280 px scan. The tilt here is only ≈13–16°, not 45°: yellow, red and blue planes and two black bars (≈2.7% of the width) lean together against one upright black rectangle. Colour fills 61% of the square: blue 26%, red 18%, yellow 17%, black 16%, white 22%)*
- **Colour scheme for apartments in Drachten, 1921** (V&A): added. A small card of four abutting swatches, grey, blue, yellow, red, with no dividers. *(visually checked: V&A slide photo, strong blue cast; used for layout only, not for hexes)*

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Space** (Contra-compositie VII, 1924): one large central plane (≈60%) ringed by thick bars; the format is turned 45° while the grid stays upright, so the frame cuts four triangles off the grid's corners.
- **Colour relations** (VII): grey-white ≈76%, black ≈20%, the three primaries together ≈5% and each confined to a corner triangle at the edge; the most saturated colours are the smallest and touch only black and the frame, never each other.
- **Line and edge:** bars at ≈8% of the width read as planes; all edges hard; the stepped white frame repeats the lozenge outline.
- **The procedure:** draw one big upright plane with thick bars around it; rotate the container 45° (not the content); put each primary into one of the triangles the rotation crops off; keep the centre neutral.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Theo van Doesburg.** *Set two orders against each other at one angle, the order the content obeys and the order the field obeys, on a shared module, so the surface never comes to rest.*
Process:
1. Fix the working order first: upright, on one module `m`. Everything that is read or operated lives in it and never rotates.
2. Lay a counter-order over the same format, turned against the first by ONE angle (45°, or 13–16° in the mild mode), built on the same module: a counter-order band is one cell's diagonal wide (√2 × `m`), so its edges land on the working order's lattice points.
3. Choose which order the frame sides with. (a) Frame with the content: the field is turned, and its planes run off at least two edges. (b) Frame with the counter-order: the container is turned, the content inside stays upright, and the frame cuts triangles off the corners.
4. Distribute weight one of two ways: one dominant plane of 45–60% with 4–8 others at falling sizes (in (a) central-high and cut by an edge; in (b) central, neutral and ringed), or 3–4 near-equal planes of 16–26% each. Never a centred, mirrored arrangement.
5. Make every division a plane: neighbouring field planes meet edge to edge with no separator; any bar is 2.7–20% of the width, wide enough to read as a plane. Thin lines exist only as a quiet lattice that shows the module.
6. Commit to one colour register and go all the way: none (value only, dark 26–40%); heavy (saturated planes ≥60%, quiet ground only 16–22%); or scraps (≈5% in all, only in the corners that 3(b) cuts off, the most saturated the smallest, touching only structure and frame). One off-key neutral plane keeps the set from looking pure.
Why it works:
- The eye takes the frame and gravity as its reference. An order turned against that reference reads as leaning or moving, so the still surface carries energy. If the content turns too, the tilt becomes the new upright: a rotated page, harder to read and no longer tense.
- Coincidence signals intent. When the counter-order's edges land on the working order's lattice points, the two read as one system built twice; with unrelated measures the field reads as a backdrop that happens to be behind the content.
- A plane cut by the frame is completed by the eye beyond it, so the field feels larger than the format and the content reads as a window laid on it. Planes that all fit inside read as objects on a page.
Colour relations: three registers, never mixed (value only · saturated ≥60% with ground 16–22% · scraps ≈5% at the cut corners); in the heavy register one plane may hold ≈48% and the others fall away in size; planes meet with no stroke; one greyed or off-key plane among the saturated ones.
Space and line: one angle per surface; band = √2 × `m`; bars 2.7–20% of the width; planes off ≥2 edges or corners cut by a turned frame; near-square field; content and controls upright always.
Type requirement: type belongs to the working order: a display line fitted to a solid squared block exactly as wide as its column (a family with a width range, or letters built from module strokes); two lockups may cross at 90° (one overprinted on another, short meta text turned up an edge), never set oblique; reading text sits on its own opaque upright panel, not across the seam between two field planes.
Motion and interaction: the two orders move at different rates along the counter-order's axis (field at about 0.4× the content, switched off under reduced motion); a state change snaps a field plane along that axis in 3–4 steps over 180–260ms; hit areas are the upright boxes (≥44px), never the turned shapes.
Fails when: the screen is long-form reading or a full-bleed table, so the field is either covered or competes with the text; the viewport is narrow enough that under a third of the field shows; mode (b) on a non-square or space-poor format (a turned container wastes about half its bounding box); anything that needs text or controls on the turned order.
Signature (do not carry together): a 45° tilt · one large red rhombus cut by the edge · black bars thick enough to be planes.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Van Doesburg's procedure → a language-lesson screen in sage, cream and clay, soft and rounded (phone, 390 × 844).
1. *Working order.* Upright, module 26px, 15 columns. The exercise card, the answer chips (each 52px high) and the progress row sit on it. Corners 12px; the procedure does not care.
2. *Counter-order.* One angle, 14° (mild mode). Three bands turned 14°, each 37px wide (√2 × 26), so their edges pass through lattice points of the 26px grid at the card's top-left and bottom-right corners.
3. *Frame.* Mode (a): the field is turned, the frame is upright. All three bands and two large planes run off the left and bottom edges.
4. *Weight.* One dominant plane, 52% of the screen, centred at 50% / 38% and cut by the top edge; five others at 14, 9, 6, 4 and 3%.
5. *Divisions.* Field planes butt with no stroke. The only bars are the three 37px bands (9.5% of the width). A faint 26px lattice (4% contrast) shows behind the card for the top 120px only.
6. *Register.* Value only: the dominant plane is sage at L 78, the others sage at L 70, 62 and 88, plus one off-key plane in greyed clay (the "impure" one), 6%. Dark share 28%. No saturated colour in the field; the clay answer-check control is the single strong note and it lives in the upright order.
- *Type.* The lesson title is fitted to a squared block exactly 8 columns wide. The lesson number is turned 90° up the right edge (two digits, decorative; repeated in the header for screen readers). Exercise text sits on an opaque cream card.
- *Motion.* On scroll the field slides along its 14° axis at 0.4× the content speed. A correct answer snaps the dominant plane 26px along the axis in 3 steps over 220ms. Reduced motion: both off; the plane changes value instead.
- *Wide layout.* On a tablet in landscape the progress summary takes mode (b): a square panel turned 45° with upright rings inside and the three streak markers in the cut corners (scraps register, 4%).
Result: a soft, monochrome, slightly leaning lesson screen. No 45° rhombus, no primaries, no black. What survived: two orders on one module at one angle, the upright functional layer, planes cut by the frame, and a colour register held to its end.

## Space & composition
- **Two grids, one module.** Keep a strict upright column grid for content. Put a second grid rotated 45° behind it, built on the same unit. In Counter-Composition VI a diagonal bar is exactly one cell's diagonal wide (√2 × the cell side), so both its edges land on grid diagonals. The 45° angle belongs to 1924–25; the 1929–30 Simultaneous Counter-Composition tilts its second system by only ≈13–16°.
- **The diagonal belongs to the field, not to the content.** At the Aubette the doors, seats and windows stayed upright and only the color planes tilted. Text, inputs and buttons never rotate.
- **Planes run off the edge.** Cropped tilted rectangles imply an endless field; bleed them off two viewport edges.
- **Square formats.** Most counter-compositions are square; a 1:1 field turned 45° becomes the hidden rhombus scaffold.
- **Dominance or equal weight.** In Counter-Composition V one red plane takes ≈48% of the field, central-high and cropped by the top edge, with eight smaller planes around it at falling sizes. Simultaneous Counter-Composition instead sets four planes of near-equal weight (17–26% each). Counter-Composition VIII has no colour: one black L takes 40%.
- **Bars as planes.** Black bars run from ≈2.7% of the width (Simultaneous) to ≈19% (the L in Counter-Composition VIII), so they read as planes, not lines. Hairlines do exist, but only as a quiet background grid (Counter-Composition VI).

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

The base is primaries plus non-colors, with one or two extras to break Mondrian's purity: greys in Counter-Composition V, and greys and greens at the Aubette. In the paintings colours sit edge to edge with no stroke between them; at the Aubette the tilted panels are separated by raised light bands. Colour is optional: Counter-Compositions VI and VIII are black, grey and white only. When colour is used it is heavy: primaries cover ≈64% of Counter-Composition V (plus 19% black) and 61% of Simultaneous (plus 16% black); white or grey ground is only 16–22%.
- `#DE4132` vermilion red (Counter-Composition V, book scan; the gallery photo shows the same hue darker): the dominant tilted plane and the primary action, up to ≈48% of the field
- `#02204F` ultramarine blue (Simultaneous Counter-Composition; deep, near navy): the secondary plane and links
- `#F9C303` cadmium yellow (Simultaneous): small accent and focus state (≈5% in V, 17% in Simultaneous)
- `#0E0E0C` near-black (Counter-Composition VIII, AIC): bars and type
- `#E7E9E4` off-white (Counter-Composition VIII, AIC; neutral-cool, not warm): ground (the paintings' whites are never pure)
- `#C4C5C8` light grey (Counter-Composition VIII, AIC; Counter-Composition V also has a darker grey patch, ≈`#8F8C82` under gallery light): the "impure" Elementarist plane for disabled or muted UI
- `#2B5840` muted green (Aubette panel, 2014 photo; lighting-dependent): a rare extra drawn from the Aubette, used once at most

## Form, line & typography
The letters are capitals made only of right-angle, even-weight strokes on a 5×5 cell grid. Counters are square and there are no curves or diagonals. Width varies so lines justify into rectangles; set display type as solid justified blocks. Body text stays plain and readable. **Fonts:** *Archivo* or *Archivo Black* (Google, has a width axis for justifying blocks), *Space Grotesk* for squarish UI text, and *Silkscreen* or a custom 5×5 grid face for one display word only. (Architype Van Doesburg, The Foundry 1997, is the commercial revival.)

## Rhythm, motion & time
Elementarism admitted the "fourth dimension," time, and Van Doesburg designed the Aubette to be experienced by moving through it. Motion should feel like a plane snapping into its slot, not like floating.
- Background planes move by translating along the 45° axis only, at a different scroll speed from the upright content. The two systems slide past each other.
- Transitions are stepped (`steps(4)`) or a sharp `cubic-bezier(.7,0,.2,1)` at 180–260ms. Never use bounce.
- Shared-element moves take an L-path: x, then y.

## UI translations
1. Render a full-bleed background of 4–8 rectangles rotated 45°, cropped by the viewport, behind an upright 12-column content grid.
2. Size every diagonal bar to the diagonal of one grid cell (√2 × the module, e.g. 34px on a 24px module) so its edges land on the grid's diagonals and both grids share one module.
3. Give about 45–50% of the hero to a single tilted red plane, high and cropped by the top edge. Huddle content in the remaining upright corners.
4. Separate adjacent color panels by touching edges with no borders. Black bars are planes, 3–20% of the panel width, used only where a real structural division exists.
5. Set the page title as a justified block that spans its column exactly, in bold sans with variable width or tracking tuned to fill it.
6. Overprint the logo or section label on a large faint glyph or monogram behind it, as the 1921 masthead did.
7. Reserve yellow for one element per screen: the current focus or selection.
8. On scroll, move background planes along a 45° vector at 0.4× content speed (`transform: translate(x, x)`).
9. On hover, have a card's color plane slide in from one corner along its diagonal in 4 steps, without fading.
10. Use square cards (1:1) and square avatars. Rotate only the decorative layer, never the tap target.

## What this artist would reject
- Drop shadows, and hairlines anywhere but a quiet background grid. Depth comes from overlapping planes.
- Gradients, glassmorphism and soft blurs. Color is a flat, bounded plane.
- Pure symmetry and a centered hero. Balance is reached through tension, not mirroring.
- Rotating text or controls for effect. The functional layer stays orthogonal.
- Floaty spring animations. Motion is direct and stepped.

## Signature prompt seed
Design the screen as two colliding systems. An upright 12-column grid holds all text and controls, and behind it a field of flat rectangles rotated exactly 45° bleeds off two edges. One vermilion plane takes about 45% of the field, high and cropped by the top edge, with smaller blue, yellow, grey and black planes touching it edge to edge and no outlines. Dividers are thick black bars that read as planes, as wide as one grid cell's diagonal. Headlines are bold justified blocks filling their column. Square cards, no radii, no shadows, no gradients. Background planes slide diagonally at a different scroll speed, and transitions snap in steps.
