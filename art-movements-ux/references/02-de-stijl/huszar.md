# Vilmos Huszár — De Stijl

## Essence
Huszár treated a composition as a field of separate rectangles that can be shuffled: letters, rooms, a puppet's limbs and a cigarette girl's face are all assembled from the same flat, interchangeable planes. Where Mondrian held still, Huszár made the planes move (the dancing figure), wrap round space (the interiors) and sell things (Miss Blanche).

## Key works examined
- **De Stijl masthead/cover, 1917 (used until 1921)**: a woodcut in which black rectangles of varied size spell "De Stijl" on white, above a block of justified text. Lettering is built from the same units as the image. *(visually checked: Centraal Museum scan of the signed design "Vignet voor De Stijl". Letter strokes are one unit ≈3.5% of the block width, broken by gaps of 0.5–0.8 unit so no letter is closed; the image block below is 66% black, with gutters of only ≈0.5 unit, and its masses range from 1 unit up to ≈17 units tall, interlocking L-shapes rather than a 1×/2×/3× family; two corners carry stacks of 4 thin stripes)*
- **Bruynzeel boys' bedroom, Voorburg, 1918–c.1920**: Huszár did the color and Klaarhamer the furniture. One color scheme runs across cupboard, beds, carpets, cushions and four stained-glass windows, so color is the system holding the room together. *(visually checked: photo of the room as reconstructed and displayed, Commons. Walls are white above and light grey below; yellow, dark blue, light-grey and dark-grey rectangles float on them, never touching each other or the wall edges; one long thin blue stripe and a yellow band run along the wall heads; the alcove ceiling is dark blue. The furniture is deep oxblood red with black panels and black posts. Colours are lighting-dependent)*
- **Mechanical Dancing Figure, 1920**: a key-and-string marionette about 1 m tall with transparent red and green planes, projected as a silhouette on a lit screen at Dutch Dada soirées. Each pose makes a new composition. *(visually checked: museum photo of the figure, Commons, dated 1922 there. Dark riveted metal body built of rectangles on one module; colour is only small slit windows of transparent red and teal, ≈14% of the figure; the windows never overlap each other, and the cast shadow shows separate red and green patches, so the "overlap makes a third tone" claim is not confirmed)*
- **Space-Color-Composition, Juryfreie Kunstschau Berlin, 1923**: the wall reads "color by Huszár / form by Rietveld". Color and structure are credited as separate layers. *(only the Centraal Museum model of the exhibition plan could be obtained: white walls, grey floor, no colour shown, so the colour claim is unchecked)*
- **Miss Blanche Virginia cigarettes, 1926–28**: a full identity covering packaging, press ads, display cards, coupons, a mirror case and an ad cart. It shows the "New Woman" through flat geometric planes, with variant labels (Yellow, Green) coded by color. *(retry pass, visually checked: five Stedelijk scans, display cards and a coupon catalogue cover, 1926.)* Seen: the card is one full-bleed colour field (yellow `#EFC130`–`#FED01D`, 68–78% of the card; pale green on the Green Label) inside a dark or coloured frame ≈3% of the width; the woman is assembled from 5–6 flat planes (black hat, red lapel, white shirt, blue glove, face left in the ground colour) with no outlines. On the Green Label card (17169) only, the MISSBLANCHE wordmark is built from rectangles with a small stacked "ISS"; on the 1926 card 17157 it is a flowing script "Miss Blanche". Both sit over a bold red sans "VIRGINIA" and black "CIGARETTES". Variants change the frame and accent colour (Yellow Label: red-orange frame; Green Label: green frame and green garment), keeping the layout identical. The coupon catalogue is the black-on-kraft version: one square of hatched rectangles as the only image.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Vilmos Huszár.** *Build word, picture and surface from one unit, and lay colour over them as a separate sheet, so any arrangement of the pieces is a finished composition.*
Process:
1. Fix one unit `u` (≈3.5% of the block's width). Every stroke is 1`u`, every gutter 0.5`u`, and every mass is a whole number of units, from 1`u` up to ≈17`u`, including interlocking L-shapes. Letters and image use the same pieces.
2. Polarise density: pack the pieces into one cluster at about two-thirds filled, parted only by 0.5`u` gutters, and leave everything around it at zero. No middle density. The ground between pieces is part of the figure: letters stay open (gaps of 0.5–0.8`u`), and their insides are leftover ground.
3. Pair the charged with the still at one width: the irregular cluster sits on a calm, even, squared block of exactly the same width.
4. Lay colour as its own layer, placed after and independently of the structure: a colour plane may cross a break, turn a corner, or float clear of every edge and of every other plane. It never outlines or fills exactly one component.
5. Depict with planes: build any figure from 5–6 flat pieces with no outline, and leave one of them as bare ground.
6. Make variants and states by reassignment, not redesign: keep the arrangement and change only which colour is ground, frame (≈3% of the width) and accent; or keep the colours and move pieces by whole units into another complete pose.
Why it works:
- Likeness groups. When letters and picture are cut from the same unit they read as one object, and identity sits in the construction rule, not in a colour or a drawing, so it survives any change of palette. That is what lets a variant change everything but the layout.
- At two-thirds filled with thin gutters, figure and ground are near balance: the eye flips between dark masses and light channels, and the cluster stays active without moving. At middle densities the same pieces read as an even grey texture.
- When colour edges do not coincide with structural edges, the eye splits the surface into two layers. Colour is then free to mean something of its own (a variant, a category) across components. Colour that follows component edges is read as the component's decoration.
Colour relations: two registers. Structural: ground and one dark value only inside the cluster (≈66% dark), with colour as a few detached planes, one strong plane per view (5–9%) and the others clear of it. Flooded: one saturated ground of 68–78% in a thin frame, a figure of 5–6 flat planes, one piece left as ground. Each hue marks a variant or category and is also named in text. (UI extension, not observed: where two colour sheets must cross, give the crossing its own third flat tone and a label. The checked figure keeps its coloured windows apart.)
Space and line: one unit; strokes 1`u`, gutters 0.5`u`, masses 1–17`u`; no drawn dividers, the ground separates; cluster ≈2/3 filled over a still block of equal width; square corners follow from the unit.
Type requirement: the signature word is built from unit strokes and broken open, at display size only, with a plain-text equivalent; reading text is a sturdy face of even colour that makes a uniform block at the cluster's width. Square the block by fitting the measure; if justifying opens word gaps wider than the line space, set it flush left inside the same box.
Motion and interaction: pose to pose. Two or three complete arrangements, cut or stepped (no more than 3 changes a second, never blinking; a single cut under reduced motion); pieces and drags move by whole units; every intermediate frame must pass as a composition. Targets are ≥44px even when the unit is smaller.
Fails when: the layout is fluid and the unit cannot stay whole (it must snap, or the gutters go ragged); the content is photographs or long continuous text with nothing to cut into pieces; there are more than four or five variants, so whole-surface colour coding runs out; a colour plane crossing components lands under text and breaks its contrast.
Signature (do not carry together): block-built letters in black on white · a flat poster figure on a saturated yellow ground · a jointed mechanical figure with coloured windows.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Huszár's procedure → a trail-guide app in forest greens, bone and rust, with rounded cards (phone, 390 × 844).
1. *Unit.* `u` = 12px (3.4% of the 358px content block). Strokes 12px, gutters 6px, masses from 12px up to 204px.
2. *Cluster.* The trail's elevation profile is drawn as 22 interlocking pieces (columns and L-shapes on the 12px unit) in the top 38% of the screen, 64% filled, 6px gutters, dark green on bone. Around it: nothing. The trail's short name is built from the same 12px strokes, each letter left open by a 6–9px gap, with the name repeated as plain text below.
3. *Still block.* Directly under the cluster, exactly 358px wide: the route description in an even-coloured face, flush left inside a squared box (justifying at this width opened gaps, so the fallback applies).
4. *Colour layer.* One rust plane, 7% of the screen, marks "steep section". It is placed by distance, not by component: it starts over the last third of the profile, crosses the gutter and continues over the first two lines of the stats row. A pale moss plane (4%) floats over the map thumbnail, clear of its edges and of the rust plane. Text under the rust plane was re-checked at 5.2:1.
5. *Figure.* The empty state (no trail chosen) is a walker made of six flat pieces with no outline; the face is left as bone ground.
6. *Variants and poses.* Difficulty variants keep the layout and change ground, frame and accent: easy = bone ground, green frame (12px, 3%); hard = the flooded register, rust ground at 72%, bone frame; each also says "Easy" or "Hard". The profile has three poses (overview, climb, descent) reached by moving pieces whole units, 2 changes a second at most.
- *Crossing (UI extension).* Where "steep" and "exposed" overlap, the overlap is a third flat tone with its own label, not a blend.
- *Interaction.* Dragging the position marker moves it 12px at a time; its hit area is 44 × 44px.
Result: a green-and-bone outdoor app. No black woodcut, no yellow poster, no primaries. What survived: one unit for word and picture, a packed cluster over a still block, colour as a detached layer, and variants by reassignment.

## Space & composition
- **Rectangles as tiles, not lines.** Unlike Mondrian's black grid, Huszár's early work leaves out the dividing bars. Blocks float on white and touch only at corners or across narrow gutters. The white between them is part of the figure.
- **One small module, masses of any size.** In the masthead the letters and stripes are 1 unit (≈3.5% of the block width) and the gutters about half a unit, but the image masses run from 1 to ≈17 units and lock together as L-shapes. The cluster is 66% ink. Density is high in the cluster and zero around it, with no middle tones.
- **Image block over text block.** The cover stacks a charged, irregular mosaic over a calm, fully justified slab of type. Wild and still share one column width.
- **Color can be detached from form.** In the Berlin room, color planes were laid across walls, floor and ceiling regardless of where the architecture breaks. A blue plane may turn a corner. Color is its own layer over the structure.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

The logic is primaries used as **separable planes**, each a solid field with hard edges and no gradients. In the dancing figure, transparent red and teal sit in separate slit windows (≈14% of the figure) on a dark metal body; in the photographed object they do not overlap. In the reconstructed Bruynzeel room, the big red is a deep oxblood on the furniture, while yellow and blue float as rectangles on white and grey walls. In the Miss Blanche range, a hue marks a product variant (yellow label, green label). That is color as data, not decoration. *(Miss Blanche obtained in the retry pass: five Stedelijk scans, see Key works.)*

## Form, line & typography
The 1917 masthead letters are pixel glyphs: every stroke is a rectangle, and counters are leftover white. That maps straight onto a bitmap/grid face for display, set beside a sturdy grotesque in justified blocks for body text. Corners are square (radius 0), and line weight is replaced by **block weight**. Fonts that fit (Google Fonts): **Silkscreen** (constructed block letters, display only), **Space Grotesk** (body, square-shouldered), **Archivo Black** (heavy titling for justified slabs).

## Rhythm, motion & time
Motion is **pose-to-pose**, not fluid. Like the marionette's key mechanism, elements jump between a few discrete states (steps(), not ease-in-out). Each state should be a complete, balanced composition. Color planes can slide over one another with opacity, so new tones appear where they cross. Hover and press move a plane by a whole grid unit, never by a few nudging pixels. A loading state can be the logo's rectangles rearranging between two or three fixed configurations.

## UI translations
1. Build the wordmark/hero title from absolutely positioned rectangles on an 8px module: strokes 1u, gaps 0.5–1u, and break every letter so no counter is closed. No font for the signature element.
2. Remove the visible grid lines between panels. Separate cards with 8–12px gutters of the ground color, so white does the dividing.
3. Stack a dense, irregular rectangle cluster (top 40% of the screen, about two-thirds ink, half-module gutters, masses from 1u up to whole interlocking L-shapes) over a fully justified text slab (`text-align: justify; hyphens: auto`) of exactly the same width.
4. Put color on a separate token layer from components. Let a single colored plane span two unrelated cards or wrap from header into sidebar, ignoring component boundaries.
5. Code product tiers or categories with one flat primary each (yellow tier, green tier) and apply it to the whole card surface, not a little badge.
6. Animate between states with `animation-timing-function: steps(3)`. Every intermediate frame must look like a deliberate composition.
7. On drag or hover, translate panels by whole module units (`transform: translate(calc(var(--u)*2))`), never by fractional pixels.
8. Overlap two translucent color planes (`mix-blend-mode: multiply`, 65% opacity) to mark an intersection, e.g. a filter AND a selection. (A UI extension: the checked dancing figure keeps its red and teal windows apart.)
9. Allow exactly one red plane per viewport, used for the primary action or current state.
10. Use radius 0 and no shadows. Depth comes only from overlap order and opacity.

## What this artist would reject
- Gradients, glassmorphism blur, soft shadows: planes must stay flat and hard-edged.
- Color stuck to component edges, like a tinted card border or an accent stripe on a button.
- Smooth, springy easing everywhere. He wanted deliberate, readable poses.
- Visible hairline grids and dividers. The ground should separate things.
- A logo that is just a font in a weight.

## Signature prompt seed
Design the screen as a field of warm off-white in which flat, hard-edged rectangles float, separated by gutters of ground color rather than lines. Build the title from the same 8px block module as the imagery, then set body text in a fully justified slab matching the cluster's width. Treat color as an independent layer: a single red plane, plus blue and yellow fields that cross card boundaries, with translucent green overlapping to make new tones. Zero radius, no shadows. Transitions snap through two or three complete poses using stepped timing, like a marionette changing position.
