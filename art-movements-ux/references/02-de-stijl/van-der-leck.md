# Bart van der Leck — De Stijl

## Essence
Van der Leck never let go of the figure. He cuts a real scene (workers leaving a factory, passengers on a deck) into short flat bars of red, yellow, blue and black that float apart, never touching, on an open white field. The scene stays legible, held together only by where each bar sits and which way it leans.

## Key works examined
- **Batavier Line poster, 1915–16** (lithograph, 77.7 × 114.6 cm, Kröller-Müller). A commission for Wm H. Müller & Co.'s Rotterdam–London line. Passengers become flat colour planes; the version usually reproduced today is a 1920 alteration he disowned. *(visually checked: Kröller-Müller scan of KM 121.068. Pre-De Stijl: cream paper, black ruled compartments around every panel, figures in flat red-brown, blue and black, a green waterline, thin monoline capitals in red and black)*
- **Composition 1917 No. 4 (Leaving the Factory)** (oil, 95.5 × 102.2 cm). Tight verticals at the top read as factory and chimney, staggered small blocks in the middle are the crowd, and horizontal stripes below are the street. *(visually checked: Kröller-Müller scan. No diagonals. Top 42%: red verticals and black window squares, 45% ink; a middle band of black dashes over a row of yellow chips; a scattered lower zone; a bottom row of short black dashes. White 64%, red 16%, black 10%, blue 5%, yellow 1.5%)*
- **The "Leaving the Factory" series, 1916–17.** The same scene moves from realistic to nearly abstract over several versions. A model for progressive disclosure. *(Checked: **Composition 1917 No. 3 (Leaving the Factory)**, Kröller-Müller scan, the same layout as No. 4 with the black squares thinned to dashes: white 84%, red 7%, black 5%, blue 3.4%, yellow 0.5%. Added: **Composition 1916 No. 4 (Mine Triptych)**, Kröller-Müller scan: two black side panels and a white centre; ≈20% of the bars tilt, and in the centre panel red, yellow and blue segments butt end to end inside one bar)*
- **Delft Salad Oil poster, 1919 (unprinted).** Lettering too radical for the client; type becomes image. *(not obtained)*
- **Metz & Co. identity, from 1930.** Branding plus a rectilinear geometric alphabet; fabrics in his vermillion, cornflower and canary, first used in the Sonneveld House. *(retry pass, visually checked: Cooper Hewitt scans of Metz & Co. shopping bags and a box lid, c. 1952, objects 18649073/77/79, "Bart van der Leck: Artist"; fabrics still not obtained)* The mark: METZ in letters built only from separate straight strokes with visible gaps at every joint, diagonals only in M, Z, A; wide letters (M wider than tall), thin monoline strokes ≈1/12 of the cap height. Ink on cream `#EAE7DA` (bag) or bare grey card (box).

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Bart van der Leck.** *Reduce a real subject to the fewest separate marks that still read as it, and let the untouched field between the marks do the joining.*
Process:
1. Start from a subject that already has structure (a scene, a process, a dataset, a word), never from an invented pattern. Lay it out whole, then delete: keep only the segments that give position, direction and kind.
2. Reserve the field: 64–84% of the surface stays untouched and remains ONE connected shape, with no enclosures. All marks together take 16–36%.
3. Cut the marks to one thickness family (main marks ≈3% of the width) and hold every mark clear of every other by 0.8–1.1× that thickness. Nothing touches, overlaps or encloses.
4. Zone by density, not by frames: bands with falling fill (about 45% → 36% → 20–25%), each band with its own mark shape (long uprights, short chips, a loose scatter, a row of dashes). One full-width mark may cap the densest band.
5. Colour by kind, ranked by area: the largest kind 6–16%, the darkest neutral 1–10%, a third kind 3–5%, the lightest and brightest kind smallest at 0.5–2.6%. Different kinds are always parted by field (they may abut only as segments of one mark).
6. Keep marks upright or level by default. At most one mark in five may lean (30–45°), every lean carries the same meaning (movement or direction), and a surface may have none.
7. Mode: show the subject at several degrees of reduction, from recognisable to bare, as stages of one view.
Why it works:
- The eye completes a figure from aligned fragments, and doing the completing holds attention. With gaps about one thickness wide, marks group by nearness and alignment yet stay separate. Much narrower gaps fuse them into outlined shapes; much wider gaps scatter them and the subject is lost.
- A field with no enclosures has no nesting, so grouping can only come from likeness (fill, shape, direction) and nearness. The viewer reads what kind of thing a mark is before how important it is, which suits content of many peers.
- Density itself segments the surface: a change in how tightly marks are packed is seen as a border. Frames would add a second system and break the field into pieces.
Colour relations: field 64–84%, one near-neutral value; 3–4 kinds, each one flat fill, ranked by area (largest 6–16% down to smallest 0.5–2.6%); the darkest neutral may fill small solid marks as well as strokes; no tints, no gradients; the field may invert to the darkest value in a side zone.
Space and line: one thickness family; gap 0.8–1.1× thickness; bands of falling density with a different mark shape per band; no borders, no nesting; leaning marks ≤1 in 5, or none; calm near-square formats.
Type requirement: display letters built from the same separate strokes as the marks: wide, light (stroke ≈1/12 of the height), every joint open, word spaces drawn as marks so each line fills one width, and a solid block may lead a line. Use them for one to three words at sizes where a stroke is ≥2px; all reading text is in a plain continuous face.
Motion and interaction: the view assembles and comes apart. Marks arrive one after another along their own axis (120–200ms each, 40–60ms apart; in place under reduced motion); a state change swaps a mark's kind, shown by shape or direction as well as fill; a thin mark that is a control owns the field around it up to a 44px hit area. The lightest kind never carries text or a state alone.
Fails when: the content is continuous prose or a dense table (it cannot be cut up, and the field drops under 60%); there is no real subject to reduce, so the marks become confetti; a form with many inputs, where each boundary must be found at once; labels longer than three words in the broken letters.
Signature (do not carry together): red, blue and yellow bars on white · letters broken at every joint · a street or factory scene cut into fragments.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Van der Leck's procedure → a build-pipeline status board, dark slate with a mint, a violet and a grey (wall display and desktop, 1920 × 1080).
1. *Subject.* The last 40 pipeline runs, each a row of stages over time. Laid out whole it is a Gantt chart with axes, boxes and labels. Deleted down: one mark per stage (position = time, length = duration, kind = outcome), no axes, no row lines, no boxes.
2. *Field.* Slate at L 12 covers 74% and is one connected shape. Marks 26%.
3. *Marks.* Thickness 56px (2.9% of the width) for stage marks, gap 50–60px between any two. Nothing touches.
4. *Bands.* Top 40%: running builds as long upright marks, 44% filled, capped by one full-width mark (the deploy window). Middle: queued jobs as short chips in rows, 35%. Below: finished runs scattered by time, 20%. Bottom row: evenly spaced short dashes, one per hour.
5. *Kinds.* Passed: mint, 14%, level bars. Structure and time dashes: a pale grey, 6%. Waiting: violet, 4%, square chips. Failed: the brightest, a near-white, 2%, and always the one shape nothing else uses (a short upright pair), so it is found without colour.
6. *Lean.* Only marks that are moving lean: a stage being retried tilts 35°. Three of the 31 visible marks, under one in five.
7. *Stages.* Zoom level 1 shows the board as above; level 2 restores stage names beside the marks; level 3 is the full log. Same subject, three reductions.
- *Type.* The project name, two words, in open-jointed strokes 1/12 of their height, word space drawn as a level mark, justified to the cap mark's width. Stage names and logs in a plain monospace.
- *Motion.* On load, marks slide 12px into place along their axis, 150ms each, 50ms apart. A failure swaps kind in place (shape and fill), no flashing.
- *Pointer.* Each mark's hit area extends 25px into the gap on all sides.
Result: a dark board of separated mint and violet marks. No white ground, no primaries, no figures. What survived: a real subject cut to separate marks, one connected field, gaps equal to thickness, bands of density, and colour by kind.

## Space & composition
- **White is the medium, not the background.** 64–84% of the surface stays untouched (1917 No. 4: 64%; No. 3: 84%; the Mine Triptych's centre: 83%), read as one shape with the bars as events inside it.
- **Separation is the rule.** In the 1917 compositions elements never touch or overlap. The gap of white between neighbours is about 0.8–1.1× the bar's thickness (factory zone of No. 4: bars 2.8–3.1% of the width, gaps 2.2–3.2%). Think `gap`, never `border`. The exception is the 1916 Mine Triptych, where segments of different primaries butt end to end inside one bar, with angled cut ends.
- **Banded semantics.** In *Leaving the Factory* the canvas splits into horizontal zones of falling density: a top 42% of red verticals and black window squares (45% ink) capped by one full-width blue bar, a middle band of black dashes over yellow chips (36%), a scattered lower zone (20%), and a bottom row of evenly spaced short black dashes (25%).
- **The diagonal is allowed.** Unlike Mondrian, van der Leck used diagonals: about 20% of the bars tilt in the 1916 Mine Triptych (mostly 30–45°), and that tilt carries the motion. The 1917 factory compositions have none, so the diagonal is optional.
- **Near-square formats.** 95.5 × 102.2 is almost 1:1, and the poster is roughly 2:3. Calm frames; tension lives in the fragments.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

The logic: from 1916 on, pure primaries plus black, laid flat with no gradient, each colour used in small amounts so the white stays in charge. Colour tells you what something is, not how important it is. Red is always the largest colour (5.7–16%), then black (1–10%), blue (3–5%), and yellow last (0.5–2.6%). Black is not only line: in 1917 No. 4 it fills the factory's window squares. A field can also be black (the Mine Triptych's side panels). Edges are brushed and slightly soft, not taped.

| Hex | Role |
|---|---|
| `#DCDBDB` | Field: the white ground (1917 No. 4; near-neutral, not warm), 64–84% of every screen |
| `#CC5042` | Vermillion: action and focal fragments (1917 No. 4; an orange-leaning red, the largest colour) |
| `#1C42A2` | Blue: structure and navigation bars (1917 No. 4) |
| `#E2CE69` | Canary yellow: highlights and state change (1917 No. 4; soft, ≤3% of the area) |
| `#39393B` | Black: thin bars, type strokes, and small filled squares (1917 No. 4; a soft charcoal) |
| `#4D5C94` | Cornflower (Metz textile variant): secondary or quiet surfaces (Metz textiles not obtained; nearest seen is the softer blue of the 1916 Mine Triptych) |

## Form, line & typography
His letters follow the same rules as his pictures: built from straight bars with a few diagonals, strokes that stop short of each other, and counters that open into the surrounding white. The Metz alphabet started out as stencil lettering, which is why the strokes break (confirmed on the Metz bags: wide, light, monoline, every joint open; so prefer a wide light stencil, not a condensed one). In *Het Vlas* the type and the illustrations are clearly cut from the same pieces. The 1994 revival, Architype van der Leck, isn't free. Alternatives:
- **Big Shoulders Stencil Display** (Google Fonts): condensed and stencil-broken, for headlines.
- **Major Mono Display** (Google Fonts): geometric, built from unusual constructions, for labels and numerals.
- **Jost** (Google Fonts): plain geometric sans for body text, so the display type stays special.

## Rhythm, motion & time
The figure-to-abstraction series is itself a motion idea: interfaces can *assemble* and *disassemble*. On load, bars slide 8–16px into place one after another, staggered 40–60ms apart, with no fades and nothing bouncing. On hover, a fragment shifts or rotates a few degrees. Use short ease-out tweens (120–200ms), clipped like stencil cuts, never springy.

## UI translations
1. **Layout:** Build every screen from a 12-column grid, but put content in rectangular "bars" whose total area stays between 15% and 35% of the viewport. Leave the rest as `#DCDBDB`-family white.
2. **Layout:** Keep a uniform gap (`gap: 0.75em` or `12px`) between all blocks and never use borders or dividers. Separation comes from white alone.
3. **Layout:** Split long pages into horizontal bands of falling density: a header band of tightly packed vertical slivers (nav) under one full-width rule, a middle band of short chips in rows, a scattered zone of staggered cards, and a footer row of evenly spaced short dashes.
4. **Color:** Give every primary one semantic job: red for actions, blue for navigation, yellow for state, black for text and small filled markers. Keep red the largest colour and yellow the smallest. Never put two primaries next to each other without white between them, except as segments of one bar (Mine Triptych).
5. **Color:** Use solid fills only. No gradients, no shadows, no tints below 100% except the single cornflower secondary.
6. **Type:** Set headlines in a stencil face at large size with wide tracking (`letter-spacing: 0.08em`), so the breaks in each letter echo the gaps between blocks.
7. **Type:** Build icons from 2–4 disconnected strokes (CSS bars or SVG `<line>`s with gaps) instead of closed outlines.
8. **Composition:** Tilt at most one element in five (badge, primary CTA or progress marker) by `rotate(-30deg)` to `rotate(45deg)`, or none at all.
9. **Motion:** On first load, bring blocks in on a stagger from the edge they point toward, as if the composition is assembling.
10. **Interaction:** Progressive abstraction for illustration and empty states. Show a recognisable figure built from bars; on task completion the bars drift apart into pure abstraction.
11. **Interaction:** Use colour instead of hover outlines. A hovered card changes fill from blue to red; nothing else moves.

## What this artist would reject
- Rounded cards with drop shadows sitting on a grey canvas. Volume and elevation make a lie of the flat plane.
- Containers that touch or nest: boxes inside boxes inside a bordered panel.
- Gradients, glassmorphism, blurred backdrops.
- Full-bleed hero photography that crowds the white field out.
- Subjectless decoration: he insisted on staying tied to ordinary life.
- Colour used as a brand wash, like a blue header bar running across the whole width.

## Signature prompt seed
Design this app screen on a near-neutral white field (#DCDBDB family) covering 64–84% of the viewport. Build content from flat rectangular bars and small squares, mostly vermillion, then black, blue, and a little canary. The bars never touch, have a white gap about equal to their own thickness, and use no borders, shadows or gradients. Arrange them in horizontal bands: dense vertical slivers on top, scattered staggered blocks in the middle, long thin rules below. Tilt one element diagonally, or none. Set headlines in condensed stencil type with wide tracking. Build icons from disconnected strokes. Animate blocks assembling one after another on load, with crisp 150ms motion and no bounce.
