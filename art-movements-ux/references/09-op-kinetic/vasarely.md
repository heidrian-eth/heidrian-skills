# Victor Vasarely — Op Art

## Essence
Vasarely turned painting into a combinatorial system: a colored square with a contrasting form cut into it (the "plastic unit") is one letter of an alphabet that assistants, factories and the public could all use. Depth and movement aren't drawn in. They come from programmatic variation across a grid, where small shifts in each cell's size, hue or tone make the flat surface swell, sink or vibrate.

## Key works examined
- **Zebra (1937):** two interlocked zebras made only of black and white stripes on black, with no outlines. Contour can come entirely from where a pattern changes. *(visually checked: secondary source, artchive.com, the 1937 original, 408 px.)* Seen: black ground ≈68%, light stripes ≈32%; two zebras knotted together fill the frame and run off the left, right and bottom edges; no outline anywhere: a body exists only where the curved light stripes are, and the boundary between the two animals is where stripe direction changes. Stripes taper and swell along their length and bend around volumes (neck, haunch). *(Earlier Substitute checked: **Zèbres, 1959**, AIC 59973, a later version: black stripes #292021 (43%) on beige #D3B797, no outlines; stripes are NOT uniform: they taper and bend, from ~1.5% to ~5% of the width (about 3:1), and every contour is where stripe direction changes, at varied angles rather than a clean 90°.)*
- **Vega III (1957–59) → Vega-Nor (1969, 200×200 cm):** a checkerboard that swells into a sphere. Distorting the grid creates volume without any shading. *(Vega III / Vega-Nor not obtained. Substitute checked: **Vega, from Album I, 1955/1959**, AIC 151197: base checker cell 12.5 px; in the bulge lens cells grow to 27 px (2.2×) at the peak and shrink to 8–9 px (0.7×) at the lens rim before returning to 1×; a second, mirror lens pinches cells to 5 px (0.4×). The checker sits inside a wide black frame #1B1C17 (64% of the sheet). A few odd units (discs, one lozenge) are scattered in the field. **Vega-Nor itself visually checked in the retry pass** (Buffalo AKG K1969:29 scan): a ≈21 × 21 grid of square-in-square units whose centred sphere fills ≈85% of the field; cells at the peak grow to ≈2.9× the edge pitch and compress to ≈0.5–0.6× at the sphere's rim. There is no white and no checkerboard: every cell is a thick grid frame plus an inset square, the frames ramp crimson #C0022F → plum #741236 → ochre #865427 → near-black #312725 by quadrant, and the insets alternate ultramarine #0A4CA4 (22%) and dark teal #294B4A (24%), so the volume comes from size plus a two-axis colour ramp.)*
- **Unités plastiques (patented 2 March 1959):** forms are cut from a colored square and recombined. This is the first design token system, in all but name. *(visually checked: **Banya, 1964**, Tate T00753, and **Blue and Green**, n.d., AIC 29176. Blue and Green is not a plain 3 × 3 grid: it is three columns of three touching square cells, staggered, with the middle column shifted down by part of a cell (a thin band fills the gap at its top, and at the foot of each outer column), each cell one ground + one inset form (disc, square, triangle); insets measure 54% (square) to 89% (disc) of the cell. Its colours stay in one analogous family: ultramarine #0034C6, emerald #1D7159, light green #54C751, sky #74A2F0, navy #142655. Banya sets parallelogram-in-square units on a 45°-rotated square: reds #EA3C23/#C82D38/#9F2A40 against greens #2D9E72/#3FBF71/#81E56D, each colour 10–15% of the area.)*
- **Folklore Planétaire (presented 1963):** the alphabet is published as a numbered palette of about 16 colors and 17 forms, meant to be a universal, industrial visual language. *(object not obtained; a Fondation Vasarely installation photo shows a large multicolour disc-in-square grid with yellow #FBCC0A, vermilion #F34F26, green #53AE53, blue #0B3C92, plum #994557 under gallery light, used only as a lighting-dependent check.)*
- **Planetary Folklore Participants No. 1 (1969):** a magnetic relief kit (edition of 3,000) with 390 coded elements in 19 colors and "programmation" sheets for recording layouts. A design system shipped to users. *(not visually checked)*
- **Renault losange (1972, with Yvaral):** a diamond made of parallel stripes, with opposite corners offset so it reads as 3D. Renault used it for about 20 years. Op Art compressed into a brand mark. *(not visually checked)*
- **Supernovae, 1959–61** *(added, visually checked: Tate T00676)*: a black/white square grid (black #333434 41%, white #E6E4D7 37%) in which cell size and disc size step across the field; white gaps separate the squares, so here cells do NOT touch.
- **Capella, 1964** *(added, visually checked: AIC 23979)*: two stacked panels of horizontal black/white bars whose widths swell and thin across the middle, a stripe-field lens.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Space** (Zebra 1937): one tangled figure filling the frame, cropped by three edges; the ground is the darkest and largest area.
- **Colour relations:** two values only (black ≈68%, light ≈32%); the figure is made of the light value in stripes, so it never becomes a solid mass.
- **Line and edge:** stripes of varying width bend to imply volume; contours are only changes of stripe direction or phase.
- **The procedure:** choose a dark ground; draw the figure only as light stripes; change stripe direction to separate overlapping parts; vary stripe width along the curve to model form; never draw an outline.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Victor Vasarely.** *Write a closed alphabet of two-layer units, fill the field with it without a gap, then bend the whole lattice with one function, so that volume and focus come from distortion and never from shading.*
Process:
1. Fix one cell that tiles in both directions (equal sides) and give it exactly two layers: a ground, and one inset figure 55–90% of the cell wide. Fix a short list of figures (3–6) and an indexed colour set. From here on every element is a permutation: figure × ground index × figure index.
2. Fill the field edge to edge with cells that touch. Space comes only from shrinking the inset, and calm only from lowering the contrast between the two layers. No cell is left blank.
3. Colour by ramp, not by pick: one or two hue families per field, 4–6 value steps in each. Inside a cell the two layers contrast strongly; between neighbouring cells the same layer moves one step. Run one ramp along each axis.
4. Apply ONE displacement function to the whole lattice: cell size depends only on the distance from a peak, 2.2–2.9× at the peak, 0.5–0.7× in a ring at the rim, 1× beyond it. The cell count and the connections between cells never change. The lens may cover up to 85% of the field.
5. Mark exceptions inside the alphabet: swap figure and ground, or exchange the figure for another from the list. Keep them few (one to a handful per field), and never add an outline, a shadow or a new shape.
6. Two-value mode: with only a dark (43–68% of the area) and a light, draw the figure as stripes of the light that taper about 3:1 and bend, and separate overlapping parts only by a change of stripe direction.
Why it works:
- Texture gradient: cells that swell and then crowd together at a rim are what a regular lattice looks like on a sphere, so the eye chooses "regular grid on a curved surface" over "irregular grid on a flat one". Enlarge the centre without compressing the rim and it reads as one big tile; break the lattice's continuity and it reads as collage.
- In a closed alphabet every unit is predictable, so a single permutation is found before reading starts. Add an outside form or an outline for emphasis and nothing is the odd one any more.
- One-step neighbours group by likeness into a continuous surface, while the strong step inside each cell keeps the unit legible: the field shimmers and does not clash.
Colour relations: one or two hue families, 4–6 values each; layer to layer, high contrast (an inversion must survive with hue removed, ≥3:1); neighbour to neighbour, one step; the two inset tones near-equal in share (22% : 24%); a two-value field splits about 40 : 40 with a mid tone at the edge; no blank ground.
Space and line: one cell size before distortion; inset 55–90%; peak 2.2–2.9×, rim 0.5–0.7×, lens ≤85% of the field, centred or off-centre, optionally paired with a pinch (0.4×); no drawn lines, since every edge is two flat colours meeting.
Type requirement: letters as units: capitals at an even advance (fixed pitch, or tracked 0.2em or more) so a word is a row of cells; a second fixed-pitch voice for the code that names each permutation; text sits on a cell's flat ground at 1× or larger, never in a rim cell.
Motion and interaction: change travels through the lattice; objects do not travel across it. Focus moves the peak to the chosen cell and the neighbours rescale by distance over 300–500ms; under reduced motion the lens is fixed (the illusion is complete when still). Size the base cell so that an interactive rim cell is still ≥44px (base ≥88px at 0.5×), or keep controls out of the rim. A state is an inversion plus a second cue (a figure swap, the peak, a label), never the two colours alone.
Fails when: items differ in aspect or length (prose, wide tables); the field has fewer than about 9 cells per side (no rim to compress); a narrow screen pushes rim cells below a touch target; several things must be primary at once; the content has no natural unit.
Signature (do not carry together): a checker bulging into a sphere · discs in squares in saturated primaries · zebra stripes.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Vasarely's procedure → a warehouse bin picker in pale greys (tablet, 1024 × 768). Monochrome, sparse, utility.
1. *Alphabet.* Cell = one bin, 88px before distortion. Two layers: ground, and one inset figure at 60% (full bin: square), 75% (part-full: disc) or 85% (empty: ring cut from the ground tone). Three figures, ten indexed tones. The bin code ("C-12") is the permutation's name.
2. *Field.* 11 × 8 cells touching edge to edge under a 64px flat bar. No gutters; a quiet aisle is one where figure and ground sit two steps apart, not 5.
3. *Ramp.* Two families only: a neutral grey and a blue-grey, five values each. Value steps by one per row (shelf height); the family alternates by aisle. Figure against ground inside a cell is at least 3:1.
4. *Lens.* One peak, on the bin to pick: 2.4× (211px). The ring three cells out compresses to 0.6× (53px, still a touch target). Beyond it cells are 88px. Lens diameter 60% of the short side; the 88 cells are all still there and still joined.
5. *Exception.* The bin to pick is the one inverted unit (dark figure on light ground becomes light on dark), and it is also the peak and carries the only quantity label. Out-of-stock substitutes swap the figure, not the colour.
- *Type.* Bin codes in fixed-pitch capitals on the cell ground, shown only in cells of 1× or more.
- *Motion.* Confirming a pick moves the peak to the next bin; cells rescale by distance in 400ms. Reduced motion: the lens jumps.
- *Phone.* At 390px the rim would be 26px, so the lens is dropped and only steps 1–3 and 5 run (a 4-column field at 88px).
Result: a grey stockroom map that swells around the next pick. No primaries, no discs-on-black, no sphere poster. What survived is the closed two-layer alphabet, the ramp between neighbours and one displacement function.

## Space & composition
- **The square cell is the only container.** Use a strict square module, such as 8×8 or 12×12 cells, filling a square or near-square field. Avoid 12-column gutters. Cells touch edge to edge with no gaps, and any spacing comes from the inner form shrinking inside its cell. Columns may be staggered by part of a cell, as in Blue and Green.
- **Each cell has two layers:** a ground square and one inner figure (circle, square, rotated square, ellipse, half-disc), inset at 55–90% of the cell size (measured 54% for a square, 89% for a disc in Blue and Green). Figure and ground swap colors across the field.
- **Use a field-wide displacement function rather than local layout.** The Vega bulge is a radial lens: cells at the peak grow to about 2.2× and cells at the lens rim compress to about 0.7× before returning to 1× (measured, Album I Vega). The 1955/59 Vega pairs a bulge with a mirror pinch lens (0.4×); the lens sits off-center. Vega-Nor (1969) pushes harder and centres it: ≈2.9× at the peak, ≈0.5× at the rim, sphere ≈85% of the field.
- **No empty space.** Calm areas come from cutting contrast between neighboring tones, not from leaving blanks.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

Vasarely's numbered palette had three reds, three greens, three blues, two violets, two yellows, black, white and grey. The logic is **tonal ramps across the grid**: hue fixed along one axis, value stepping along the other. Figure and ground contrast strongly within a cell, but neighboring cells differ by one step, so the field shimmers instead of clashing.

| Role | Hex |
|---|---|
| Deep ground / night field | `#1B1C17` (Vega frame, 64% of the sheet) |
| Ultramarine (primary ramp anchor) | `#0034C6` (Blue and Green) |
| Violet (ramp midpoint) | `#9F2A40` (no true violet in the works checked; nearest is Banya's crimson-plum, ~11%; the Fondation grid shows plum #994557; in Vega-Nor the midpoint of the crimson-to-black frame ramp is a darker plum #741236, ~7%) |
| Vermilion (warm accent / focal) | `#EA3C23` (Banya, ~11%) |
| Chrome yellow (highest-energy figure) | `#FBCC0A` (Fondation installation photo; lighting-dependent) |

| Paper white | `#F9FAF4` (Vega checker white) |

Measured: a single work keeps to one or two hue families (blues + greens, or reds + greens), with 4–6 value steps per family; the black/white works split roughly 40/40 with a mid-grey edge.

Use these as endpoints; interpolate 6–9 OKLCH lightness steps per hue.

## Form, line & typography
The shapes are a short list of hard-edged, strokeless primitives: circle, square, lozenge, ellipse, split disc. Line appears only as **stripe fields** (Zebra, Renault), where a contour is just a change in stripe direction or phase. Type should be geometric sans in caps, tracked 0.2em or more so letters read as units in a row.
- **Jost** (Futura-like) for headings and UI.
- **Rubik Mono One** for blocky display numerals.
- **Space Mono** for unit codes like "C-12 / R3," echoing the codes on the Folklore tiles.

## Rhythm, motion & time
Motion is a **wave of value or scale passing through the grid**, never an object moving across it. On hover or focus the lens moves to that element, and neighbors scale by distance (CSS `scale` from a `--d` custom property, 300–500ms, ease-in-out). Load-in sweeps tones diagonally, 15–25ms per cell. Stripe fields shift phase a few pixels on scroll, echoing his Kinetic-period layered Plexiglas. Under `prefers-reduced-motion`, freeze the bulge; the illusion works when still.

## UI translations
1. Build every surface from one square token, `--u` (e.g. 56px): cards, avatars, buttons are 1u, 2u or 4u, never off-multiple rectangles.
2. Define components as **ground + inner form + color index**, e.g. `<Unit form="disc" ground="blue-3" figure="yellow-1">`. Style variants are permutations, not new designs.
3. Place a Vega-style radial lens on the dashboard: the main KPI tile sits at the bulge peak at about 2.2×, the ring around it drops to 0.7×, and tiles outside the lens stay at 1×.
4. Color each list row one step further along a ramp (`blue-1` → `blue-7`) to show order or time, so the sequence is visible before anyone reads it.
5. For states, swap figure and ground instead of adding a border or shadow: a selected tile inverts its two colors.
6. Draw empty/loading states as Zebra stripe fields whose stripes taper about 3:1 and bend, with shapes defined only by where stripe direction changes (no outlines).
7. Build the logo or hero mark from offset parallel stripes, with opposite corners shifted in opposite directions, to get depth with no gradients.
8. Ship a "Folklore" mode where users rearrange their home grid, saved as a shareable code (`D3-B5-Y1…`).
9. Set headings in widely tracked Jost caps; label swatches and forms with catalogue codes in Space Mono.
10. Break the system exactly once per screen: one inverted unit marks the main call to action.

## What this artist would reject
- Drop shadows and blur as depth; depth comes from distortion.
- One-off hero illustrations outside the component grammar.
- Beige-and-grey neutrals with a single brand accent.
- Rounded rectangles with arbitrary padding and radii.
- Outlined icons and hairline borders; edges are meetings of flat color.
- Layouts users can't recombine.

## Signature prompt seed
Design this app screen as a Vasarely plastic-unit field. The whole canvas is a seamless grid of square cells, each a flat colored ground holding one inset geometric form (disc, square, lozenge, ellipse). Components are permutations of form and numbered color, never one-offs. Run ultramarine-to-violet tonal ramps across rows, with chrome yellow and vermilion figures. Bulge the grid radially around the primary content so its tiles swell to 2× and the edges compress. Show states by inverting figure and ground, and use Zebra stripes for emptiness. Set the type in tracked geometric caps. Interactions send scale waves through neighboring cells.
