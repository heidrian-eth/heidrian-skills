# Julio Le Parc — Kinetic art (Argentine; GRAV)

## Essence
Le Parc picks a small, closed set of parts (14 pure hues, a few hundred mirrored squares, one light source) and lets a rule, a breath of air, or the visitor's body produce endless variations of them. The work is never a fixed image. It is a system that is running, and the viewer is expected to set it off, rearrange it, or even break it. His game works rest on one idea: taking part matters more than finishing.

## Key works examined
- **Fourteen Colors – Sequence, 1959** (Met; gouache, 49.7 cm square). A grid of squares and circles where shifting color steps make a still image look like it moves. *(not obtained: the Met API returns no image for this object. - **Projet Couleur / Feuilles du Dossier Couleur, 1959–70.** Colors are written down as numbers, and arrangements are worked out like permutations. He reckoned that painting every combination of one method would take 150 years. *(not visually checked; the 150-year figure is confirmed in his own text "La couleur" on julioleparc.org)*
- **Double Concurrence—Continuous Light, 2, 1961** (MoMA). A black box with a 20 × 20 cm lit opening. Inside, 54 plastic squares of 3.6 cm hang on 18 threads in front of a mirror, with screens and filters you can swap. It is a tiny stage for light. *(not obtained: moma.org blocked. Related boxes checked: **Mobile argent sur noir, 1960** (153 × 153 cm, official site): mirror rectangles on threads, ~11 columns × 16 rows, filling ~50% of a black panel #131313 (78% of the photo is black); **Formes virtuelles en situations diverses**, Tate T03774: an open black box split in two by a mirror, black/white concentric ellipses and lens shapes distorted by curved mirrored walls.)*
- **Continuel-Lumière Mobile, 1960 onward.** Curtains of small steel discs or squares on nylon, lit by projectors. The real artwork is the moving spatter of reflections they throw on the room. *(visually checked: **Continual Mobile, Continual Light, 1963**, Tate T00678: a 7 × 7 grid of small two-sided squares on nylon threads in front of a white ground #F1F0F2; the grid fills ~66% × 66% (≈44%) of the face; squares tilt at random so each reads pale or dark. Official site: **Continuel-lumière mobile, 1960–67** (~12 × 15 squares, grey ground) and **Continuel mobile, NT Louvre, 1964** (black squares on black). **Lumière en mouvement, 1962–99**: a 4 × 4 grid of black cells in which green-white light crescents curve in different directions. **Continuel-couleurs-lumière, 1965**: blue light discs in vertical rows on near-black.)*
- **Une journée dans la rue (GRAV), 19 April 1966.** A day-long schedule across Paris: gifts handed out at the Châtelet metro at 8 a.m., buildable structures on the Champs-Élysées, a giant kaleidoscope, and paving slabs at Montparnasse that moved underfoot. *(visually checked: Tate Etc. photograph of one station, "Unstable Floor in GRAV's A Day in The Street 1966", b/w)* Seen: about nine identical square dark slabs, roughly shin-high, laid loosely edge to edge on the pavement, each tilting a few degrees under a foot; passers-by in street clothes cross them with arms held out for balance. The work is only a closed set of identical units whose state (tilt) is set by the user's weight; nothing is coloured or signed.
- **Miroir en vibration and 3 jeux avec balles de ping-pong, 1965.** Press a button, and something shivers or a ball rolls through a course. The museum piece becomes a toy. *(visually checked as the official site's photo plates for **Miroirs en vibration, 1965** and **Jeu avec deux balles de ping-pong, 1965**: black-and-white photos of perforated panels, a spray of rods and blurred light traces of the moving parts; no colour.)*
- **La Longue Marche, 1974.** Ten 200 × 200 cm canvases. A ribbon in the 14 colors winds across all of them with no center and no hierarchy. *(visually checked: secondary source, wearethemutants.com, images credited to Galerie Perrotin: all ten panels plus an installation view)* Seen: one band of hue stripes running from dark blue-violet through crimson, orange, yellow and green back to dark blue (11–14 stripes distinguishable at this size), edged dark on both sides, ≈18% of the panel side wide. On every panel the band enters at the left edge and leaves at the right edge at exactly the same height (≈40–58% down), so the ten squares chain into one continuous line; inside each panel it is knotted differently: overlapping ovals, a meander, interlinked rings, a plait, an S-loop, a reef knot, a comb of vertical loops. The pale grey ground covers 23–54% depending on the knot. Within a panel the figure is roughly centred; across the row there is no centre: the installation reads as an even line of knots.
- **Séquences progressives, 1959; Instabilité, 1959; Instabilité visuelle, 1959–70; Double progression, 1959–91** *(added, visually checked: official site)*: black/white (and grey) fields: cut discs rotating step by step on black; irregular black blobs on white drifting in rows; a square grid where dot size steps 0 → max → 0 toward a calm centre. The progression logic predates colour.
- **Untitled, 1969**, Tate P08217 *(added, visually checked)*: a screenprint of a single airbrushed Y-shape in blue-greys on a grainy olive-black ground. Le Parc did use soft gradients, in his "Modulation" period; the strict flat 14-hue rule belongs to the colour series.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Space** (La Longue Marche): a row of equal squares; one band of fixed width (≈18% of the side) enters and exits each square at the same mid-height point, so continuity is guaranteed at every seam; between the seams the band may loop, overlap and fold freely. Ground share varies by knot (23–54%), which gives the row its rhythm of dense and open panels.
- **Space** (Une journée dans la rue, Unstable Floor): a small field of identical units on the ground plane, no frame, no centre; the public walks through it.
- **The procedure** (Unstable Floor): lay out identical units; give each one a single degree of freedom driven directly by the user's body; leave everything else neutral.
- **Colour relations** (La Longue Marche): the stripes keep their order, so colour is a band, never a field or a point; but they do not always travel together: in at least three panels the band splits lengthwise (at its lightest step) into two strands that take separate paths and rejoin. Both outer edges are the darkest stripe, so where the band crosses itself the overlap reads by value (dark edge over light interior). Ground is a neutral pale grey, lower in chroma than any stripe.
- **Line and edge:** all stripes equal width, hard-edged; curves are constant-radius arcs joined to straights.
- **The procedure:** fix the hue sequence and the band width; fix one entry and one exit point per frame on the same axis; generate a different knot topology in each frame (loop, ring, plait, meander), never repeating one; chain the frames into a row with no designated centre.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Julio Le Parc.** *Close the set of parts and the order of colours, state one rule, and let the rule, chance and the user's hand run every variation, with no variation and no part ranked above another.*
Process:
1. Close the set: one unit (two alternating at most) on an even lattice of 7 × 7 up to about 29 × 29, and one ordered colour sequence of fixed length (14 in the works measured) at full strength, every step the same width. Nothing enters from outside the set, and no step is tinted or shaded.
2. Never show a colour alone. Colours appear as runs in sequence order (the whole run, or a mirrored one). Order the sequence so that value rises to one light step in mid-run and falls to dark at both ends: a run then reads in value as well as hue, and where it crosses itself the dark edge separates the layers.
3. Write one generating rule (advance one step per cell, rotate by a fixed angle, nest, mirror) and apply it without exception. Number the outputs as a series and show the rule.
4. Give each unit one degree of freedom and hand it to something outside the author: air, light, or the user's hand or weight. Keep everything else about the unit neutral.
5. Frame the running system: the active lattice takes 44–50% of the face, on a neutral ground lower in chroma than any step.
6. Series mode: carry one band of the full run (15–19% of a panel's side) through a row of equal panels. It enters and leaves every panel at the same point on the same axis; between the seams it takes a different path each time (ground 23–54%), and it may split lengthwise at its lightest step and rejoin. The row has no centre.
7. Make every state reversible: one action scatters the arrangement and one restores it.
Why it works:
- A closed set with a visible rule lets the viewer predict the next state, so any arrangement is a legitimate variation and none is an error. That is what makes handing over control safe. Hide the rule and the same output reads as randomness or as a trick.
- Equal steps in a fixed order give no step more area or a special place, so the eye follows the sequence and not a hero. A colour used alone becomes a signal, and hierarchy returns.
- Fixed seams carry continuity from panel to panel (good continuation), which frees everything between them to vary; the value wave keeps the band one object through its own overlaps.
Colour relations: n ordered steps, equal in area; value is a single wave across the run (lightness measured L* 22 → 88 → 33), so the steps are far from equal in contrast; hue intervals uneven; ground neutral, 20–54%; no tint, no blend between steps, no lone step; type takes no colour from the sequence.
Space and line: even lattice, 7–29 units per side; active area 44–50% of the face; band 15–19% of the side, seams at one height; curves are constant-radius arcs joined to straights; hard edges; no centre across a series (a single panel may be concentric or mirrored).
Type requirement: a fixed-pitch voice with tabular numerals for series codes and counts ("07/14"), and a flat, even-width display voice; type is monochrome; labels sit beside a run and never on it, because contrast changes at every step.
Motion and interaction: idle units drift at low amplitude on unsynchronised periods of 6–11s, but only under a named idle state (a class such as `is-idle`, set while nothing in the lattice is focused, hovered or pressed), with a visible pause control. Under reduced motion the idle state is static: each unit frozen at a fixed, staggered tilt. Sequences advance in discrete steps, one per action, with no flashing. A state is a position in the sequence plus its number, never the colour alone.
Fails when: one thing truly outranks the rest (a critical alert, a single purchase action); colour must mean something fixed (status); the items are not interchangeable; rearranging has a real cost that cannot be undone.
Signature (do not carry together): a rainbow-ordered striped band tied in loops · small mirrored squares hanging on threads in a black box · concentric rings of 14 colours.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Le Parc's procedure → a release-checklist tool in dark slate (desktop, 1440 × 900). Sober, monochrome, a place where people expect a red alert.
1. *Closed set.* One unit: a 44px square plate, 9 × 9 = 81 plates, one per check. One sequence of 8 steps in a single slate-to-bone arc, every step the same width. No ninth tone exists anywhere in the product.
2. *Runs, never one.* Value goes dark, light at step 5, dark again. The sequence is always visible whole, as an 8-step strip above the lattice. A plate shows its own step plus its two neighbours as thin edge bands, so no tone stands alone.
3. *Rule.* "Advance one step each time a check passes a stage." The header shows the rule and the series number: "Run 07/14 · order: owner, shift +1". A permute control changes only the order, and prints the new rule.
4. *One degree of freedom.* A plate can only move along the 8 steps, and only when its owner or the pipeline moves it. Size, shape and place never change.
5. *Frame.* The lattice is 620px square: 48% of a 900px-square face, centred in the left of the window on a ground with less chroma than step 1. Captions and the log sit on the flat ground to the right.
6. *Series.* The last ten runs are ten equal panels in a row. One band of the 8 steps, 18% of a panel's height, enters and leaves each panel at 50% height; its path between the seams is that run's order of events, so no two panels match and none is larger.
7. *Reversible.* "Scatter" resets every plate to step 1 after one confirmation; "Restore" returns the last run.
- *States.* Each plate has a fixed caption beneath it with its step as a numeral ("5/8"). Failed checks are listed in words in the log; they are not a different colour.
- *Idle.* While the lattice has class `is-idle`, plates still waiting tilt ±4° on 6–11s periods; a pause button sits in the header. Reduced motion: they rest at fixed tilts of 0–4°, staggered by row.
Result: a grey board of small plates that the team advances together, with its history as a chain of knots. No rainbow, no mirrors, no black box. What survived is the closed set, colours only as ordered runs, the stated rule and state handed to the user.

## Space & composition
- **Modular square cells.** The Séries canvases are square (171 × 171, 120 × 120 cm), the openings are square, and the units are square. Build on a strict square module (for example 8 px × n) and never use a golden-ratio layout. The 1974 Ondes and Volumes virtuels break this with 1:2 and 1:3 formats.
- **The sequence is the focal point.** La Longue Marche (seen in a secondary source) has no centre across its ten panels, though each knot sits roughly centred in its own square, but the 1970 Séries checked are strictly centre-symmetric: Série 14-5E and 14 n°15 are 14 concentric rings, Série 29 mirrors its runs from yellow edges to a violet centre. Nothing is a hero element; the order of the run leads the eye, whether it spreads across the field or radiates from the middle.
- **Two grounds.** The colour paintings sit on off-white canvas (#F6EEE0; 20% of Série 14-5E, 52% of Ondes 110). The light works sit in black boxes. The 1961 box reportedly gives the light ~15% of the face (unverified), but in the mobiles checked the active grid fills ~44–50% of the face. In UI: near-black chrome around a large active panel (about half the viewport), or an off-white page for colour sequences.
- **Panels as frames of a film.** Series like Séquences progressives and the ten-panel Longue Marche read like stills from one movie. Lay out carousels and dashboards as numbered panels where each one moves the rule forward a single step.
- **Density from repeating one unit.** Identical squares (7 × 7 in the Tate mobile), or at most two alternating units (squares and discs in Série 29's ~29 × 29 grid, with thin white gaps). Use one tile component and let the variety come from position and state.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

His rule was strict: 14 pure hues, never mixed with black or white, no colors outside the set, and no color used alone. They always come as a run or scale. He got there by starting with 7, then going to 10, then 12. Treat the 14 as a closed hue ring and spend all your contrast on *order*, not on tints.

|---|---|---|
| Ground / chrome | `#131313` | the black box (Mobile argent sur noir, 78% of the photo) |
| Light / reflections | `#F1F0F2` | the white ground and glints of the Tate Continual Mobile |
| Ring step 1 | `#D10103` | red (measured step 3 of 14, Série 14-5E) |
| Ring step 4 | `#FEB400` | orange-yellow (measured step 6) |
| Ring step 7 | `#00A63B` | green (measured step 9) |
| Ring step 10 | `#1B2783` | ultramarine (measured step 13) |
| Ring step 13 | `#3A0209` | no magenta exists in the ring: the dark end is aubergine (`#3A0209` in Série 14-5E, `#491035` in Série 14 n°15), and the ring closes from violet through aubergine back to crimson |
| Canvas ground (added) | `#F6EEE0` | off-white canvas around the colour works (20–52% of the surface) |

**The measured ring** (Série 14-5E, centre outward; each ring an equal band, 20–25 px of a 350 px radius, i.e. 1/14 of the run):
`#3A0209 #890111 #D10103 #E60301 #FE5F00 #FEB400 #FED61B #87CD36 #00A63B #00825E #005A62 #003D69 #1B2783 #342D56`. Série 14 n°15 repeats the same order (crimson #910921, red #D00A12, orange #FE6A01, yellow #FDD831, green #01AE52, emerald #008B6B …) starting from the blue end.

**The ring is NOT equal-lightness.** OKLab L runs 0.22 (aubergine) → 0.40 → 0.54 → 0.58 → 0.69 → 0.82 → **0.88 (yellow)** → 0.77 → 0.63 → 0.54 → 0.43 → 0.35 → 0.33 → 0.33 (violet). These are full-strength pigments, so the sweep is also a value wave: dark → light at yellow → dark. Hue spacing is uneven too: three steps sit within 25–29° (crimson, red, vermilion), then jumps of ~37° on either side of yellow (orange → orange-yellow, yellow → yellow-green). Do not generate the ring with an evenly spaced, constant-L OKLCH formula; use the measured hexes. Show colours as ordered runs (the paintings show all 14 in order, or mirrored runs as in Série 29). A single colored badge breaks the system.

## Form, line & typography
The shapes are the square, the circle, the thin vertical thread, and the band that cuts a circle into slices. The type should feel equally modular and engineered, like a lab notebook more than editorial design. Write labels the way he titled his works, as series codes: "Série 14-2 · n°3".
- **Space Mono** for codes, counts, and step numbers.
- **Archivo** (wide/expanded) for display, like the flat, even letters of 1960s exhibition posters.
- **IBM Plex Sans** for body text.
Keep the type monochrome on the dark ground. Color lives in the system, not in the words.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- **Continuous, low-amplitude drift.** Continuel means always running. Idle states should never freeze. Give tiles a slow, out-of-sync sway (rotateY ±12°, 6–11 s periods with randomized phase), like squares on nylon in moving air.
- **Light that slides across a surface.** Reflections crawl over walls. Animate a soft radial highlight across cards with `background-position`, tied to the cursor or to device tilt.
- **Stepping, not easing.** The color sequences move forward in discrete steps. Use `steps(14)` timing for hue cycles, the way a gouache series moves one frame at a time.
- **Pulse and jolt.** Lumière saccadée and the "surprise movement" boxes jerk around. Add one deliberate `steps()` jitter on activation, then return to calm drift.

## UI translations
1. Build the main surface as a square grid of identical tiles (7 × 7 in the Tate mobile, up to ~29 × 29 in Série 29; at most two alternating shapes). Every piece of content is a tile state, never a different component.
2. Give every colored sequence its hues in ring order, at least 3 adjacent steps. Never use a lone accent color.
3. Use no tints or shades of the accent hues. Hover and disabled states change *position in the sequence* or opacity, not lightness. Because the ring's own steps range from L 0.22 to 0.88, a step move already changes contrast: check text contrast at every step, and put labels outside the coloured run.
4. Frame the active workspace as a lit window set in near-black chrome; in the mobiles checked the lit grid fills ~45–50% of the face (the 15% figure for the 1961 box is unverified).
5. Number everything like a series: "Variation 07 / 14". Show progress as moving along the ring.
6. Add a "permute" control that reshuffles the layout or palette order by a rule. Show the rule (for example "shift +3") so the variations are legible.
7. Let a cursor-following light source throw reflected squares (CSS `box-shadow` or canvas) onto the background, like a Continuel-Lumière mobile.
8. Animate idle tiles with independent sway on random phases. Respect `prefers-reduced-motion` by swapping sway for a static staggered offset.
9. Make onboarding a timed itinerary like Une journée dans la rue: small gifts (a reward or a preset) at fixed points, each asking the user to *do* something physical, such as drag, tilt, or rearrange.
10. Make it safe to break things. Give users a one-tap "dismantle" that scatters the layout and a "reassemble" that puts it back. Messing it up is part of the work.
11. Lay type flat on dark: Archivo Expanded headings in off-white, Space Mono series codes, and no colored text.
12. Hand over agency: buttons should trigger visible mechanical results (a vibration, a rolling marker), not just change a route.

## What this artist would reject
- The single brand accent color sprinkled across badges. Colors only appear as an ordered scale.
- Hero-centric layouts that point the eye at one star element.
- Frozen, finished screens. A UI with no idle life is a picture, not a system.
- Gradient blends and tinted pastels in a colour sequence. He kept the 14 hues pure, flat and hard-edged (his airbrushed 1969 "Modulation"-type prints are a separate, monochrome register).
- Mystified "magic" interactions that hide their rules. GRAV's manifesto, Assez de mystifications, demanded a conscious participant.
- Precious, untouchable interfaces where nothing can be rearranged or undone.

## Signature prompt seed
Design this screen as a running kinetic system in the manner of Julio Le Parc. Use a near-black chrome around a lit square window (about half the viewport) holding a square grid of identical tiles. Colors come only from his measured ring of 14 pure pigment hues (aubergine, crimson, red, vermilion, orange, orange-yellow, yellow, yellow-green, green, emerald, teal, petrol, ultramarine, violet), whose lightness rises to yellow and falls to violet; always shown as equal-width ordered runs, never alone, never tinted. Tiles sway on independent slow phases, and a cursor-driven light throws reflected squares across the background. Label everything as series codes in Space Mono ("Série 14-2 · n°5"). Add permute, dismantle, and reassemble controls with visible rules, so the user plays, breaks, and rebuilds it.
