## Index: one-line processes (scout here first, then read only the entries you need)
**Bauhaus**
- **Josef Albers**: Freeze the geometry so that colour is the only variable, then tune each colour by its neighbours until one edge dissolves and another jumps.
- **Herbert Bayer**: Decide the order in which things must reach the eye, then give each rank a channel of its own (size, the one colour, orientation, attachment) and delete every variant that carries no information.
- **Wassily Kandinsky**: Set opposed forces against each other on a charged, mostly open plane: one heavy pole that holds the field, lighter counter-forces that pull away from it, and every role distinct in several properties at once.
- **Paul Klee**: Grow the surface in strata from one small module: tile the whole field, step the tone tile by tile toward a core, then let a fine lattice, a single thin line and one odd note travel over it.
- **László Moholy-Nagy**: Treat every surface as a filter of light with a declared state, and let overlap carry depth: wherever two planes cross, a third value must appear.

**De Stijl**
- **Piet Mondrian**: Strike an equilibrium between unequal parts: one large quiet mass held in place by small intense counterweights at the frame.
- **Theo van Doesburg**: Set two orders against each other at one angle, the order the content obeys and the order the field obeys, on a shared module, so the surface never comes to rest.
- **Gerrit Rietveld**: Keep every part whole and readable as itself: let parts pass each other at the joints instead of merging, and spend colour only on what ends or moves.
- **Bart van der Leck**: Reduce a real subject to the fewest separate marks that still read as it, and let the untouched field between the marks do the joining.
- **Vilmos Huszár**: Build word, picture and surface from one unit, and lay colour over them as a separate sheet, so any arrangement of the pieces is a finished composition.

**Constructivism & Suprematism**
- **Kazimir Malevich**: Make the empty field the subject: float a few unequal planes in it, clear of the frame, and rank them so that the largest is the quietest and the loudest is the thinnest.
- **El Lissitzky**: Set one active form against one passive mass on a line of force and make everything else point along it, so that the eye, and then the hand, travels a route.
- **Alexander Rodchenko**: Fix one source and make the whole surface project from it: the message swells toward the reader, the ground converges back on the source, and the darkest colour holds the loudest one.
- **Lyubov Popova**: Lay two systems over each other, soft fields beneath and hard vectors above, and tie the vectors to the frame so that the surface reads as a structure holding a load.
- **Varvara Stepanova**: Set a tiny budget first (one unit, one way of building, two inks and the ground) and get every variety from repetition, offset, inversion and use.

**Swiss / International Typographic Style**
- **Josef Müller-Brockmann**: Measure the field from the text line, hang everything from one off-centre axis, and let a single figure generated by a ratio carry all the scale while the words stay small and exact.
- **Armin Hofmann**: Oppose one hard, still sign and one soft, moving image at extreme scale, let them touch at exactly one point, and give changing information a fixed slot so the opposition never has to move.
- **Emil Ruder**: Compose the unprinted area first, as the largest shape on the page; make every dark thing a grey texture of one family; and produce variety by stepping one variable at a time.
- **Karl Gerstner**: Do not compose: list the parameters of the problem, give each a short ordered row of values, and treat every design as one selection, so the set of selections is the work.
- **Wim Crouwel**: Declare one square cell and build everything from whole cells, the big word included; freeze the constants of the series, so the only free choices left are one whole-region colour and what the cells spell.
- **Wolfgang Weingart**: Keep the inherited structure in plain sight and overdrive one of its variables at a time (spacing, step, frame weight, layer) until text starts to read as image, while a plainly set copy stays within reach.

**Modernist Architecture**
- **Le Corbusier**: Measure everything on two interlocked scales taken from one body dimension, free the partitions from the structure, and string the views on one continuous path; colour then sets each plane's depth.
- **Ludwig Mies van der Rohe**: Make the system so regular that it disappears, so attention falls on the space flowing between a few free planes and on the one surface where all the richness is spent.
- **Frank Lloyd Wright**: Grow every part from one unit, pin the whole to one heavy core, and throw long low bands outward past their support, so the eye is led out along the horizon.
- **Alvar Aalto**: Start from the body's real posture, keep a strict plain frame, and break it exactly once with a free curve where people gather; strong colour marks only what hands and feet follow.
- **Luis Barragán**: Shut the world out, then ration what returns: one colour, one light and one opening per room, each felt against the room before.

**Japanese Ma & Wabi-Sabi**
- **Katsushika Hokusai**: Keep one small subject constant and make each view by changing what stands in front of it: the largest, busiest form is context, and its hollow points at the subject.
- **Tadao Ando**: Close the container in one surface treatment, empty its middle, and admit the opposite end of the value scale through a single exact cut that the user must cross the emptiness to reach.
- **Kenya Hara**: Leave the surface nearly bare, spend its only saturation on one figure, and press every piece of information back to the edges, so the viewer supplies the meaning.
- **Ikko Tanaka**: Assemble the image from a few flat planes snapped to a coarse grid and held by one dark mass, so that every edge is two colours meeting and the eye finishes the figure.
- **Kazuyo Sejima (SANAA)**: Remove rank: pack unequal rooms into one soft envelope at a single line weight and a constant gap, and let the only emphasis be a small shift of position or what shows through.

**Art Nouveau & Vienna Secession**
- **Alphonse Mucha**: Build a fixed frame that does the pointing (one large quiet shape behind the focus, bands that hold the words), then change only one colour key and one emblem per variant.
- **Gustav Klimt**: Flood the surface with intensity, then make the one calm, differently rendered patch the focus; separate things by a change of texture, not by lines.
- **Antoni Gaudí**: Let position decide everything: a strict repeated structure whose members change girth where they carry more, coloured one step at a time along its length, with the colour broken into fragments and allowed to fall on the neutral parts.
- **Josef Hoffmann**: Take one square as the unit of everything, leave the planes blank, and let a thin line on every edge and a cluster of small squares at every opening do all the describing.
- **Charles Rennie Mackintosh**: Raise tall plain bays in a strict repeat, spend all the ornament at one height and all the irregularity in one narrow bay.

**Minimalism / Light & Space**
- **Donald Judd**: Abolish ranking: one stated rule sets both the units and the gaps between them, so the interval counts as much as the thing and the run is read as a whole.
- **Agnes Martin**: Hold every difference just above the threshold of notice: one even division and near-equal fills, so the surface is a single tone from far away and a patient structure up close.
- **Dan Flavin**: Colour is thrown, not applied: thin sources on the edges light an empty field, and zones are made by whose light reaches them.
- **James Turrell**: Hold one field and one opening still, and change what the opening looks like only by changing its surround, more slowly than the eye can follow.
- **Sol LeWitt**: State a rule, run it to exhaustion, and show every result in an identical cell in sorted order: completeness replaces choice.

**Op & Kinetic Art**
- **Bridget Riley**: Repeat one unit over the whole field, lock every property but one, and move that one along a single axis on a composed tempo: long calm, geometric build, off-centre climax, shorter release.
- **Victor Vasarely**: Write a closed alphabet of two-layer units, fill the field with it without a gap, then bend the whole lattice with one function, so that volume and focus come from distortion and never from shading.
- **Carlos Cruz-Diez**: Do not paint the colour you want: set narrow bands side by side so that it arises between them, and make which colour arises depend on where the viewer is.
- **Jesús Rafael Soto**: Hang a sparse layer of identical strokes a small gap in front of a dense one, draw no shape, and let form exist only as density, as one hue, and as the interference the viewer's own movement makes.
- **Julio Le Parc**: Close the set of parts and the order of colours, state one rule, and let the rule, chance and the user's hand run every variation, with no variation and no part ranked above another.

**Postmodern & New Wave**
- **Ettore Sottsass (Memphis)**: Build the thing as a standing figure: separately treated parts joined in the open, mirrored about one axis, and balanced on a base narrower than its reach.
- **April Greiman**: Compose in depth, not on the sheet: stack planes that each keep the grain of the tool that made them, tilt the planes, and keep the words level.
- **David Carson**: On a surface meant to be looked at before it is read, let one display element carry the feeling of the piece as an image, against a quiet ground and square small text (expressive surfaces only).
- **Paula Scher**: Fill the frame with words at the volume they would be spoken, and hold the crowd with one blunt structure: one flat ground, one host shape and one rule about angle.

**Growth & Form**
- **Ernst Haeckel**: Order by counting: build every body from one unit turned a whole number of times, double the count at each ring outward, and seat the bodies in mirrored slots round one axis.
- **Karl Blossfeldt**: Subtract until only the repeating module is left, enlarge it to fill the frame on its axis, and let the interval between repeats, never the module, carry time.
- **Frei Otto**: Place only the supports and say which edges are free; everything between them is found by relaxing one continuous surface to equilibrium, so every change is shared by the whole field.
- **Barbara Hepworth**: Make one closed convex whole, then open it once, off-centre; the opening, finished as the inside of the same surface, becomes the subject.
- **Nervous System**: Design the rule and not the object: a seed, an environment and one gradient, stepped locally until the space is full, and shown as a family of outcomes.

### 4.1 BAUHAUS: function, primary geometry, color as perception
The movement: form follows role, shape and color carry meaning, and design is a teachable grammar.
*The problem it answered:* How to make design teachable: reduce a surface to elementary variables (point, line, plane, value, hue, material state) and find out what each one does to the eye. Form is then derived from the job and from perception, one variable at a time, instead of from style or ornament.

**Josef Albers.** *Freeze the geometry so that colour is the only variable, then tune each colour by its neighbours until one edge dissolves and another jumps.*
Process:
1. Fix one layout of 3–4 regions and use it unchanged on every view. From here on nothing but fill may vary, so every difference the viewer sees is a colour difference.
2. Arrange the regions so each one borders only its neighbours in a single order, outermost to core (nested, or stepped from one side). No region touches a non-neighbour, so every edge is a controlled pair.
3. Offset each inward step unequally: `u` at the sides, 0.5`u` on the heavy side, 1.5`u` on the opposite side (`u` = 1/9–1/10 of the format). The core sits off-centre along one axis and keeps 11–36% of the area.
4. Build one ladder of 3–4 flat, opaque fills that runs monotonically from the outermost region to the core in value, in chroma, or in both. Regions meet at hard edges with no line, gap or shadow between them.
5. Space the ladder unevenly: outer neighbours 3–5 L apart at near-equal chroma; the last step into the core is the big one, and the core is the extreme of the ladder (highest chroma, about 1.4× its neighbour or more, or the lightest or darkest value).
6. Judge every fill in place against its real neighbours, never as a swatch. Before adding a fill, try an existing one on a second ground: one fill on two grounds gives two apparent colours.
7. Make variation serial: the next view or state is the same layout with a new ladder.
Why it works:
- Simultaneous contrast: a fill is pushed away from its surround in value and hue, so a ladder ordered by adjacency controls how every region looks. A region touching a non-neighbour gets an uncontrolled edge and the tuning is lost.
- Edge strength is the hierarchy. Close outer steps fuse into one field and the single large step isolates the core with no outline. Equal steps make a striped target with no focus.
- The unequal offset makes the regions read as stacked planes with weight. Centred, they read as a bullseye or a tunnel, and the eye fixes on the geometry instead of the colour.
Colour relations: one ladder per view, in one hue family or from a near-neutral outer field to one saturated core; outer steps 3–5 L apart; the core the most saturated or the extreme value; area shares run 36 : 28 : 36 (three regions) or 40 : 30 : 20 : 11 (four); no second accent, no transparency, no gradient.
Space and line: no drawn lines; offsets 1 : 0.5 : 1.5 per step; symmetric on one axis and weighted on the other; region sizes 10/8/6, 10/6/4 or 9/7/5/3 units; a step may be skipped.
Type requirement: one plain text voice in few weights, in an ink at the opposite value extreme from the ladder (≥4.5:1 on every region that carries text; text sits on at most two regions); display built from a few modules on the unit `u`.
Motion and interaction: geometry never moves. A state change re-tunes the ladder: the focused region takes the large step (≥3:1 against its neighbour) over 600–900ms, core first; selection also changes a label, mark or weight, because two outer fills 3–5 L apart cannot signal state alone.
Fails when: the content is many peer items (a 20-row table has no 3–4 level order); status hues must appear (they break the ladder); the core holds photographs or video whose colours override the tuning; reading text must sit on mid-value regions.
Signature (do not carry together): concentric squares · the low-set 1 : 3 margin · a single yellow or ochre family in flat unmixed paint.

**Herbert Bayer.** *Decide the order in which things must reach the eye, then give each rank a channel of its own (size, the one colour, orientation, attachment) and delete every variant that carries no information.*
Process:
1. Rank the content into at most four reading ranks: seen at a glance, read, consulted, kept for reference. Everything that follows assigns channels to ranks, so an unranked item cannot be placed.
2. Cut redundant variants before composing: one letter case per rank, one family, marks and glyphs built from 2–3 primitives. A distinction that does not change the meaning is removed.
3. Give rank 1 a single dominant figure. If it is a number or word it takes about 20% of the surface; if it is an image, map or chart it takes 70–90% and text shrinks to an edge column. Never two.
4. Hang all text from one hard edge on the reading side, 5–8% in from the frame (about 1/3 across only when the figure occupies the near side). Mark zones with solid bars, not boxes; one bar may thicken into the field that holds the rank-1 item.
5. Spend the single saturated colour on rank 1 or on the furniture that names the page, never on both. It may be large (up to 20%). Inside data, other hues appear only as low-chroma ramps, plus at most one opposed pair of hairline colours for a paired quantity.
6. Demote by orientation and distance: rank-4 text goes to the far edge (70–90% across) in a narrow column turned 90° (decorative or secondary text only; never a control), at the smallest size that still meets the 12px floor. It is found only by someone who looks for it.
7. Attach every label to the thing it names with a hairline leader ending at the part. No detached legend where a leader will do.
Why it works:
- Size, colour and orientation are separate preattentive channels. With one channel per rank, the order is read before any word is. Two ranks sharing a channel (two sizes that are close, two accents) make the eye arbitrate, and the sequence is lost.
- Salience is oddness: one saturated element among neutrals wins at any size. A second one halves it; a saturated data palette under it drowns it, which is why data colour is kept low in chroma.
- A leader replaces a look-up. A detached legend makes the reader hold a code in memory and travel back and forth; connection does the same work in one fixation.
Colour relations: neutral ground and one ink carry everything but rank 1; one saturated colour, ≤20%, in one place; data ramps of low chroma in 3 steps; one opposed pair as hairlines only; a neutral bar may stand in for the saturated one in a variant.
Space and line: one hard text edge at 5–8% (or ≈1/3); one dominant figure; bars of two kinds (a solid bar, and a band of thin rules under it); hairline leaders; a rotated edge column at 70–90% across; no boxes.
Type requirement: one family with a heavy and a regular weight, simple round forms and no redundant alternates; one case for display, small spaced capitals only for technical labels; numerals strong enough to be the rank-1 figure; rotated text limited to short reference labels, never instructions.
Motion and interaction: a state change is a change of rank. The promoted item takes the rank-1 channel (its bar extends along the text edge and fills, 240–320ms, no overshoot) and the previous holder gives it up; the dominant figure stays pinned while lower ranks pass it; hover and selection always add a bar or weight change, not colour alone.
Fails when: the content has no ranks (a feed or gallery of equal items); several items need the alarm colour at once (an incident board); categories in the chrome need more than one saturated hue; rank-4 text is long or legally required to be legible, so it cannot be rotated or shrunk.
Signature (do not carry together): lowercase geometric sans · heavy black bars with one red block · a text column turned 90° at the right edge.

**Wassily Kandinsky.** *Set opposed forces against each other on a charged, mostly open plane: one heavy pole that holds the field, lighter counter-forces that pull away from it, and every role distinct in several properties at once.*
Process:
1. Tone the whole plane first as an uneven ground: graded from one side to the other by 5–10 L or a shift of temperature, never flat. In the open mode it keeps 65–75% of the area.
2. Place the heavy pole: the darkest mass, round, 20–46% of the width, its centre 12–42% across and 20–36% down, so it sits toward a corner. Give it the largest soft edge on the surface.
3. Answer it on the far side with a counter-mass 1/3–1/2 its size that opposes it in at least two properties (lighter, warmer, more angular, harder). The surface now has two poles and a direction between them.
4. Scatter satellites at 1/3 to 1/12 of the pole in clusters, almost all hard-edged, a few echoing the soft edge at small size. Keep density uneven and leave one quadrant nearly silent (under 5% covered).
5. Bundle the contrasts: every role differs from every other in two or more properties together (shape, temperature, value, edge). The most advancing bundle (warm, light, acute) is the smallest, ≤5% of the area.
6. Where two forms overlap, give the overlap its own third tone, lying between the two and never darker than both.
7. Optional: add lines in two weights about 1 : 5 apart (≈0.2% and ≈1% of the width), oblique, starting in a cluster and ending in open ground, never closing a box.
Why it works:
- Visual weight: a dark, round, soft-edged mass off-centre acts as a centre of gravity, and everything else reads as held by it or escaping from it. Centred, it becomes a target; with no answer on the far side, the plane tips and reads as one object on a background.
- Redundant coding: when shape, temperature and value change together, a role is recognised from any one of them, and the opposition reads as a force, not a colour choice. Change one property alone and the roles blur into decoration.
- A soft edge reads as atmosphere or emitted light and detaches a form from the plane. Kept rare, it marks the pole; given to everything, the surface turns to fog and nothing is near or far.
Colour relations: uneven low-chroma ground 65–75%; the pole holds the darkest value; warm, light, high-chroma notes are the smallest (2–5%); the largest coloured mass is cool; overlaps are a third tone; flat uniform ground and pure paper white are excluded.
Space and line: pole 20–46% of the width, off-centre toward a corner; counter-mass 1/3–1/2 of it; satellites 1/3–1/12; clusters beside open fields, one silent quadrant; two line weights 1 : 5, oblique, open-ended; lines are optional.
Type requirement: two voices that oppose in at least two properties (round and even against sharp and narrow; heavy against light); one word or figure may act as the pole; text sits only on quiet ground, never over clusters, overlaps or the graded soft edge.
Motion and interaction: interaction moves an element along the bundle, never in size or height. An action steps lighter, warmer and harder-edged, fast (120–160ms); focus steps darker and cooler and takes the soft edge as its ring, slowly (400–600ms); committed states change one property and stop dead. Leave a rest of ≥200ms after a burst. Interactive satellites keep a 44px hit area whatever their drawn size.
Fails when: the content is uniform rows, a form or long reading (no poles to set); the ground falls under about half without switching to the dense mode below; satellites are decoration behind text; every element is interactive, so small scattered targets multiply.
Signature (do not carry together): a black-cored, haloed disc at upper left · yellow triangle, red square, blue circle · crossing black diagonals on cream.
Mode (dense): pale ground about 10%; saturated patches cover the rest; the darkest values gather in one off-centre knot (30–45% across) with a smaller dark mass at the far edge. One work only.

**Paul Klee.** *Grow the surface in strata from one small module: tile the whole field, step the tone tile by tile toward a core, then let a fine lattice, a single thin line and one odd note travel over it.*
Process:
1. Choose one module, 1/8–1/12 of the width, and tile the entire field with it. Nothing is left empty and nothing is cropped; tiles may be slightly unequal, but no tile is promoted by size.
2. Choose a core two modules wide (an axis or a zone) and give every tile a ring number by its distance from the core.
3. Step tone by ring, 3–4 steps: value and chroma rise together toward the core. The outermost ring is the largest (40–45% of the area) and the dullest, and its tiles are near neighbours in hue and value.
4. Reserve the extremes for the smallest group: the lightest tiles (≤2% of the area) and the pure fills, each a single tile, no two alike adjacent, set in the core beside their opposites. Or replace them with one round note of 1–2.5% of the area placed in the open zone of the opposite temperature.
5. Lay a second stratum over everything at about 1/10 of the module: an even lattice of small marks (pitch ≈1% of the width, mark ≈40% of the pitch). Its density, not an outline, fills and defines areas.
6. Draw last, with one line weight (≈0.3% of the width): a few lines that outline one large figure (up to about 60% of the field) across the tiles without displacing any. A line may end in a pointer.
Why it works:
- Grouping by gradation: with size held constant, the eye reads the tone steps as a slow approach to the core, so hierarchy needs no large element. Make the tiles unequal in importance by size and the field breaks into cards.
- Scale separation: strata about ten times apart in grain do not compete; the coarse one is read as colour, the fine one as texture, the line as a figure. At similar scales they merge into noise.
- Salience by scarcity: purity and extreme value confined to under 2% make those cells ring against a dull majority. Spread them and the field flattens to confetti.
Colour relations: outer ring dull and dark, 40–45%; each ring inward lighter and more chromatic; pure fills as single non-adjacent tiles; opposites touch only in the core; the one odd note stands in the zone of opposite temperature; the ground tone may double as the fill of many tiles.
Space and line: one module over the whole field; hierarchy concentric, not by size; three grains (module, 1/10 module, hairline); one line weight; an outer margin of 4–8% of the short side with a hairline and small caption beneath, so the whole reads as an object of hand size.
Type requirement: letters that sit in a module like a tile (even widths, tabular figures), stroke close to the drawn line's weight, modest sizes with rank carried by ring tone; text only on tiles that give ≥4.5:1, with no lattice under reading text.
Motion and interaction: things appear in the order they were made: tiles step tone ring by ring (30–40ms a step), the lattice follows, and the line draws itself last (900–1400ms; instantly under reduced motion). Selecting a tile makes it the core: the rings re-step around it by value and chroma, and nothing moves or grows. Pointers carry a text label.
Fails when: the content is one hero image or video (the tiling competes with it); long reading text; fewer than about a dozen units (no rings can form); several saturated alarms must show at once, which destroys the scarcity the core depends on.
Signature (do not carry together): hand-wobbled colour squares · a small red sun with an arrow · wiry pictograms with a handwritten caption.

**László Moholy-Nagy.** *Treat every surface as a filter of light with a declared state, and let overlap carry depth: wherever two planes cross, a third value must appear.*
Process:
1. Build a value ladder of 4–5 steps, at least 8–10 L apart, and give hue no structural job. The ground takes one end of the ladder and, in the open mode, about 80% of the area.
2. Declare a state for every surface from a short list: opaque (a ladder value), translucent (shifts whatever is beneath by exactly one step), transparent (an outline or hairline only). Three or four states for the whole system.
3. Set one oblique axis, 15–35° off the horizontal, and hang the planes along it asymmetrically. Nothing is centred on the format.
4. Repeat one simple form at two or three sizes along the axis, 1 : 0.33 (optionally 0.6 between). The smaller reads as farther.
5. Make every crossing visible. Where planes overlap, shift one ladder step, always in the direction the upper plane's state dictates (it may lighten what is under it). An overlap that changes nothing is not allowed: separate the planes or change a state.
6. Run a few hairlines the full extent of the format. They are the only elements that reach the frame, and they tie the floating planes to it.
Why it works:
- Perceived transparency: when an overlap takes a value consistent with one surface filtering another, the eye infers which is in front without shadow or outline. If the shift is inconsistent or absent, the layers collapse into a flat mosaic.
- Size gradient: the same form repeated at a third of the size is read as the same thing farther away. Different forms at different sizes are read only as big and small.
- With hue out of the structure, value is free to mean one thing, the passage of light. Add structural hue and each overlap needs a colour mixture the eye must also believe.
Colour relations: a neutral or single-hue ladder; ground at one extreme, ≈80% in the open mode; mid values ≈9%, the opposite extreme ≈5%; translucent planes sit one step from the ground; any hue acts as a tint over the ladder, never as a role.
Space and line: one oblique axis at 15–35°; forms at 1 : 0.33; open, asymmetric distribution; hairlines edge to edge in two directions; planes otherwise stay inside the frame (running a plane off the edge is an option, not a rule); no shadows.
Type requirement: one heavy weight that reads as a solid plane on the ladder; text stays on an area of a single value with ≥4.5:1 and crosses a plane boundary only if that holds on both sides; emphasis is a solid bar at the ladder's far end, not a hue.
Motion and interaction: a state change is a change of material state. A layer goes transparent → translucent → opaque (about 400ms) and what lies under it shifts one step; hard cuts between sections, slow dissolves (600–900ms) within one, never repeated fast enough to flicker; a pointer may brighten its neighbourhood by one step, but nothing is revealed by hover alone.
Fails when: status or brand hue must carry meaning; the content is tables or forms (overlap hides data and the oblique axis fights rows); copy has to sit on crossings; displays or contrast modes erase single-step shifts.
Signature (do not carry together): a circle cut by translucent bands on bare canvas · white camera-less silhouettes on black · a tilted glass-like plane with hairlines.

### 4.2 DE STIJL: orthogonal planes, primaries, balance through inequality
The movement: the universal reduced to the rectangle, primary colors plus non-colors, and asymmetric equilibrium.
*The problem it answered:* How do you get order and life from the fewest elements, with no symmetry, no illusion of depth and no ornament? De Stijl's answer: reduce to flat right-angled parts, a neutral field and a few ranked intensities, then make the relations between them (unequal sizes, gaps, crossings, a counter-order) carry everything.

**Piet Mondrian.** *Strike an equilibrium between unequal parts: one large quiet mass held in place by small intense counterweights at the frame.*
Process:
1. Fix the frame and ONE separator weight `t` (1.2–2% of the short side; a line, a gap or a value step). It never varies, so from here on only interval can vary.
2. Place the dominant: the emptiest, quietest cell, 22–53% of the area, off-centre. A large one (≈50%) sits 5–10% of the format from the middle; a small one (≈22%) sits up to a quarter of the format away. It takes the main content and no accent.
3. Subdivide only the leftover strips, never the dominant. Neighbour pairs differ by 1:1.6 to 1:4; a strip may be as thin as 2–4% of the width. Optionally add one interval of about 1.5`t` (a doubled separator) so sizes run from tiny to huge with nothing "almost equal".
4. Load intensity last, as counterweight, in one of two ranges. Chips: the strongest signal fills a cell of 1–8%, the others ≤7% each, total ≤15%. Block: the strongest fills one corner cell touching two edges, up to ≈18%, the others step down about 6:3:1 on the opposite edges, total ≤28%, quiet ground still ≥65%. In both: every signal has a different size and shape, sits in an edge cell or one cell in from it, never in the dominant, and no two signals touch. The larger the signal, the farther out it goes.
5. Open the system: let one or two separators stop at another separator or short of the frame, and step the quiet cells 1–3 L apart, so they read as planes and not as boxes on a background.
6. Test by covering: hide any signal cell. If the layout does not tip, the cell was decoration; remove it or move it outward.
7. Mode for dense live content: move the signals out of the cells and into the separators (separator ≈2.3% wide, carrying separator-wide units; signal share may rise to 35–40%).
Why it works:
- Visual weight is roughly intensity × area × distance from the centre. A small saturated cell far out balances a large pale one near the centre, like a small mass on a long lever. Centred, it becomes a target and the rest a frame; a large one that is not pinned into a corner becomes the ground and the tension is gone.
- With one separator weight, thickness cannot signal rank or depth, so the eye reads the only variable left: spacing. Hierarchy becomes cell size and rhythm becomes interval.
- A signal cut by the frame reads as part of a field that continues beyond it, not as an object on a background. The surface stays flat and the empty cell reads as a thing, not as what was left over.
Colour relations: quiet ground 65–90% in 2–3 near-equal values of one temperature; structure 6–9% at maximum value contrast to the ground; 1–3 signals at full saturation, ranked unequally in area (the largest 2–6× the next), each a flat field separated from the others by quiet cells; a neutral cell (mid-value, or the structure's own value in a corner) may stand in for a signal.
Space and line: one separator weight (max/min ≤1.3–1.5); dominant 22–53%, off-centre; neighbour ratios 1:1.6–1:4; thinnest strip 2–4%; an optional doubled separator with a 1.5`t` gap; one or two separators that stop; right angles only inside the frame.
Type requirement: the same inequality in type: one heavy element and everything else light (two weights, far apart), seated flush against a separator in a cell corner and never centred; a family with a wide weight range and widths that can be fitted to a cell. Text on a signal cell must still reach 4.5:1, or the cell stays empty.
Motion and interaction: a state change is a new equilibrium. Selecting moves the separators so the chosen cell takes area and its neighbours give it (250–300ms, linear or stepped; an instant cut under reduced motion); signals travel only along separators, in discrete steps and never blinking; nothing fades in, floats or overshoots. A signal cell that is a control also carries a label or a mark, never colour alone.
Fails when: the content is n equal items or a single column with no periphery; a signal sits inside the dominant, or the total passes ≈15% as chips or ≈28% with a corner block; separators take several weights; the dominant fills with saturated imagery that outshouts the counterweights.
Signature (do not carry together): black orthogonal bars · red, yellow and blue primaries · white ground.

**Theo van Doesburg.** *Set two orders against each other at one angle, the order the content obeys and the order the field obeys, on a shared module, so the surface never comes to rest.*
Process:
1. Fix the working order first: upright, on one module `m`. Everything that is read or operated lives in it and never rotates.
2. Lay a counter-order over the same format, turned against the first by ONE angle (45°, or 13–16° in the mild mode), built on the same module: a counter-order band is one cell's diagonal wide (√2 × `m`), so its edges land on the working order's lattice points.
3. Choose which order the frame sides with. (a) Frame with the content: the field is turned, and its planes run off at least two edges. (b) Frame with the counter-order: the container is turned, the content inside stays upright, and the frame cuts triangles off the corners.
4. Distribute weight one of two ways: one dominant plane of 45–60% with 4–8 others at falling sizes (in (a) central-high and cut by an edge; in (b) central, neutral and ringed), or 3–4 near-equal planes of 16–26% each. Never a centred, mirrored arrangement.
5. Make every division a plane: neighbouring field planes meet edge to edge with no separator; any bar is 2.7–20% of the width, wide enough to read as a plane. Thin lines exist only as a quiet lattice that shows the module.
6. Commit to one colour register and go all the way: none (value only, dark 26–40%); heavy (saturated planes ≥60%, quiet ground only 16–22%); or scraps (≈5% in all, only in the corners that 3(b) cuts off, the most saturated the smallest, touching only structure and frame). One off-key neutral plane keeps the set from looking pure.
Why it works:
- The eye takes the frame and gravity as its reference. An order turned against that reference reads as leaning or moving, so the still surface carries energy. If the content turns too, the tilt becomes the new upright: a rotated page, harder to read and no longer tense.
- Coincidence signals intent. When the counter-order's edges land on the working order's lattice points, the two read as one system built twice; with unrelated measures the field reads as a backdrop that happens to be behind the content.
- A plane cut by the frame is completed by the eye beyond it, so the field feels larger than the format and the content reads as a window laid on it. Planes that all fit inside read as objects on a page.
Colour relations: three registers, never mixed (value only · saturated ≥60% with ground 16–22% · scraps ≈5% at the cut corners); in the heavy register one plane may hold ≈48% and the others fall away in size; planes meet with no stroke; one greyed or off-key plane among the saturated ones.
Space and line: one angle per surface; band = √2 × `m`; bars 2.7–20% of the width; planes off ≥2 edges or corners cut by a turned frame; near-square field; content and controls upright always.
Type requirement: type belongs to the working order: a display line fitted to a solid squared block exactly as wide as its column (a family with a width range, or letters built from module strokes); two lockups may cross at 90° (one overprinted on another, short meta text turned up an edge), never set oblique; reading text sits on its own opaque upright panel, not across the seam between two field planes.
Motion and interaction: the two orders move at different rates along the counter-order's axis (field at about 0.4× the content, switched off under reduced motion); a state change snaps a field plane along that axis in 3–4 steps over 180–260ms; hit areas are the upright boxes (≥44px), never the turned shapes.
Fails when: the screen is long-form reading or a full-bleed table, so the field is either covered or competes with the text; the viewport is narrow enough that under a third of the field shows; mode (b) on a non-square or space-poor format (a turned container wastes about half its bounding box); anything that needs text or controls on the turned order.
Signature (do not carry together): a 45° tilt · one large red rhombus cut by the edge · black bars thick enough to be planes.

**Gerrit Rietveld.** *Keep every part whole and readable as itself: let parts pass each other at the joints instead of merging, and spend colour only on what ends or moves.*
Process:
1. Name the parts before composing, by kind: planes (carry content), linear members (carry structure), ends and handles (where a part stops or is gripped). Each kind gets one treatment, and a part never changes kind.
2. Set 3–8 neutral planes at different depths and unequal sizes (tracks near 1 : 1.6 : 0.6). No two share a flush edge or a corner: wherever two meet, one passes in front of the other.
3. Overshoot every joint: each member and slab runs past the thing it meets by 1–1.5× its own thickness. Keep members thin (1–2% of the format's height) against deep planes.
4. Rank colour by the inverse of size. At the scale of the whole format the planes stay neutral (light ≈55%, dark voids ≈8%) and saturation goes only on linear members and ends (1–3% in all, the most saturated the smallest). At the scale of one object, one plane may take the strong colour (up to ≈1/3), structure takes the darkest value (≈1/3) and the ends the brightest (≈1%).
5. Fixed is neutral, movable is coloured: surfaces that stay put take the neutrals; the things the user moves, holds or sits content on take the colour.
6. Give the plan at least two named arrangements of the same parts (open and divided). Each part travels on one axis only, and no arrangement repeats another's composition.
Why it works:
- Where one thing runs on past another, the eye reads two objects at two depths; where two things stop flush at a corner, it fuses them into one box. Overshoot gives depth by overlap with no shadow. Trim the overshoot and the layout collapses into bordered containers.
- A thin element can hold full saturation without flooding the view, and bright ends act as counters: the eye ticks off the parts. Put the same colour on the big planes and they merge into one painted volume, a mood and no longer a construction.
- Because every part persists and only translates, the user tracks it from one arrangement to the next (object constancy). Fading or replacing breaks the identity, and the new arrangement must be learned as a new screen.
Colour relations: neutrals in 3–5 values carry the planes; the darkest value is structure and voids; saturated share 1–3% at format scale, each hue one kind of part; an object-scale component may invert to ≈1/3 strong plane : ≈1/3 dark structure : ≈1% bright ends; saturation never tints a fixed surface.
Space and line: 3–8 planes at different depths, unequal; overshoot 1–1.5× thickness at every joint; members 1–2% of the height; no shared edges, no closed boxes; every slab projects beyond the plane beneath it.
Type requirement: type names parts: small, plain, tabular labels placed at the crossings, and one plain upright voice for content on the planes; a face with square terminals that holds at small sizes; a label may run along a member only if it is one or two words and is repeated upright nearby.
Motion and interaction: translation along a track, nothing else. Parts move one after another (280–360ms each, 60ms apart) so the division can be watched; under reduced motion they cut to the end state in the same order. A drawn handle may be a thin notch, but its hit area is ≥44px, and "movable" is shown by the handle's shape as well as by colour.
Fails when: the content is one continuous text (a single part, nothing to articulate); it is many equal items, so every joint's overshoot becomes noise; the width is under about 360px, where depth and overshoot eat the margins (cut to three parts); the two arrangements are really two unrelated screens.
Signature (do not carry together): black rails with yellow cut ends · a red back plane over a blue seat · white and grey slabs with thin primary poles.

**Bart van der Leck.** *Reduce a real subject to the fewest separate marks that still read as it, and let the untouched field between the marks do the joining.*
Process:
1. Start from a subject that already has structure (a scene, a process, a dataset, a word), never from an invented pattern. Lay it out whole, then delete: keep only the segments that give position, direction and kind.
2. Reserve the field: 64–84% of the surface stays untouched and remains ONE connected shape, with no enclosures. All marks together take 16–36%.
3. Cut the marks to one thickness family (main marks ≈3% of the width) and hold every mark clear of every other by 0.8–1.1× that thickness. Nothing touches, overlaps or encloses.
4. Zone by density, not by frames: bands with falling fill (about 45% → 36% → 20–25%), each band with its own mark shape (long uprights, short chips, a loose scatter, a row of dashes). One full-width mark may cap the densest band.
5. Colour by kind, ranked by area: the largest kind 6–16%, the darkest neutral 1–10%, a third kind 3–5%, the lightest and brightest kind smallest at 0.5–2.6%. Different kinds are always parted by field (they may abut only as segments of one mark).
6. Keep marks upright or level by default. At most one mark in five may lean (30–45°), every lean carries the same meaning (movement or direction), and a surface may have none.
7. Mode: show the subject at several degrees of reduction, from recognisable to bare, as stages of one view.
Why it works:
- The eye completes a figure from aligned fragments, and doing the completing holds attention. With gaps about one thickness wide, marks group by nearness and alignment yet stay separate. Much narrower gaps fuse them into outlined shapes; much wider gaps scatter them and the subject is lost.
- A field with no enclosures has no nesting, so grouping can only come from likeness (fill, shape, direction) and nearness. The viewer reads what kind of thing a mark is before how important it is, which suits content of many peers.
- Density itself segments the surface: a change in how tightly marks are packed is seen as a border. Frames would add a second system and break the field into pieces.
Colour relations: field 64–84%, one near-neutral value; 3–4 kinds, each one flat fill, ranked by area (largest 6–16% down to smallest 0.5–2.6%); the darkest neutral may fill small solid marks as well as strokes; no tints, no gradients; the field may invert to the darkest value in a side zone.
Space and line: one thickness family; gap 0.8–1.1× thickness; bands of falling density with a different mark shape per band; no borders, no nesting; leaning marks ≤1 in 5, or none; calm near-square formats.
Type requirement: display letters built from the same separate strokes as the marks: wide, light (stroke ≈1/12 of the height), every joint open, word spaces drawn as marks so each line fills one width, and a solid block may lead a line. Use them for one to three words at sizes where a stroke is ≥2px; all reading text is in a plain continuous face.
Motion and interaction: the view assembles and comes apart. Marks arrive one after another along their own axis (120–200ms each, 40–60ms apart; in place under reduced motion); a state change swaps a mark's kind, shown by shape or direction as well as fill; a thin mark that is a control owns the field around it up to a 44px hit area. The lightest kind never carries text or a state alone.
Fails when: the content is continuous prose or a dense table (it cannot be cut up, and the field drops under 60%); there is no real subject to reduce, so the marks become confetti; a form with many inputs, where each boundary must be found at once; labels longer than three words in the broken letters.
Signature (do not carry together): red, blue and yellow bars on white · letters broken at every joint · a street or factory scene cut into fragments.

**Vilmos Huszár.** *Build word, picture and surface from one unit, and lay colour over them as a separate sheet, so any arrangement of the pieces is a finished composition.*
Process:
1. Fix one unit `u` (≈3.5% of the block's width). Every stroke is 1`u`, every gutter 0.5`u`, and every mass is a whole number of units, from 1`u` up to ≈17`u`, including interlocking L-shapes. Letters and image use the same pieces.
2. Polarise density: pack the pieces into one cluster at about two-thirds filled, parted only by 0.5`u` gutters, and leave everything around it at zero. No middle density. The ground between pieces is part of the figure: letters stay open (gaps of 0.5–0.8`u`), and their insides are leftover ground.
3. Pair the charged with the still at one width: the irregular cluster sits on a calm, even, squared block of exactly the same width.
4. Lay colour as its own layer, placed after and independently of the structure: a colour plane may cross a break, turn a corner, or float clear of every edge and of every other plane. It never outlines or fills exactly one component.
5. Depict with planes: build any figure from 5–6 flat pieces with no outline, and leave one of them as bare ground.
6. Make variants and states by reassignment, not redesign: keep the arrangement and change only which colour is ground, frame (≈3% of the width) and accent; or keep the colours and move pieces by whole units into another complete pose.
Why it works:
- Likeness groups. When letters and picture are cut from the same unit they read as one object, and identity sits in the construction rule, not in a colour or a drawing, so it survives any change of palette. That is what lets a variant change everything but the layout.
- At two-thirds filled with thin gutters, figure and ground are near balance: the eye flips between dark masses and light channels, and the cluster stays active without moving. At middle densities the same pieces read as an even grey texture.
- When colour edges do not coincide with structural edges, the eye splits the surface into two layers. Colour is then free to mean something of its own (a variant, a category) across components. Colour that follows component edges is read as the component's decoration.
Colour relations: two registers. Structural: ground and one dark value only inside the cluster (≈66% dark), with colour as a few detached planes, one strong plane per view (5–9%) and the others clear of it. Flooded: one saturated ground of 68–78% in a thin frame, a figure of 5–6 flat planes, one piece left as ground. Each hue marks a variant or category and is also named in text. (UI extension, not observed: where two colour sheets must cross, give the crossing its own third flat tone and a label. The checked figure keeps its coloured windows apart.)
Space and line: one unit; strokes 1`u`, gutters 0.5`u`, masses 1–17`u`; no drawn dividers, the ground separates; cluster ≈2/3 filled over a still block of equal width; square corners follow from the unit.
Type requirement: the signature word is built from unit strokes and broken open, at display size only, with a plain-text equivalent; reading text is a sturdy face of even colour that makes a uniform block at the cluster's width. Square the block by fitting the measure; if justifying opens word gaps wider than the line space, set it flush left inside the same box.
Motion and interaction: pose to pose. Two or three complete arrangements, cut or stepped (no more than 3 changes a second, never blinking; a single cut under reduced motion); pieces and drags move by whole units; every intermediate frame must pass as a composition. Targets are ≥44px even when the unit is smaller.
Fails when: the layout is fluid and the unit cannot stay whole (it must snap, or the gutters go ragged); the content is photographs or long continuous text with nothing to cut into pieces; there are more than four or five variants, so whole-surface colour coding runs out; a colour plane crossing components lands under text and breaks its contrast.
Signature (do not carry together): block-built letters in black on white · a flat poster figure on a saturated yellow ground · a jointed mechanical figure with coloured windows.

### 4.3 CONSTRUCTIVISM & SUPREMATISM: forces, diagonals, two inks, apparatus
The movement: design as construction and as a working device. Diagonal energy, red as action, no decoration.
*The problem it answered:* How does a surface stop being a picture to look at and become a construction that works: forces instead of arrangement, a stated budget instead of taste, the viewer moved instead of pleased? Each artist organises it around a different thing: the field (Malevich), the vector and its route (Lissitzky), the source point (Rodchenko), two layers under load (Popova), the unit and its budget (Stepanova).

**Kazimir Malevich.** *Make the empty field the subject: float a few unequal planes in it, clear of the frame, and rank them so that the largest is the quietest and the loudest is the thinnest.*
Process:
1. Choose the mode by how much has to be said, because the field share inverts between them. One thing: icon mode (step 6). Several things: field mode (steps 2–5).
2. Field mode: keep one unbroken ground at 65–75% of the area and hold every plane at least 5% of the short side inside the frame. Nothing bleeds off, nothing is boxed.
3. Seat one large quiet plane (20–25% of the area) a single value step from the ground (about 8 L), with no edge line. It carries the cluster.
4. Build the cluster on one shared axis 7–40° off the frame, its centroid within 5% of the centre and its length along a diagonal. Separate the main masses by slivers of ground; only thin bars may cross a mass.
5. Rank by inverse intensity: the value farthest from the ground takes 3–15% in 2–3 planes of clearly falling size; the most saturated colour takes ≤1% as one long thin bar (8:1–20:1) that leaves the cluster axis and lies near the frame's horizontal; other hues stay small (≤2% each, one secondary up to 8%) on one or two counter-angles. Sizes step about 1 : 8 : 40.
6. Icon mode: one figure centred within 1% and spanning 68–72% of the side, so the figure outweighs the ground (about 52 : 48), with one edge 1–3° off true. For two figures: the one farther in value from the ground is about 2.3× larger; the saturated one is smaller, tilted 10–15°, diagonally opposite across a gap. Quietest variant: figure and ground at equal value, split by temperature alone, the figure tilted about 12° and slid into a corner until it meets one edge.
Why it works:
- Figure and ground. A plane with ground on every side reads as an object in front of a space that continues behind and beyond it. Cut by the frame, it becomes a piece of a pattern and the ground becomes what was left over; boxed, the ground becomes a container.
- A shared tilt removes gravity: nothing rests on the frame's baseline or on a neighbour. The one near-horizontal bar is then odd in angle and in saturation at once, so it wins attention at under 1% of the area. Tilt it with the rest, or enlarge it, and it is one more plane.
- Visual weight is intensity × area. Large-and-quiet against small-and-loud equalises the weights, so no plane becomes a target and the eye keeps crossing the gaps. Equal sizes hand the screen to the saturated one.
Colour relations: ground 65–75% at the lowest chroma; one big plane a single value step from it; maximum-contrast mass 3–15% in 2–3 falling planes; peak saturation ≤1%; up to about ten small hues if none passes 2%; all flat. Icon mode 52 : 48 figure to ground. Equal-value mode: zero value difference, temperature only (non-informative surfaces only; anything meaningful still needs 3:1).
Space and line: no outlines, an edge is a colour change and may sit 1–3° off true; margins ≥5%; cluster axis 7–40° with 1–2 counter-angles; scale 1 : 8 : 40; in the equal-value mode a faint line is the only edge.
Type requirement: type behaves as a plane: a face blunt and heavy enough that a word reads as a bar from a distance, in two sizes far apart. A display word may ride the cluster axis as a decorative duplicate; reading text, labels and every control stay horizontal on the ground.
Motion and interaction: weightless and slow. Planes drift in along their own axis, heaviest first, 600–1200ms, about 80ms apart, no overshoot; reduced motion: they appear in place. A state change alters the medium, never the height or the angle: hover or focus steps the plane's value once and a small bar docks on its long side; the action bar lengthens 10–15% along its axis. The thin bar keeps a 44px hit band and a label beside it.
Fails when: the content is a dense table, a long form or n equal items (no field survives and the tilt costs legibility); the viewport is a narrow phone column where a 5% margin plus 65% ground leaves no room; the equal-value mode is asked to carry a control or a state.
Signature (do not carry together): a black square on white · tilted bars in primary colours · white on white.

**El Lissitzky.** *Set one active form against one passive mass on a line of force and make everything else point along it, so that the eye, and then the hand, travels a route.*
Process:
1. Name the pair before drawing anything: the actor (what is done) and the target (what it is done to). One of each per view.
2. Give the frame one large flat field, 50–70% of the area. For a confrontation, split the frame instead on one steep diagonal (60–65°) into a light part and a dark part, about 2 : 1.
3. Place the target: large (about 45% of the width in the split mode), closed and static, orthogonal, in the zone of opposite value so that it reads at full contrast.
4. Place the actor: pointed or bar-like, at most 10% of the area, on an axis 30–50° off the frame (45° by default), aimed at the target and carrying the only saturated colour. Contact is a parameter with a meaning: stop short by less than the tip's own width for an act still pending; overlap as far as the target's centre for an act completed.
5. Keep one orthogonal counter-element (a bar, a square, or a numeral about 1/3 of the frame height) so that the diagonal has something to push against.
6. Put each word on the axis of the form it belongs to. Scatter 5–9 small fragments along the line of force and nowhere else. Join related elements with hairlines, used only as connectors.
7. Carry the line out of the view as a route: a permanent stepped index on one edge, each step a visible label plus a shape, and elements that cross a corner or point at the next surface. Nothing is centred on its own surface.
Why it works:
- A pointed form on an oblique axis is read as moving toward where it points, so the eye follows it to the target and the pair becomes an event. Two orthogonal forms are two objects and nothing happens; several actors cancel each other.
- Each form sits on the ground of opposite value, so both read at full contrast while saturation belongs to the actor alone: salience by oddness. Spread the saturated colour to a background or a second element and the actor disappears.
- The orthogonal counter-element supplies the frame of reference; with everything tilted the view reads as a rotated page. Forms that run past an edge or a corner continue in the mind, which is what turns a composition into a route.
Colour relations: two values and one agent: flat field 50–70%; mass at maximum contrast to it 11–28%; one saturated colour 3–10%, or none at all; a mid neutral for secondary blocks at a few percent; one translucent plane allowed. The saturated colour appears only on the actor, the word naming the act and a changed state, and is always paired with the pointed shape.
Space and line: main axis 30–50°; split diagonal 60–65°; on a route surface the dark elements total 15–20%; depth by parallel projection, hard offset slabs and one overlay, never by blur; thick rules (about 1% of the short side) as brackets; hairlines as connectors only; scale jumps of about 1 : 10 on one line.
Type requirement: heavy capitals that hold as beams when scaled tenfold, numerals heavy enough to act as masses, and a small-capital label legible at 12px for the index. A short display word (≤8 letters) may sit on an axis or stand vertical as a duplicate; reading text and control labels stay horizontal.
Motion and interaction: movement follows vectors and stops hard (220–320ms, no bounce, no fade). A route change cuts along the main axis. Hover pushes a block a few pixels along its own axis and focus adds a thick bracket. Idle motion exists only as a named kit class (`idle-turn`): at most one decorative element per view, one axis, ≥30s per revolution, no text on it; under reduced motion it rests in a fixed three-quarter pose and progress is carried by a text label. Pointed controls keep a 44px rectangular hit area.
Fails when: the view has no actor and target (reference pages, settings lists, n equal items); several actions are equally primary; the content is long reading text; the screen is too narrow for a 45° axis to leave a horizontal measure.
Signature (do not carry together): a red wedge driven into a white circle · red and black diagonal capitals on cream · a stepped thumb index with geometric glyphs.

**Alexander Rodchenko.** *Fix one source and make the whole surface project from it: the message swells toward the reader, the ground converges back on the source, and the darkest colour holds the loudest one.*
Process:
1. Fix the source: one point at about 1/3 of the width on the main reading axis, or, in the orthogonal mode, one bar on the leading edge (about 5% of the width). Everything after this is placed relative to it.
2. Frame with the sender: the name of whoever is speaking runs at full width along the top and bottom edges, or stands as the largest element at the foot (about 1/3 of the height), split into two colours at a hard line (between syllables, or horizontally at half the letter height).
3. Project the message from the source along the reading axis in one widening channel that opens 30–45° and spans about 40% of the width. Set the key word to fill it, the letters growing from 1/3 to the full channel height toward the open end. Orthogonal mode: the lines step away from the edge bar, each indented differently, with the key words picked out.
4. Cut the rest of the ground into flat fields whose edges all converge on the source. No field is left without a job.
5. Colour only in full flat fields, 3–6 of them. The most saturated may be the largest (11–29%), but wherever it carries the message it is enclosed by the darkest: saturated letters inside a dark channel, and a thin light outline between the channel and the coloured ground. The opposite-temperature fields (≤1/5 together) sit behind the source.
6. Label a zone with one word built from a modular cell grid (about 3 × 4 cells a letter) that crosses the zone boundary unchanged.
Why it works:
- Edges that converge act as pointers: the eye is pulled to the vertex and then released along the widening channel, and letters that grow read as approach and as loudness. With equal letters and a centred source the same word is a caption.
- Containment. When the loudest colour is also the largest field, the message would dissolve into it; enclosing the saturated letters in the darkest value gives them the strongest edge on the sheet and keeps figure and ground apart.
- The sender's name at both edges closes the sheet, so the internal diagonals cannot lead the eye out of it, and it says who is speaking in any crop.
Colour relations: flat, undiluted, no tints or ramps; most saturated 11–29% and allowed to be the largest; darkest 13–20% as container, bar and text; light ground from about 24% (convergent mode) to about 75% (orthogonal mode); opposite-temperature fields about 17% together; the saturated colour meets its opposite only along converging edges; a continuous-tone image is the only neutral (≤7%), cut to a simple closed shape.
Space and line: source at 1/3; channel 30–45° over about 40% of the width; edge bar about 5%; every edge hard; rules are bars; the channel's thin outline is the only fine line; the orthogonal mode has no diagonal at all.
Type requirement: capitals that can be fitted to a container letter by letter (a wide width range, or outlines scaled individually), a squared monoline display face that survives being cut in two colours, and a bold plain face for staggered text. The smallest letter of a growing word stays ≥12px and the word remains real text.
Motion and interaction: mechanical: pivot, slide on a track, unfold on a hinge, 180–260ms, no overshoot. A transition is a single wipe opening from the source along the axis. A panel expands in place on a hinge to 2–3× its size instead of opening an overlay; hover widens the channel by a few degrees and thickens its outline (width, not lift). Reduced motion: states swap without travel. (Derived from the process and from written accounts of the folding furniture, which was not seen.)
Fails when: two or more messages have equal rank (two sources cancel the convergence); the content is long reading or a data table (converging fields leave no rectangle); nobody is speaking (a neutral tool has no sender); the key word is longer than about 8–10 letters or changes length in translation.
Signature (do not carry together): a megaphone wedge of type from a shouting photographed face · a masthead cut red over black · a flat poster of red, black, green and blue rays.

**Lyubov Popova.** *Lay two systems over each other, soft fields beneath and hard vectors above, and tie the vectors to the frame so that the surface reads as a structure holding a load.*
Process:
1. Choose the ground as a working colour and decide how much of it shows: none (fields fill the frame), about half (≈45%), or most (40–60%, the repeat mode). It is never neutral leftover.
2. Lay 3–5 overlapping fields slanted 25–40° off the frame, answered by one opposing slant at a different angle, never a mirror. Fields take the soft treatment: each lightens toward one edge only, or ends in a dry, broken edge.
3. Cross the fields with vectors that follow a different geometry: straight bars in 4–5 fixed widths (0.4–2.5% of the short side) at 3–4 angles, plus 2–3 arcs or rings, all meeting in a knot displaced a little from the centre. Vectors are always hard-edged.
4. Anchor to the frame: every vector (or, with no ground showing, every field) runs off an edge, and the bars leave by all four sides. Round elements overlap the structure instead of sitting inside one of its bays.
5. Assign colour by job. The darkest is structure (13–43%). The strongest hue is carried as line, not as field (about 12%), and touches the structure colour at every crossing. The lightest fields lie against the ground for the largest value step. One small note of the opposite temperature (≤5%) is the only relief.
6. For anything repeated, take one motif and change it once per instance (turn it 45°, split it, reverse its stripe direction), and hold the budget to one hue, the structure colour and the ground, in that rising order of area.
Why it works:
- Edge quality sorts depth. A hard edge reads as a thing in front and a soft edge as a surface behind, so two layers separate with no shadow. Make both hard and they fuse into one mosaic; make both soft and nothing bears a load.
- A line that reaches the frame reads as fixed there and under tension, like a strut, and as continuing beyond. The same knot floating inside a margin is a motif on a background. Displacing the knot makes the spans unequal, which is what reads as force.
- Fixed widths let lines be grouped by likeness and ranked; continuously varying widths are noise. Keeping the strongest hue thin makes it read as energy, not as mass, so it can cross everything without becoming the ground.
Colour relations: one temperature dominates with a single small opposite note. Vector mode: ground ≈45%, structure ≈13%, strongest hue ≈12% as line, light fields ≈12%, dark fields ≈14%. Field mode: no ground, 6–7 colours, the darkest up to 43%, every field ramped toward light on one edge. Repeat mode: ground 40–60 : structure 20–30 : hue 10–30, the hue rarely touching the ground without the structure colour beside it. Cover mode: two inks and the ground at about 24 : 20 : 43.
Space and line: slants 25–40°; ramps of 40–52° in a scaffold; bars in 4–5 widths; knot off-centre; a near-square format in vector mode; nearly every bay cross-braced where a frame is shown; in repeats the stripe equals the gap; hard edge means vector, soft edge means field.
Type requirement: condensed capitals drawn to stretch in both width and height until they fill a ring, a band or a circle, and sturdy enough to be reversed out of a solid band, with a plain grotesque for working text. Reading text sits horizontally on flat platforms between the vectors.
Motion and interaction: mechanical. Fields slide in along their own slant, back to front, 60ms apart; vectors draw inward from the frame (180–260ms, no overshoot). Hover or focus steps the vector that crosses an item to the next fixed width (line weight, not elevation). A control may show its mechanism (a quarter turn) if position and label change with it. Constant rotation exists only as a named idle class: decorative, ≥30s per turn, still under reduced motion, and progress is carried by filled segments and a number.
Fails when: the content is long text or a table and no flat platform is provided; there are two or fewer elements (no knot, so no load); the screen is small enough that the thinnest bar drops under 1px; an equal stripe-and-gap repeat is placed under text (it vibrates; decorative only).
Signature (do not carry together): slanted overlapping planes in brick and blue-black · bright rings crossed by black bars on bare board · a cross-braced scaffold with wheels.

**Varvara Stepanova.** *Set a tiny budget first (one unit, one way of building, two inks and the ground) and get every variety from repetition, offset, inversion and use.*
Process:
1. Fix the budget before any layout: one unit cell, at most 3 primitives drawn from a vocabulary of four (circle, square, triangle, bar), two inks and the ground. Nothing later may add to it.
2. Fix one construction rule and build every component from it, for example parallel members with a gap at least as wide as the member, so that every object is about half void. Components then read as one family at any size.
3. Fill by rule, not by composition: repeat the unit with a half-drop offset and alternate the two inks column by column. Inside a patterned area the inks cover about 75% in near-equal shares (≈38 : 36) and the ground shrinks to thin separating lines (≈25%).
4. Outside the pattern, reverse the proportion: components stand as separate pieces in one row on bare ground, heights varied, the tallest near the middle.
5. Differentiate by assignment, not by decoration: each actor, team or series gets a whole kit (an emblem, a stripe and an ink) on the same cut.
6. For a single flat sheet, mass instead of repeating: one block of the ink farthest from the ground at about 55%, the second ink at about 10%, the title filling the top third at full width, type knocked out of the block.
Why it works:
- With the variables fixed in advance, any crop is recognisable (grouping by likeness) and variety can only come from phase and position, which is what lets one rule produce a hundred results. A third ink ends the alternation: two colours are a pair, three are a palette.
- The half-drop breaks rows and columns, so the eye reads diagonals and one continuous texture, not tiles. Thinning the ground reverses figure and ground: the ground becomes the drawn line. Widen it and the units turn into spots on a background.
- When everything is built by one rule, separate pieces read as a set without touching, and the one piece in another state stands out by oddness. Irregularity is legible only against that regularity.
Colour relations: ground plus two inks, no tints. In pattern the two inks are near-equal in area and close in value, differing in temperature, and both darker than the ground. In sheet mode about 55 : 31 : 10 (far ink : ground : second ink). In built mode there is no hue at all, only a light lattice against a dark ground. An ink belongs to an actor or a state and is never the only thing that tells two apart.
Space and line: one unit cell; half-drop; pattern arms steep (60–65°); member equals gap; overlaps keep hard edges; ruler and compass only, with no corner radius short of a full circle; seams and joints stay visible. The share of patterned to bare area is a free parameter (about 30 : 70 is a hint only; it was never verified).
Type requirement: heavy monoline capitals that can be drawn from the same primitives as the unit, fill a third of a sheet at full width and survive being knocked out of a block, with a tabular companion for data. Labels stay horizontal.
Motion and interaction: a mechanism doing a job: hinge, fold, drop; linear or stepped, under 200ms, one pass. A pattern advances one unit per scroll step. Switching context recolours the whole kit in one cut. Hover or focus inverts the unit (ground and ink exchange) and adds a marker bar, so the state is never value alone. One non-control item per view (an empty state, an illustration) may be shown in its other state, collapsed or folded; an action the user needs never misbehaves. Reduced motion: states swap with no travel.
Fails when: one view must separate more than two categories by hue; the content is photographs or rich media (they bring a third and fourth ink); text or controls have to sit on the pattern (equal stripe and gap vibrates, so pattern is decorative and content sits on bare ground); the brief needs one composed hero image.
Signature (do not carry together): a two-dye chevron repeat on unbleached cloth · slatted wooden stage apparatus · boxy striped team sportswear.

### 4.4 SWISS / INTERNATIONAL TYPOGRAPHIC STYLE: the grid, objectivity, type as structure
The movement: modular grids, flush-left grotesques and meaning through position and interval. The section ends with its two rebels.
*The problem it answered:* How do you make information read as fact, not as persuasion, when there is no ornament to lean on? Fix a measure first (field, module, family or programme), then let position, interval and one extreme contrast carry all the meaning; the two rebels keep the measure in view and overdrive it.

**Josef Müller-Brockmann.** *Measure the field from the text line, hang everything from one off-centre axis, and let a single figure generated by a ratio carry all the scale while the words stay small and exact.*
Process:
1. Derive the field from the reading line: the gutter is one line of body leading, the format divides into 8–32 fields (2×4 up to 4×8; gutter ≈1/8 of a column), and every edge of every element lies on a field line.
2. Set ONE vertical axis at 27–29% of the width. All text hangs from it and nothing is centred; in label and value pairs the labels end at the axis and the values start from it.
3. Choose one carrier of scale, a word or a figure, never both. If the word carries it, the small text is 1/8–1/13 of its height. If the figure carries it, the title stays at ≤2.5× the body and the figure takes half the format or more. No middle sizes.
4. Generate the figure from one rule: one shape, one parameter stepped by a fixed ratio (each step 2× the last, 4–5 steps) or one fixed transform (a 45° turn, one overlap). It bleeds off two edges, covers 45–55% of the area, and the text of step 2 aligns to its geometry.
5. Colour last: one ground and one or two inks for the whole piece; in a series each item takes its own pair while steps 1–4 stay fixed. A third colour exists only where two inks overlap. The most intense hue is spent on the smallest text, never on a field.
6. Test by ratio: measure any two neighbouring intervals. Each is equal, a whole number of fields apart, or one step of the rule in step 4. An interval that is none of these was placed by eye; snap it.
Why it works:
- Grouping by common edge: text on one axis reads as one object however far apart the blocks are, so the remaining 70% of the width can stay empty or hold the figure. Centre the axis and the page becomes symmetric, and the figure reads as decoration around it.
- A progression at a constant ratio is read as a rule, not as a set of shapes: the eye predicts the next step, which gives direction and crescendo without depiction. Equalise the steps and it becomes a pattern; choose them by eye and it becomes an illustration.
- Scale contrast with the middle removed gives two distances of reading (across the room, in the hand) and no ambiguity about rank. A mid-size third level makes the big thing merely "large".
Colour relations: ground 45–85% in one flat value; figure or second ground 15–53% at the opposite end of the value scale or as a second hue of equal weight; overlap tone where two inks cross; signal ≤5% as small text or one thin strip; a recessive mid-value copy of an element may sit behind it as an earlier layer.
Space and line: fields from the leading; one axis at 27–29%; half the format or more empty or given to the figure; no drawn rules needed (edges are alignments); hint from one work: one block may sit half a field off, never two.
Type requirement: one family, at most two weights, lowercase and flush from the axis, ragged; a face that holds at both ends of a 1:13 jump (tight at display size, open at 12–14px); figures that align in columns.
Motion and interaction: movement runs along field lines only. Entrances use ONE fixed stagger of 80ms (inside the 70–120ms calibration range), at most four items, each move ≤320ms, no overshoot; the ratio of step 4 belongs to space (thickness, interval), not to delay. Mode from one work: on a view change the outgoing headline stays 300ms as the recessive layer behind the new one, then clears. With reduced motion, cut.
Fails when: the content has no small-text tier (a single call to action) or no room for a half-format figure (dense tables, small widgets); the figure is drawn instead of generated; text needs three or more ranks of size.
Signature (do not carry together): concentric arc bands · lowercase grotesque flush left · grey, black and one small red line of text.

**Armin Hofmann.** *Oppose one hard, still sign and one soft, moving image at extreme scale, let them touch at exactly one point, and give changing information a fixed slot so the opposition never has to move.*
Process:
1. Reduce the surface to two values, a dark and a ground, and decide which dominates (dark 31–70% of the area). Every grey that follows must come from inside the image, never from a tint.
2. Cast two bodies of opposite character: one hard (a letter, numeral, bar or dot: flat, razor-edged, still) and one soft (a continuous-tone image: blurred, grainy or graded). Give them unequal zones, about 1/3 to 2/3, neither centred, each cut by at least two edges of the format.
3. Enlarge one body until it is architecture: 50–70% of the height or more, with all running text at 1/30–1/40 of the height. Nothing sits in between.
4. Make ONE contact: move a single part of the hard body out of its zone toward the soft one, or cut the hard form into the soft mass, at the place where the two come closest (about mid-height). Exactly one, and nothing else leaves its zone.
5. If information changes over time, fence a slot for it before placing anything else: about 1/3 of the width by 40% of the height in an upper corner, closed on two sides by rules and on two by the format edges, or cut as a window into the hard body with the text reversed. The slot's content swaps; its position never does.
6. Hue is all or nothing: none; or under 1% as a few small characters; or one flat plane at full scale (≈45% against ≈50% dark). Never a medium-sized accent.
Why it works:
- Contrast of kind beats contrast of amount: a sharp flat edge beside a blurred graded mass makes each more itself (edge contrast, figure and ground), so two values are enough. Make both hard and it is a diagram; both soft and nothing holds the format.
- One escaped part reads as intention because everything else obeys its zone (salience by oddness); it joins the two bodies into one event. Two escapes read as loose alignment.
- A slot whose place is constant is found by position, not by search, and the composition does not reflow when the data changes. A floating data block makes the big sign negotiate with it every week.
Colour relations: dark and ground only, in a 30:70 to 70:30 split; greys 0–25%, all inside the image and with highlights that are the bare ground; the brightest marks are the type and the escaped part; hue as step 6.
Space and line: two zones near 1:2; the large body reaches 60–100% of the height and 2/3 of the width; text 1/30–1/40 of the height; two rules at most, both belonging to the slot; edges of the format do the closing.
Type requirement: one bold, regular-width face that stays clear when set small and reversed, and whose letters are simple enough to work as shapes at 60% of the height; one line may run rotated along an edge, full height; figures that can be redrawn as plain strokes.
Motion and interaction: the hard frame is still and only the soft layer moves (drift or blur ≤600ms). The slot changes by hard cut. Selection is the escaped part re-used: a solid mark that leaves the text line, plus a weight change; the mark is 8–12px but the target is the whole row, at least 44px high. Progress is a row of bars stepping from thin to thick, moved one step at a time.
Fails when: there is no image and no single sign worth enlarging (settings, forms, long tables); several items need equal weight; the small text must sit over the soft image (contrast cannot be guaranteed, so keep text on the flat dark or the ground); colour coding is a requirement.
Signature (do not carry together): a giant black letter cropped by the sheet · a grainy black-and-white photograph · a detached dot.

**Emil Ruder.** *Compose the unprinted area first, as the largest shape on the page; make every dark thing a grey texture of one family; and produce variety by stepping one variable at a time.*
Process:
1. Fix two constants: one square module (the page as a field of about 6 × 6) and one type family with several widths, weights and a slant. Nothing else may enter.
2. Budget the empty area before any content: 50–90% of the surface, gathered into one or two large open fields (the top third, or the middle half between a head block and a foot block), not spread as even padding. Inverse mode: the dark takes 55% and the light is packed into it.
3. Set the content as compact blocks on narrow measures, anchored to the head or the foot. Parallel content runs in columns of equal width and honest, unequal length under a header that repeats the same split. Each block occupies a different multiple of the module.
4. Vary ONE property per step: within a series, or down a stack of lines, change only size, or only weight, or only width, or only orientation, in regular steps, and let the sequence draw the overall shape.
5. Tune by grey value: squint, and treat each block as a tone. Small, light, tight setting is a lighter grey; a narrow light channel inside a dense dark mass is the brightest thing on the page. Adjust intervals (tracking, leading, gaps) until there are three or four distinct tones and one of them is full dark.
6. For density, repeat instead of adding: set one phrase several times, offset or overlaid until it knots into a texture band, then once clean beside it. Never fill the open field of step 2 to do this.
7. Hue, if any: one small mass (≤8% of the area) against the dark, or the single ink that carries all the type. Never beside a second hue.
Why it works:
- Figure and ground are reversible: an empty field with a deliberate shape and edge reads as a thing, so the dark blocks need not fill the page to hold it. Spread the same emptiness evenly and it becomes margin, and the blocks float.
- Fine detail fuses into tone: a block of small text is a grey, so hierarchy can be built from grey values inside one family. A second family adds a difference of kind, and the tonal scale is no longer readable.
- A sequence with a single changing variable is read as a rule and as rhythm; the eye compares neighbours and sees motion. Change two variables at once and each step looks like a new, unrelated object.
Colour relations: ground 50–90% (or 38% in the inverse mode); full dark ≤25% as the densest blocks; two intermediate tones made by setting, not by tint; optional hue as step 7; a tonal mode with two inks close in value and no extreme also occurs.
Space and line: near-square proportions; module 1/6 of the side; open field ≥1/3 of the height in one piece; equal columns of unequal length; hairline rules span the full width or a column, never a box; display lines may stagger left and right line by line.
Type requirement: one family with a real matrix: at least three widths, four weights and an oblique, even in colour at small sizes, with lowercase that forms clean word shapes; ragged, flush left.
Motion and interaction: motion changes intervals and axes, never position: a row opens its leading or tracking, or moves one step in width or weight (≤300ms, then still). Selection is never value alone: the selected row steps to full dark AND gains one weight step AND a hairline rule at its head (or an indent of one module); the unselected state still meets 4.5:1.
Fails when: the content must fill the surface (data grids, maps, feeds) so no open field of 1/3 survives; items need icons or colour coding to be told apart; text is too short to form a grey (single-word labels throughout); the available family has one width and two weights.
Signature (do not carry together): one sans family in lowercase · a vermilion square or circle on black and white · a near-square page.
Provenance: no museum scan was obtained. Checked from publishers' reproductions (photographs of book pages and spreads) and two secondary web reproductions; posters were small in those images, so shares are approximate and fine type detail was not judged.

**Karl Gerstner.** *Do not compose: list the parameters of the problem, give each a short ordered row of values, and treat every design as one selection, so the set of selections is the work.*
Process:
1. Write the problem as a table before drawing anything: 10–15 parameters in 3–4 groups (what it is made of, its colour, its appearance, its expression), each with 3–5 numbered values and one "combined" value. A parameter with one value is a constant; move it out of the table.
2. Order every row as equal steps between two poles, so a value is an index, not a name. Size, proportion, weight, slant, direction and colour all step this way (seen: width × weight as a 4 × 4 matrix; scale ≈1, 0.7, 0.45, 0.3; turns of 90°).
3. Fix ONE unit (a word, an image, a component) and allow only the transforms in the table. A variant is one value per row; a value outside the table means the table must be changed for every variant, not this one adjusted.
4. Give the field the same treatment: one module, every width, height and void a whole number of modules, and only the divisions that the module arithmetic admits (see Space and line).
5. Build colour as a ramp by the rule of step 2: between two poles, hue and lightness move together in equal steps (≈3–5 L per step, chroma constant) on a neutral ground that keeps the majority (≈64%). Neighbouring steps touch, each nested or inset in the last, so the edges themselves show the increment.
6. Show the programme at least once: several selections of the same unit side by side (4–16 of them), so the viewer can read the rule from the differences.
Why it works:
- Variation of one thing under visible constraints is read by comparison: with the unit constant, every difference is attributable to one parameter, so the system explains itself. Hand-tune one variant and the differences stop being readable.
- Equal steps make a scale perceptually even, so position in the row can carry meaning (first to last, low to high) without a legend of names. Unequal steps turn a scale into a set of unrelated swatches or sizes.
- Deciding in the table moves judgement to where it is cheap and shared: a wrong value is fixed once for all cases. Deciding per layout hides the rule and multiplies exceptions.
Colour relations: neutral ground ≥60%; one ramp of 5–9 steps between two poles; adjacent steps differ by 3–5 L, which separates regions but cannot carry text, so text is set only on a step far enough along the ramp to reach 4.5:1, and step meaning is always doubled by position or a printed index; a second, complementary pole is unverified.
Space and line: one module. Correct arithmetic for a field that holds several column counts: pick a period `P` divisible by every count wanted and a gutter `g` in the same units; the field is `P − g` units; a `k`-column division has columns `P/k − g` wide starting every `P/k` units. With `P` = 60 and `g` = 2 the field is 58 and the columns are 28, 18, 13, 10 and 8 for 2–6. In CSS this is 58 gapless tracks with each gutter as two empty tracks (`column-gap: 0`); `repeat(58,1fr)` with a gap, or without reserved gutter tracks, does not hold the divisions. The attribution of this field to the "Capital" grid is unverified (no image obtained); treat it as a hint.
Type requirement: one family whose widths, weights and slants form a full matrix, so each axis can be a row of the table; sizes on a scale tied to the module; ranged left.
Motion and interaction: motion is permutation: a change of selection snaps to the next value in its row (150–200ms, or stepped), never through values that are not in the table. One visible control lets the user turn a parameter; that control has a 44px target and a text label of the current value.
Fails when: there will only ever be one or two instances (the table costs more than it returns); the content is a single authored narrative that needs one exception; a parameter cannot be ordered (brand names, legal categories), so index carries no meaning.
Signature (do not carry together): nested bands stepping through a cool colour ramp · one photograph repeated at descending scales and turns · a ruled parameter table set in grotesque.

**Wim Crouwel.** *Declare one square cell and build everything from whole cells, the big word included; freeze the constants of the series, so the only free choices left are one whole-region colour and what the cells spell.*
Process:
1. Fix one square module for both axes: period ≈1/28 of the short side, of which ≈80% is cell and ≈20% is gutter. Draw it, quietly, as a continuous layer over every ground, light or dark; it never moves or stops at a region edge.
2. Snap every position and size to whole cells and hang content from the top-left origin. Empty cells stay empty; nothing is centred or stretched to balance.
3. Divide the format once with a hard edge on a cell line into two regions of opposite value, between 50:50 and 90:10. No other large division.
4. Construct the single display element from the module itself: stroke exactly 1 cell, height a small whole number (5 cells), corners rounded by ≈1/4 of the stroke, diagonals as one-cell steps. It may be cropped by the format edge and may sit near the limit of legibility, because step 5 holds.
5. Set all other text in one neutral face at ONE size; rank comes from position and weight only. Every word in the constructed display is repeated or labelled in this plain text.
6. Give the piece one saturated hue and spend it on a whole region of step 3 or on the whole display element, never as scattered small marks. One two-stop ramp across that single element is allowed.
7. Freeze the series: choose 3–4 constants that never change between items (a cropped mark and its position, the text size, the format, the origin) and one variable (the region hue).
Why it works:
- A visible measure makes every size readable as a count: the eye compares things in cells, so the empty cells are as exact as the full ones and a 5-cell element looks deliberate. Hide the grid and the same layout reads as sparse.
- When the unit of the letter equals the unit of the layout, type and structure are the same object (grouping by likeness), which is why one constructed word can hold a format with nothing else in it. A drawn display face on the same grid sits on it instead of in it.
- Colour over a whole region is read as the state of the piece, not as a signal inside it, so a series can be told apart at a glance while everything else is identical. Sprinkle the same hue and it becomes a set of alerts.
Colour relations: three neutrals (ground, a dark that is not the darkest possible, a grid line 1–2 steps from its ground) plus one saturated hue at 45–90% as a region, or ≤10% as the whole display element; the hue changes per item in a series; text on the hue must still reach 4.5:1, so choose its value for the text, not the reverse.
Space and line: module ≈1/28 of the short side; gutter 20% of the module, drawn as a pair of hairlines; one hard division on a cell line; display 5 cells high with a 1-cell stroke; a mark cropped by two edges as the series constant.
Type requirement: a neutral face with enough weights to rank at one size, and figures that stay clear in a coarse matrix; the display is not a font choice but a construction rule (cells, stroke, step), applied to at most one or two words per view.
Motion and interaction: the grid is fixed and content changes in place, in whole-cell steps. The display builds stroke by stroke once on entry (≤320ms in total; with reduced motion it appears complete); nothing blinks or repeats. Hover and selection fill whole cells with the dark value and invert the text, with a weight step as the non-colour partner; a target is at least 44px, so two or more cells.
Fails when: the content is long reading text or free-form imagery that cannot be cropped to cells; the display has to carry a critical instruction (it is slow to read); the screen is too small for 5-cell elements (below about 20 cells across); items in a series need different structures.
Signature (do not carry together): visible graph-paper grid · stroke-built lowercase letters with stepped diagonals · a full-bleed red or fluorescent field.

**Wolfgang Weingart.** *Keep the inherited structure in plain sight and overdrive one of its variables at a time (spacing, step, frame weight, layer) until text starts to read as image, while a plainly set copy stays within reach.*
Process:
1. Lay down the orthodox structure first and leave it visible: a frame, a division into quarters or columns in hairlines, one family in two weights. Every later step is a measured departure from this, so it must be legible as the norm.
2. Per block, choose ONE variable and push it past tolerance while the block beside it takes the opposite extreme: spacing opened until each letter stands alone (about 2 cap heights per letter) against spacing crushed until letters touch; or a fixed indent added per line (2–4 characters) so the block's edge becomes a diagonal. Only short or secondary text is treated; the information is also set once, normally.
3. Add solid masses as structure: 12–30% of the surface in the darkest value, as bars and blocks that carry reversed labels and run past their text unevenly toward one edge.
4. Weight the enclosure unequally: heavy on one side (≈14% of the width), thin on the opposite (≈3%), and break one corner with a mechanical edge (steps, a 45° cut, a sawtooth). The dense material sits inside, pushed off-centre.
5. Make tone by screening, not by tinting: greys are coarse dots or fields of parallel hairlines (each grey 15–25% of the area), and 3–5 planes overlap. Labels never sit on a screened field; they sit on the solid masses of step 3 or on bare ground.
6. Colour mode last. Default: none (darkest value, screened greys, ground). Otherwise exactly two inks plus the ground, in one of two ways: flat (one ink is the field, ≈60%, the other is line and hatching inside large letters) or screened (both inks as dot gradients that overprint into a third colour).
Why it works:
- A deviation is only visible against a norm (salience by oddness): with the frame and the normal block present, the crushed or opened block reads as a decision about spacing. Remove the norm and the same block is merely badly set.
- Proximity makes letters into words. Open the spacing far enough and each letter groups with the field instead, so the word turns into a row of marks and slows the eye: the text becomes image without any image being added.
- Fine regular marks fuse into tone, and overlapping screens fuse into a third value, so depth and grey come from the reproduction process itself. An unequal frame gives the dense core a direction, where an even border would make it a picture in a mount.
Colour relations: darkest value 12–30% solid; one or two screened greys at 15–25% each; ground the rest and always present as margin and reversed type. Ten of the twelve checked works are without hue. Each colour mode was seen in one work only: two flat inks (field ≈60% and a lighter line ink), and two screened inks overprinting (ground ≈26%, the mixed tones the majority). No third ink, and none of the checked works uses a red-violet process ink.
Space and line: visible hairline frame and divisions; frame 14% against 3%; one broken corner; step of 2–4 characters per line; bars overshoot on one side only; rules are type-high or hairline, nothing between.
Type requirement: one family, two weights, with letters sturdy enough to survive touching and to stand alone when spaced, and to hold when reversed out of a solid at small sizes.
Motion and interaction: reveals arrive in discrete steps, one line per beat (60–80ms a step, ≤320ms in all), once. Press shifts the layer 2px, like a plate out of register. Hover may open a word's spacing, but the accessible name and a normally set copy stay unchanged; layers that slip on scroll stop under reduced motion; nothing flashes.
Fails when: the text is long or must be scanned fast (prices, errors, forms); the structure being broken is not shown, so the break reads as a bug; every block is overdriven (no norm left); labels have to sit on screened or layered fields.
Signature (do not carry together): stair-stepped text blocks and bars · labels reversed out of heavy black bars · coarse halftone collage.

### 4.5 MODERNIST ARCHITECTURE: the body moving through space
The movement: proportion from the body, free planes, sequence, light and material.
*The problem it answered:* Once structure no longer dictates enclosure, what stays fixed (a measure, a module, a frame, a core, a wall) and what is set free (planes, path, light, one curve, one colour)? Each architect fixes a different thing and rations a different thing, so that a body moving through the space always knows where it is and what matters next.

**Le Corbusier.** *Measure everything on two interlocked scales taken from one body dimension, free the partitions from the structure, and string the views on one continuous path; colour then sets each plane's depth.*
Process:
1. Take one dimension from the user's body or reach (a target size, a line of text) and build two geometric series from it: ratio ≈1.62 per step, the second series exactly double the first, so each term of one falls between two terms of the other. The first sets internal spacing and type, the second structural gaps. No value outside the two series exists.
2. Lay a regular support grid (3–5 bays) and then place partitions without regard to its lines. The grid carries, the partitions only divide, so any partition may be moved or removed without touching the rest.
3. Detach the main mass from what carries it: a recessed band below it in the opposite value (main mass about 2× the band's height), or a thin line of light under the heaviest element (<1% of the height). Never a cast shadow.
4. Open each plane by 15–30% of its area, in one of two ways and never as an equal grid: one continuous strip (≈1:16, 85–90% of the plane's width) in a thin plane, or 5–13 deep openings of unequal size scattered in a thick plane of the opposite value.
5. Colour by depth, as a chord: one ground (≈50%) plus 2–3 small flat fields, or 2–3 value steps of one hue family. One flat colour per plane; cool and neutral planes go behind, the warmest and most saturated is nearest and smallest (from <1% up to ≈13%).
6. Order the views on one unbroken path that doubles back, so each turn reframes what was just seen from the other side. Stages are reached by travelling, never by a cut.
Why it works:
- Scale reference: when every size is a term of two series built on a body dimension, any two neighbours are in a ratio the eye has already met, so the whole reads as measured. A linear scale gives near-equal steps at the large end and arbitrary ratios between distant sizes.
- Depth from temperature, value and overlap: with structure and partition separated, a plane's position is free, and colour is the cheapest way to state it. Colour every plane equally and nothing advances; the plan goes flat.
- Continuity of path: a view reached by moving is located relative to the last one. Cut between views and the user holds a list of screens, not a building.
Colour relations: ground ≈50%, lowest chroma; a chord is the ground plus 2–3 small fields or 2–3 steps of one family (pale, mid, deep); saturated colour is rare by area (<1% to ≈13%); one flat colour per plane, no gradient across a plane; inside a thick opposite-value plane, colour shrinks to chips ≤1% set in the openings.
Space and line: two series, ratio ≈1.62, offset by 2×; support grid of 3–5 bays; main mass ≈2× its recessed base; strip ≈1:16 over 85–90% of the width, or 5–13 unequal openings; openings 15–30% of their plane; at most one curved partition, and only against an orthogonal frame (one work).
Type requirement: sizes are consecutive terms of the first series, so type and spacing are one scale (a size step is always ≈1.62×; intermediate sizes do not exist); one plain face that survives large jumps in size; stage labels numbered along the path.
Motion and interaction: movement follows the path (translate along a shallow incline, 500–700ms, near-linear) and is replaced by an instant change under reduced-motion; hover and focus add light to an opening and a visible outline, never size; light itself may drift over 10–20s, never in response to input.
Fails when: users must jump between views at random and at speed (the path becomes delay); the content needs fine, even gradations of size (dense tables, code: 1.62× steps are too coarse); the content is n equal items, which no opening rule or chord can rank; small openings are used as targets under 44px.
Signature (do not carry together): a white box on thin columns · one long strip window · primary-coloured flat panels set in raw grey concrete.

**Ludwig Mies van der Rohe.** *Make the system so regular that it disappears, so attention falls on the space flowing between a few free planes and on the one surface where all the richness is spent.*
Process:
1. Fix one module and repeat it across the whole format without exception (equal intervals, unbroken from edge to edge). Every gap is a whole multiple of it; the single permitted fraction is a quarter module, used only where an edge overhangs its last support.
2. Give up ground before anything is shown: in the first view the object takes about half the width (≈54%) and the rest is empty, plain surface with one name and one way in. The emptiness is spent, not left over.
3. Lay the two largest fields as a pair of plain, parallel bands at one end of the value scale (≈30% of the view each, the only texture a fine grid of joints on the module). Everything else stands between them as an island.
4. Stand 2–4 planes free between those bands: different lengths, each stopping short of the frame at both ends, sliding past one another so the way between them narrows and widens. No four planes ever close a box. Put the one dense, opaque block off-centre (about 45/55).
5. Spend the richness once: one plane (12–25% of the area, up to ≈45% of the width) carries the highest chroma and the only strong texture, mirrored across its seams. Seat it on a plain field at the opposite value extreme from the bands, about half its area (≈12%).
6. Let variation come only from the content behind the grid: cells may differ in what they hold (≈10% of cells at most stand out in value), but no line, interval or edge of the grid itself ever changes.
Why it works:
- Grouping by likeness: a perfectly uniform repeat fuses into a single surface and stops being read line by line, so the eye is released to the exceptions. Vary the module anywhere and the structure becomes the subject.
- Figure and ground with no enclosure: a plane that stops short at both ends is read as an object in continuous space, and the gap beside it as a way through. Close the corners and the space becomes a set of boxes with doors.
- Salience by oddness, bought with restraint: the rich surface is valued against ≈60% of plain field. A second rich surface halves the first and both read as decoration (stated in the texts; seen in one building).
Colour relations: no applied accent; each surface quality has one job. Plain bands ≈60% (two fields, one value extreme, lowest chroma) : rich plane 12–25% (peak chroma, mid value, strong texture) : opposite-value plain field ≈12% : translucent or reflective planes take the rest. As an object in a context, be the plainest and furthest in value from the neighbours (one work).
Space and line: one module; overhang exactly 1/4 module; object ≈54% of the first view's width; lid and floor ≈30% each; free planes of unequal length; core at 45/55; one line weight, thin, constant and straight, crossing at right angles with no joint detail; reflective supports ≈5% of the width.
Type requirement: one face of constant stroke with no flourish and tabular figures, in few sizes; text hangs between the grid lines with baselines on the module, so a table shows the module without drawing it; labels may be capitals with slight extra spacing.
Motion and interaction: the planes stay and the viewpoint moves (lateral translation, 400–600ms, no scaling, no overshoot; planes at different depths move at 0.3–0.5× the scroll rate, switched off under reduced-motion). Content is revealed by sliding a plane aside. A component's states are positions on the module, never free sizes; focus is a full-weight outline, not a colour shift.
Fails when: content lengths are irregular and cannot sit on one module (user-written text, mixed media feeds); the format is too small to give up ground or to let planes stand free (a phone column turns every plane into a box); the first view must be dense (operational dashboards); several items each claim the rich surface.
Signature (do not carry together): a veined amber stone wall on a black carpet · mirrored cross-shaped columns · a dark metal-and-glass tower lattice behind an empty plaza.

**Frank Lloyd Wright.** *Grow every part from one unit, pin the whole to one heavy core, and throw long low bands outward past their support, so the eye is led out along the horizon.*
Process:
1. Choose one unit and derive every dimension, joint and line from whole multiples of it. Break it only where people pass. Changing the unit changes the whole character, so it is chosen first.
2. Set the anchor: one tall, narrow mass, off-centre, at the value extreme furthest from the ground and the only rough surface. It is the single vertical; nothing else may be tall.
3. Throw 2–3 bands out from the anchor at staggered heights and in opposite or crossing directions. Each is long and thin (depth 1/6 to 1/10 of its length), each projects beyond the one below and beyond its container so that its end is unsupported. Separate stacked bands by a recessed strip of the anchor's value, 20–25% of the band's height.
4. Stress one direction of joint only: the lines that run with the bands are drawn, the lines across them are suppressed. No dividers cross a band.
5. Concentrate detail where the view does not pass: pack it into the top ≈40% and a base band of ≈15% of any panel and leave the middle ≈45% clear. Build the detail from thin lines on the unit (≈0.5% of the panel width, doubled or tripled for emphasis instead of thickened), with small colour units only at their junctions.
6. Squeeze the approach: the way in is off the main axis, low and at the anchor's value, then the main room releases sideways along the bands (about 0.6× the normal height before, 1.6× after).
7. Mode for a long collection: replace the set of rooms by one continuous descending band with a fixed spine, entered at the summary.
Why it works:
- Grouping by likeness: parts that share one unit read as one grown thing even when their sizes differ widely. Mix units and the parts read as bought separately.
- Visual weight and leverage: a long thin band needs a visible heavy mass to hang from. With the anchor, the unsupported end reads as reach; remove the anchor and the same band reads as a floating strip, centre it and the reach becomes symmetry.
- Direction from joint emphasis: with cross joints suppressed, every surface has a grain, and the eye runs along it past the frame. Detail kept to the top and base frames the clear middle, where the content or the view is.
Colour relations: ≈90% neutral in one temperature family, as two or three values of one hue (lit, turned, shaded) and not as different hues; the anchor at the far value extreme; line ≈25% of a detailed panel, clear field ≥50%, colour ≈6% as small units at line junctions (1–2 hues, the rarest ≈1%); colour is never a plane.
Space and line: one unit; bands 1:6–1:10; upper band overhangs lower; recessed strip 20–25% of band height; one tall mass only; lines thin, straight (diagonals allowed), ≈0.5% of the width; detail 40 / 45 clear / 15.
Type requirement: letters that look built on the unit (even stroke, wide, geometric), with line height a whole multiple of it; labels run along a band in capitals spaced widely enough to read as part of the band (up to 2–3 letter widths at building scale, one work); reading text in columns no wider than about 70 characters, whatever the band's length.
Motion and interaction: everything travels along the band axis (slide 24–40px, 500–800ms, never scaling from the centre; none under reduced-motion). A header starts compressed and gains height and light past a threshold. Selection thickens the chosen band's leading edge and lowers the contrast of the others, plus a visible focus outline.
Fails when: the format is tall and narrow (a phone in portrait leaves no length for a band or a cantilever); the content is column-led and needs cross dividers (comparison tables, calendars by day); there is no single core to anchor to (a feed of equal posts); reading text is forced to the full band width.
Signature (do not carry together): long low brick bands under deep eaves · a plant drawn in leaded glass with small coloured squares · a continuous spiral ramp.

**Alvar Aalto.** *Start from the body's real posture, keep a strict plain frame, and break it exactly once with a free curve where people gather; strong colour marks only what hands and feet follow.*
Process:
1. Fix the posture before the layout: where the user's eye rests, how far the hand reaches, at what distance and angle the surface is seen. Put the primary action inside the reach zone and keep the resting line of sight free of the brightest and busiest elements.
2. Build a strict frame of plain rectangular masses joined at right angles (an L wrapping an open middle), with blank surfaces. Show rank by stepping the levels, the overview at the top, not by boxing.
3. Break the frame once: one free curve per view, placed where activity is densest, asymmetric, with lobes of unequal depth and straight sides. Nothing else departs from the right angle, so the curve cannot become a style.
4. Make one joint and reuse it everywhere: long straight runs that end in one tight bend of a single radius. No sharp corner and no second radius.
5. Light from many identical small sources in a regular grid over the main area (dozens, not one), so the surface is evenly lit with no hot spot and no visible lamp in the line of sight fixed in step 1.
6. Put the strongest colour where the body goes: the route and the touched parts (the path underfoot, edges, rails, handles) and the one curve of step 3, as lines and strips. At most one whole plane takes a second strong hue, and it faces an open, light side.
7. Vary the repeat inside the system: supports and intervals differ slightly from one to the next, and surfaces grow warmer and more textured toward the private end.
Why it works:
- Scale reference in use, not in elevation: a layout fitted to the real posture puts effort where the body is weakest. Designed for an upright viewer at a desk, the same layout puts the action out of reach and the glare in the eyes.
- Salience by oddness: one curve in an orthogonal frame is the odd thing, so it marks the place of activity without size or colour. Multiply the curves and they become texture; remove the frame and the curve has nothing to differ from.
- Grouping by likeness along a route: one colour on everything the feet and hands follow fuses separate parts into a single line through the building. Spend that colour on decoration and the route can no longer be told from the room.
Colour relations: light low-chroma field as the main surface; one mid-value warm tone for whatever is held; the strongest hue as lines and strips on the route (a few percent), repeated on the single curve; one second strong hue as rails and at most one whole plane opposite an open side; the darkest value underfoot; small soft tints for low-stakes objects.
Space and line: orthogonal frame; one curve per view; one bend radius; ≈60 identical light sources in a ≈9 × 7 grid (one work); stepped levels; intervals varied by roughly a sixth (not measured); the curve's lobes unequal.
Type requirement: reading size and line spacing set for the real viewing distance and angle found in step 1, larger than a desk default; open letterforms that survive glare and tilt; sentence case; a gentle scale with emphasis by weight, so rank steps like the levels and never shouts.
Motion and interaction: movement settles without overshoot (280–420ms; none under reduced-motion); feedback travels from the touched control along the marked route to what it changed; touched parts answer with a pressed state (inset plus a warmer value), not only a colour change; the light may shift slowly with the time of day.
Fails when: several tasks of equal density compete for the one curve (dashboards, multi-pane editors); the product already needs many category colours, so the route colour cannot be reserved; the route is marked by colour alone (it must also be a continuous line, a position or a label); the curve is applied to small components, or controls in the reach zone fall under 44px.
Signature (do not carry together): bent pale laminated wood · a wavy-outlined vessel or counter · a lemon-yellow floor through white rooms.

**Luis Barragán.** *Shut the world out, then ration what returns: one colour, one light and one opening per room, each felt against the room before.*
Process:
1. Enclose. Remove everything that belongs to another room (global chrome, cross-links, status). What remains is 4–6 large planes, and nothing small carries colour.
2. Sequence before composing. Order the views as rooms and set each to compressed (about 1/3 width, dim, low) or released (full, bright, tall). Alternate them, and make the step extreme: at least 2× in width and in luminance.
3. In each room assign the quiet field (35–45%, lowest chroma) and a ground plane (≈25%, the darkest and the only textured one).
4. Add one or two colour planes, each a whole surface edge to edge, in different hues: the farther one larger and cooler (15–20%); the nearer one narrow (5–10% of the area, ≤1/5 of the width), the warmest and most saturated, overlapping the far plane and standing on the seam between colour and quiet field.
5. Light it from a source the viewer cannot see, from one direction: let the light grade across a plane, spill a faint tint of the near colour onto its neighbours, and split a plane into two values of one hue instead of adding a hue.
6. Cut one opening sized to the hand in a plane at least 10× its size. End each passage with a small view (about 1/8 of the width) of the next room, in the opposite temperature.
Why it works:
- Perception adapts. After a dim, narrow, colourless stretch the same room reads brighter, larger and more coloured, so the feeling is made by the difference between consecutive states, not by any single screen.
- Saturation and area trade off. A large saturated field tires the eye and goes flat; a narrow one beside a large quiet field and a cooler plane stays vivid. Warm, narrow and overlapping in front of cool and large stacks three depth cues with no shadow.
- Colour that covers a whole plane, and light with no visible source, read as properties of the space and not as objects in it. The only object left is the opening, so it reads as the way through.
Colour relations: quiet field 35–45% (lowest chroma) : dark textured ground ≈25% : far plane 15–20%, cool, mid chroma : near plane 5–10%, warm, peak chroma; adjacent planes clash in hue and meet at a hard edge with no line; second tones come from value (shadow), never from a new hue; a gradient exists only as light falling off from its source.
Space and line: no drawn lines; every edge is two planes meeting; divisions off-centre near 1:3; near plane ≤1/5 of the width; opening to plane ≥1:10; compressed to released ≥1:2.
Type requirement: small, calm, low-detail letters set wide apart, placed like the opening (text height ≤1/40 of its field); one size tier for labels; reading text in a narrow measure on the quiet field; the face must stay legible small and letterspaced.
Motion and interaction: slow and directional. A transition is a threshold (≈200ms narrow and dim, then 700–900ms widen and brighten); ambient light drifts ≤3% in value over a minute, as a named ambient state that is static under reduced motion (thresholds then become instant value changes); a state change recolours or relights a whole plane, always with a label; nothing small moves.
Fails when: the task needs everything at once (dashboards, comparison tables); users jump non-linearly at speed, so thresholds become delay; colour lands on small components; more than three coloured planes or equal saturation everywhere; the compression is timid.
Signature (do not carry together): rough pink or magenta stucco walls · yellow light through glass · courtyard water and framed sky.

### 4.6 JAPANESE MA & WABI-SABI: the interval, emptiness, light
The movement: *ma* (the meaningful gap), emptiness as invitation, indirect approach, one material.
*The problem it answered:* How to make an interval (a gap, a pause, a dark or bare stretch) do the work that objects usually do, so that what is withheld directs attention. The viewer completes the work by looking through, crossing, filling in or choosing a route; the designer's job is to shape the gap, not to fill it.

**Katsushika Hokusai.** *Keep one small subject constant and make each view by changing what stands in front of it: the largest, busiest form is context, and its hollow points at the subject.*
Process:
1. Fix the constant: one subject whose silhouette is still recognisable at 0.5–2% of the area (4–11% of the width), and one label slot (≈4% of the width × 20% of the height, in a corner) that never moves between views. Everything else may change.
2. Stack the view in three flat planes (near, middle, far), each one value with no gradual recession. Put the dividing line anywhere from 50% to 75% down; it is a parameter, set per view.
3. Give the near plane the largest and most detailed form (40–65% of the area), and shape it so that it encloses a hollow of the ground value, 15–35% of the area.
4. Seat the subject whole inside the hollow, low and off its centre, at 1/4 to 1/15 of the hollow's width, with clear ground on every side. The hollow frames and never clips: no figure, word or number is cut by the near form. Aim the near form's strongest contour at the subject.
5. Draw every form in one thin even line (0.15–0.2% of the width) and fill it flat. Take line and fills from one colour family in 2–3 depths; the darkest depth replaces neutral black. Allow one gradient per view: a fall-off from an edge of the far plane.
6. Change state by re-tinting one whole plane (20–35% of the area) and leaving the layout untouched; never by sprinkling small marks.
7. Mode for teaching: build each form from 3 primitives and show the pair, construction and finished form in the same pose, in two stacked panels of equal height, same line weight.
Why it works:
- Salience by oddness, plus a scale reference: a small, calm, closed shape among large active detail is the odd one, and the near form tells the eye that the small thing is far and large, not minor. Enlarge the subject and it is an ordinary hero; remove the near form and it is a small thing in a bare field.
- Figure and ground: an enclosed region of ground reads as a shape in its own right, so what sits inside it is found without size or colour. If the hollow clips the subject, the subject joins the near form's texture and cannot be read.
- Constancy across a series: with subject and label slot fixed, every difference between views is information about the vantage. Change both and each view has to be learned from nothing.
Colour relations: bare ground and its tints 60–80%; one colour family in 2–3 depths ≈15–20%, carrying outline, near form and text (darkest depth on ground ≥4.5:1); an optional second, warmer family only as a whole state plane of 20–35%; the subject is defined by silhouette and position, not by a special colour.
Space and line: three flat planes; near form 40–65% with a hollow 15–35%; subject 0.5–2% of the area and whole; hollow 4–15× the subject's width; dividing line 50–75% down; one line weight; mass on one side and open ground on the other, never mirrored.
Type requirement: a compact label that survives small, stacked or vertical setting inside a ruled box; short captions that tag forms one to one; all text in the darkest depth of the colour family; the subject, if it is a number, keeps a readable absolute size (it is small relative to the near form, not small in pixels).
Motion and interaction: the subject and the label stay pinned while the near plane is replaced or moves; a state change re-tints a plane over 300–400ms; reveals are separated by rests of 200–300ms; under reduced motion all of it is a cut. If the subject is a control, its hit area is ≥44px whatever its drawn size.
Fails when: there is no single constant subject (mixed dashboards, feeds); the subject is a table or long text that cannot be small; the screen is so small that a hollow of 15–35% cannot hold the subject at reading size; the subject must be read at a glance from a distance (alerts).
Signature (do not carry together): a curling wave with claw-shaped foam · deep blue on buff paper · a snow-capped cone · a vertical title cartouche.

**Tadao Ando.** *Close the container in one surface treatment, empty its middle, and admit the opposite end of the value scale through a single exact cut that the user must cross the emptiness to reach.*
Process:
1. Fix one surface treatment in three values (lit, shaded, deep) and one module of 2:1. Every edge, opening and text block lands on a module line; the module shows only as faint seams and dots, which are texture and carry no information.
2. Close the arrival face: a blank, symmetric plane with one opening on its axis (≈1/4 of the width, ≈2/5 of the height) and nothing else.
3. Cut the container crosswise into three bands of about equal depth. Empty the middle one, light it, and route the path through it. At most one element may span the emptied band.
4. Make one extreme of value the medium: 25–50% of the key view sits at the deep value and the rest at the middle value, about 90% neutral in all.
5. Cut the opposite extreme once: a single aperture at the peak value, running edge to edge across its plane, 4–6% of the plane's width and 5–7% of the view. It marks the one destination. Nothing else, text included, takes the peak value.
6. Admit hue only inside an opening (one hue, seen through a cut), never on a surface, a control or text.
7. Mode for a transparent, repetitive screen: cap it with a lid 1–2% of the height and give the lower ≈50% to a still field that repeats the upper half at lower contrast.
Why it works:
- Luminance contrast after adaptation: in a view that is up to half deep value, a patch of 5–7% at the peak is the strongest signal available, so it needs no hue and no size. Multiply the cuts and each is a window; widen one and it is a lit wall with glare and no event.
- The emptied band is an interval between two states. Crossing it resets the eye, so arrival is felt against the stretch before. Fill the band and the result is three stacked sections.
- One treatment removes hue and texture as variables, so only value (light) and module (measure) can be read. A cut that runs edge to edge reads as a property of the wall and not as an object on it.
Colour relations: neutrals ≈90% in three values of one treatment; deep value 25–50% of key views; peak value 5–7%, in one aperture only; one hue, only inside an opening; one warm natural accent ≤2%. Text takes the end of the scale furthest from its plane, short of the peak (light neutral on the deep value, the deep value on the middle value), always ≥4.5:1; quietness comes from low chroma, small size and scarcity, never from low contrast.
Space and line: 2:1 module; three bands with the middle empty; one axial opening on a blank face; one aperture edge to edge; the only lines are module seams and cut edges, all sharp; at most one oblique element (its angle could not be measured). Control boundaries ≥3:1 or carried by a value step plus position.
Type requirement: small, low-chroma, widely spaced text locked to module lines, in a face that stays open at small sizes on a dark plane (regular weight, not thin, below about 18px); one large thin word allowed in the emptied band; text is never the brightest thing on the screen.
Motion and interaction: structure never moves, light does. Arrival starts at the deep value, holds briefly, then light enters from the aperture's side (0.9–1.4s, once per session, skippable, a cut under reduced motion). Focus is made by dimming the surround while the target keeps ≥3:1 and gains a visible focus mark. The crossing costs one scroll or one step and never hides the destination from keyboard or screen-reader order.
Fails when: the task is repeated or urgent (checkout, tools used many times a day, anything safety-critical), where the threshold is a tax; several actions have equal standing; the content is coloured imagery that must sit on surfaces; long reading text has to live on the deep value; poor screens or bright surroundings collapse three near neutrals into one.
Signature (do not carry together): fair-faced concrete with tie-hole dots · a cruciform slot of daylight · a blank street wall with one door.

**Kenya Hara.** *Leave the surface nearly bare, spend its only saturation on one figure, and press every piece of information back to the edges, so the viewer supplies the meaning.*
Process:
1. Write the surface's single proposition and choose one figure to hold it. A second proposition needs a second surface.
2. Choose the ground and leave 60–90% of it bare (≈60% where pictures appear, 85–90% on covers and text pages). Build it from 2–3 values of one temperature a few percent apart, or from one continuous field divided once at 50% of its height.
3. Place the figure alone. On a bare ground: on the vertical axis, in the upper half (centre of mass ≈37% down), ≈10% of the area. On a divided field: small (5–6% of the height), at ≈80% across, beside the dividing line. Keep a clear gap between it and everything else.
4. Give the figure the only saturated colour on the surface. If the field itself carries the colour, reverse it: the figure is then the only dark, hard-edged point. Either way the difference in kind is used once.
5. Press the information back into a second channel with no chroma and no weight: one weight throughout, small, widely spaced, flush on one margin, in blocks at the corners. The identity mark stays small, near the figure or at the periphery, never in a centred bar. The colour of the figure never touches the text.
6. Allow one display element per surface at 3–5× the body size, in the same weight; everything else stays ≤1.5×. Reading text runs in a measure of 65–75 characters.
Why it works:
- Salience by oddness, used once: the single saturated thing in an achromatic field is found at once at 10% of the area. A second coloured thing makes a palette, and the viewer starts comparing instead of looking.
- Completion by the viewer: where little is specified, the viewer projects, and a bare even field acts as a scale reference that makes a small figure read as a presence in a large space. Fill the field and the figure is one item in a layout.
- Two channels for two acts: the figure is seen by chroma, the information is found by position. They do not compete, and the text is there when wanted. Ink the text in the figure's colour and there are two figures.
Colour relations: bare ground 60–90% in 2–3 near values (≈1.1:1 apart, decoration only); one saturated hue on the figure, ≈10% of a bare ground, or a field that is itself one hue graded from deep at the far edge to pale at the dividing line (saturated share ≈40%) with a figure under 1%; information achromatic at ≥4.5:1 on its ground; only emblems that repeat what the text already says may sit tone on tone.
Space and line: one figure; bare share 60–90%; information in corner blocks on one margin; top margin ≥1/5 of the height; no rules except one hairline beside the display element; a divided field splits at 50%.
Type requirement: one regular weight for everything; hierarchy from isolation (a clear space at least 3 lines deep around a heading), position and the single display element; small sizes with wide spacing and generous leading, so the face needs open counters and even colour at 13–15px; labels name what a thing is, not what to do with it.
Motion and interaction: a beat of empty surface (150–250ms) and then a slow arrival (600–1000ms), cut under reduced motion; a control answers like material giving way (a 1px press or a tone step) and never grows or lifts; focus still gets a visible mark at ≥3:1.
Fails when: the content is comparative or dense (tables, dashboards, search results), because hierarchy by isolation needs room; several actions have equal weight; a small screen with 60%+ bare pushes the only action below the fold; the figure is weak or generic, so the surface reads as an unfinished or broken empty state.
Signature (do not carry together): off-white paper with one red figure · a horizon at mid-height with a tiny walker · blind-embossed emblems and wide-spaced serif capitals beside a hairline.

**Ikko Tanaka.** *Assemble the image from a few flat planes snapped to a coarse grid and held by one dark mass, so that every edge is two colours meeting and the eye finishes the figure.*
Process:
1. Lay a coarse grid of near-square cells over the whole frame, 3–4 cells a side, with no margin: the composition runs to every edge.
2. Reduce the subject to the 3–5 features that identify it and build it from 10–18 primitives (rectangle, circle, half-cell triangle), each snapped to a cell or half-cell.
3. Size the primitives unequally: 3–4 large planes carry about 55% of the area and the rest are small.
4. Anchor with the darkest value as the largest connected mass (34–77% of the frame in the measured works), then one flat ground plane (20–30%), then one light plane. The ground is a plane with a shape, not what is left over.
5. Spend hue in small flat pieces: 4–6 hues, none above ≈8% and 8–30% together, pale and saturated ones set directly against the dark mass. The strongest hue marks the most important feature and may fill one whole cell. No outlines: every piece is bounded by a neighbour of a different value.
6. Give the text one cell (a small flush block in a top corner), or make the text the image, each glyph a plane. Never give it a band that competes with the figure.
7. Make the whole nearly symmetric, then break it once: one piece offset by half a cell, or one diagonal line.
Why it works:
- Closure: with only the identifying features kept and locked to a grid, the viewer completes the figure, and the completing makes it memorable. Add detail and it is an illustration; drop an identifying feature and it is a pattern.
- Simultaneous contrast against a dark anchor: small patches beside a large dark mass look brighter and more saturated than they are, so 8% of hue is enough, and the mass holds scattered pieces as one body. Equalise the areas and the result is a quilt with no figure.
- With no outline there is no line weight to read, so every boundary is a decision about two planes, and figure and ground can trade places. Outlines would turn the planes into icons on a background.
Colour relations: dark anchor 34–77% : ground 20–30% : light plane ≈15% : 4–6 hues 8–30% in all, each ≤8%; all fills flat (one soft blush seen once); neighbours that mark a functional boundary differ by ≥3:1 in luminance, and two mid-value hues never touch where the edge has to be read; text sits on the anchor or the ground, not on a hue cell, at ≥4.5:1.
Space and line: grid of 3–4 near-square cells a side; full bleed; 10–18 primitives with 3–4 dominant; hairlines only as structure between columns of text; near-symmetry with one deviation. Modes: one two-part motif tiled on a lattice, with ground ≈60% and two hues that swap parts from unit to unit; or an image massed from the content itself in a single brand colour (one late poster, secondary source).
Type requirement: two voices far apart in scale (about 4–5:1): a display face with strong thick-and-thin contrast whose heavy strokes can act as planes and be recoloured one at a time, and a small plain sans block sized to one cell and set flush.
Motion and interaction: the image assembles: planes land one at a time as hard-edged cuts (60–90ms apart, 180–240ms each, under 1s in all, all at once under reduced motion), with no fade and no blur. A state change swaps a plane's fill and also changes its shape or adds a mark, so state is never carried by colour alone; nothing lifts or scales.
Fails when: the content is photographic or continuous (maps, gradient charts); there is long reading text, which has only one cell to live in; the product needs many semantic status colours, which collide with hues already spent on the figure; the subject has no identifying features (abstract services), which yields generic geometric clip-art.
Signature (do not carry together): a face built from squares and circles · black with pale and vermilion chips · many-coloured brush-serif characters on black.

**Kazuyo Sejima (SANAA).** *Remove rank: pack unequal rooms into one soft envelope at a single line weight and a constant gap, and let the only emphasis be a small shift of position or what shows through.*
Process:
1. Draw one envelope: a single continuous outline with soft corners and no front. Give it 3–4 entry points on different sides, all of equal standing.
2. List the contents as peers: 6–20 units from one shape family. Let size follow content (areas may differ by 10× or more), but give every unit the same treatment. Circulation spaces are units of the same family, not leftover corridors.
3. Pack the units with one constant gap, their straight sides parallel to the envelope. No unit is centred or isolated as a hero.
4. Fix one boundary weight for every unit and the envelope, as thin as the medium allows (0.15–0.35% of the frame width). Thinness is a matter of width, not of faintness: a boundary that marks a region keeps ≥3:1 against both neighbours.
5. Keep the structure achromatic within a narrow band of value (surfaces about 5 L apart, which groups but does not bound). Let colour enter only from the content or the context seen through or reflected: a low-chroma tint over up to 1/3 of a view.
6. Emphasise by displacement only: shift one unit 5–10% of its width out of line with its neighbours, or open the single gap where two units are offset. Never by size, weight or colour.
7. Mode for one large space: remove the partitions and zone a continuous surface by gradual change (slope, tone), with a few cut-out openings.
Why it works:
- With one treatment there is no pre-attentive rank, so the eye scans instead of being led and the user chooses the route. Add a hero and every other unit becomes secondary.
- A constant gap groups by uniform proximity: the units read as one field and the gap as a continuous figure. Vary the gap and clusters appear, which is hierarchy by another name.
- Against uniformity a small deviation is highly salient, so a 5–10% shift does the work a larger or bolder element would do. With structure thin and colourless, the content and the people in it become the figure.
Colour relations: structure in near-equal neutrals about 5 L apart (≈1.1:1, grouping only); one boundary value at ≥3:1 on those surfaces; no accent of its own; borrowed colour at low chroma over up to 1/3 of a view; a reflective plane reads darker than its surroundings, because it shows the ground. Primary and secondary text both ≥4.5:1.
Space and line: one envelope; 6–20 units; one gap; one boundary weight of 0.15–0.35% of the frame width; soft outer corners, with the units' own corners soft or square but of one family; one displacement of 5–10%; no shadows and no elevation.
Type requirement: one family in one or two neighbouring weights from light to regular; headings ≤1.25× the body; a small label centred in each unit or carried in the gap; hierarchy by position alone. Light weights only at sizes where they hold (regular below about 16px).
Motion and interaction: moving is crossing one field: sections pan, they are not replaced; a change dissolves in 400–700ms (a cut under reduced motion). Hover lets what lies beneath show through and also darkens the unit's boundary; keyboard focus adds a 2px mark at ≥3:1, the one permitted exception to the single weight.
Fails when: there is one action that must be found (checkout, sign-up, an emergency control), since equal rank hides it; importance really is unequal; a narrow screen turns the packing into a list, so envelope and gap vanish; more than about 20 units leave users without bearings; a guided, ordered flow is needed.
Signature (do not carry together): white surfaces and clear curved glass · a circular or lobed envelope with a thin bright edge · a mirrored canopy on needle-thin columns.

### 4.7 ART NOUVEAU & VIENNA SECESSION: ornament as system, the total work of art
The movement: Gesamtkunstwerk (design everything to the spoon), with ornament concentrated by rule rather than sprinkled.
*The problem it answered:* Industry had made ornament cheap and style arbitrary, so a building, its furniture and its printed matter no longer belonged together. The answer was to make ornament a rule: generate everything from one line, module or structure, and concentrate richness where attention should go instead of applying it everywhere.

**Alphonse Mucha.** *Build a fixed frame that does the pointing (one large quiet shape behind the focus, bands that hold the words), then change only one colour key and one emblem per variant.*
Process:
1. Fix a tall format (1:1.9–1:2.9) and stack it in bands that never move between variants: a title band (8–13% of the height; omitted in sets without text), the subject field, a foot band (up to 15%).
2. Place one large simple closed shape behind the subject: 75–95% of the width, touching or close to the top of the subject field, its centre on the subject's focal point, which sits 12–26% from the top. Make it the calmest area on the surface: lowest chroma, plain inside, with a regular border.
3. Let the subject break that shape in at least two places and run out toward the frame, so the shape reads as behind the subject and never as a box around it.
4. Oppose the two line kinds: the frame is still and regular (straight bands, a repeat about 4% of the width); the contents are drawn in one continuous line that is heavy on outer contours and light inside. Never make both fluid, or both rigid.
5. Hold every large fill at low chroma and close in value, and let one contour colour at the far end of the value scale do all the separating. Then give each variant one key hue, carried by its two largest soft areas, with the stronger share in the lower 40%; the shape from step 2, the ground and the contour stay the same in every variant.
6. Lock the words into the bands: letters widened or narrowed until the line fills its band exactly, drawn in the contour colour of step 5.
7. Pack detail round the shape and let it reach the frame; leave flat either the corners outside the shape (≈10%) or one blank zone beside the subject, not both.
Why it works:
- A complex subject seen against the largest calm area separates at once (figure and ground), and the concentric border leads the eye to the centre. Take the shape away and the subject sinks into the detail; make the shape the most intense thing and it competes with what it should present.
- With skeleton, ground and line constant, the one thing that differs is read as the message (grouping by likeness). Change two things per variant and the set stops being a set.
- A dark contour lets fills sit close in value without merging, so colour can stay quiet; a still frame against a moving line makes each legible by contrast of kind.
Colour relations: greyed throughout (large areas all low chroma; only small accents rise above); a pale warm-or-cool ground shared by the set; one dark contour hue; one key hue per variant on ≈25–40% of the area in two values; the backing shape neutral, paler inside than its border; an intense tone may cover the subject's main mass (≈10%) but never the backing shape.
Space and line: tall bands; backing shape 75–95% wide; focus 12–26% from the top; frame repeat ≈4% of the width; two line weights from one pen (outer ≈2–3× inner); no shadows.
Type requirement: a display face whose width can be fitted to a band without breaking (closed, rounded, even colour), used for short titles only; reading text in a plain face on the flat inside of the shape or the foot band, in the contour colour, at full reading contrast, never across detail.
Motion and interaction: the frame never moves. Changing variant swaps the key hue and the emblem in place (400–600ms cross-fade; instant under reduced motion); selection thickens the contour as well as tinting it; a variant is always named in its band, not told by hue alone.
Fails when: there is no single subject per view (tables, feeds, comparisons); the format is wide, so the backing shape cannot reach 75% of the width; titles are long or translated and cannot fill a band; many variants are shown small side by side, where greyed key hues cannot be told apart.
Signature (do not carry together): a figure with streaming hair before a halo · whiplash tendrils and flower borders · chalky lithograph tones on tan paper with hand-drawn lettering.

**Gustav Klimt.** *Flood the surface with intensity, then make the one calm, differently rendered patch the focus; separate things by a change of texture, not by lines.*
Process:
1. Fill the format: no empty ground. Build the field (55–80% of the area) from the content itself seen at texture scale, in one hue family and one fine grain, held in a value band narrow enough to fuse into a single tone at arm's length.
2. Raise one continuous silhouette for the main object: same hue, one clear value step brighter, about 20% of the area, tall, reaching near the top, with no outline.
3. Inside the silhouette, code kinds of content by texture grammar, not hue: one angular or gridded language, one round or organic language, and a shared filler motif running through both so they stay one body. Mix the two languages only where the content itself merges.
4. Reserve the focus: a patch ≤3% of the area (≤8% with its satellites) in the top fifth, rendered in the one mode used nowhere else (smooth, plain, low chroma, low detail). Never repeat that mode.
5. Pack the accents: one or two hues, ≤2% each, as clusters of small cells inside the pattern near the focus, never scattered and never on the field. Anchor the base with one band (10–15%) of a cooler contrasting hue in the finest grain, ended abruptly.
6. Over length, pulse the density: stretches 85–90% bare, carrying only thin drawing, then one section packed edge to edge.
7. Mode for content with no single subject: skip steps 2–4. Align everything parallel to the frame, set one level division at about 2/3 of the height, repeat the upper band below it as broken texture, and render the whole field in one size of mark, so that density of texture, not value contrast, carries the hierarchy; the lightest and the warmest tones stay ≤7% each, in bands near the top.
Why it works:
- Salience is difference from the surroundings. Where everything is rich, the smooth, pale, plain patch is the odd one and wins: emphasis by relief, so the focus can stay tiny and quiet.
- Fine detail at low contrast fuses into a tone. The field can hold a great deal of matter without competing, and a value step, or only a change of grain, is enough to make an edge. Half-merged things reward a second look.
- Each level of the nest changes exactly one variable (value, then texture grammar, then rendering mode), so the eye descends ground, object, kind, focus in order with no frame anywhere.
Colour relations: near-monochrome; one hue family covers 75–80% in two values (dark grainy ground ≈55% : bright object ≈20%); a near-black for the angular language, concentrated inside the object; cool base band 2–13%; accent clusters ≤2% each; the focus has the LOWEST chroma on the surface, so the peak of the hierarchy is the least saturated point.
Space and line: full bleed, no margins, no horizon; a format with no reading direction (near-square); object vertical and off-centre; focus 6–20% from the top; no drawn boundaries, edges are a value step (primary object) or a texture change at near-equal value (secondary); no shadows.
Type requirement: three textures that match the nest: a field face of even colour with no bold, which reads as grain; two label voices, one angular or tabular and one round, assigned by kind of content; the focal text alone in a softer, larger, generously leaded voice; display lettering sits inside a band as part of the pattern.
Motion and interaction: the surface is still and only light changes. Pointer, tilt or scroll passes a highlight over the field; arriving items brighten one cell at a time (≈120ms each, irregular 15–30ms offsets); the focus never animates and is never overlaid.
Fails when: the focus grows past about 1/5 (the field becomes a frame round a picture); the special mode is repeated (the field becomes wallpaper between cards); the field has high internal contrast or several hues (noise, and reading contrast fails); the content is sparse, so pattern has to be invented as decoration.
Signature (do not carry together): gold leaf · spiral, eye and check ornament · a pale naturalistic face in a square.

**Antoni Gaudí.** *Let position decide everything: a strict repeated structure whose members change girth where they carry more, coloured one step at a time along its length, with the colour broken into fragments and allowed to fall on the neutral parts.*
Process:
1. Lay the regular structure first: identical units at one interval along one axis (and identical rows across it). Every later irregularity sits on this grid and never moves it.
2. Size each member by what it carries: thickest and darkest where load gathers (the centre, the joints), thinner outward; let a member run unbroken for about half its length, then split into 2–4 thinner inclined members, so one end is orderly and the other dense.
3. Keep the structure neutral (lowest chroma, one value band) and hard-edged, at least half of any view.
4. Give colour by position, not by kind: each unit takes one dominant hue; neighbouring units differ by one step round the wheel; the two ends of the run have opposite temperatures.
5. Stratify by height: the strongest colour sits in the lowest zone, at hand and eye level; the top level stays plain and carries no colour.
6. Build each unit's hue from many small pieces of unequal size (about 10:1), and let it leave its unit: a soft-edged tint on the neutral structure beside it.
7. Where a surface is coloured directly, set contrasting patches inside a neutral fragment ground (≈40%) and cap every edge with one continuous smooth border. Sparse mode: scattered fragments ≤10–12% on a neutral ground.
Why it works:
- A strict grid underneath is the constant against which the broken surface is read as variation. Remove the grid and the same surface is noise; remove the variation and it is a spreadsheet.
- One hue per unit groups the fragments inside it; one step per neighbour makes the run a readable gradient, so colour tells you where you are. Random hues give a patchwork, one hue gives no location.
- Small unequal pieces fuse into one tone at a distance and become detail up close; the continuous border restores a clean contour so a broken surface still reads as one object. Girth that follows load makes mass itself the hierarchy.
Colour relations: neutral carrier ≥50%; one dominant hue per unit in 3–4 near values; neighbour step one notch, ends opposed in temperature; saturation highest low down, none at the top; colour on the carrier only as soft tint; inside a coloured surface, pale ground ≈40% and contrasting patches framed within it.
Space and line: one interval; member girth steps by position (centre to edge about 1.5–2:1, a visual estimate); split at mid-length into 2–4; structure edges hard, tint edges soft; members thicken at joints and may lean with what they carry; fragment sizes ≈10:1.
Type requirement: one family with a wide weight range, so weight can step with level as girth does (heaviest at the trunk heading, lighter at each split); soft-shouldered forms; text sits on the neutral structure or on a single flat tone at reading contrast, never across a fragmented surface.
Motion and interaction: animate the light, not the layout. Moving along the axis shifts the tint one step per unit (≈300ms); arriving items settle once with damping and no overshoot; no glinting or flashing. Position and state are always also given by label or order, since the hue ramp alone is not readable to everyone.
Fails when: the units have no natural order (the ramp invents one); hue must also mean status, which collides with hue as position; fewer than four units (no ramp to read); small screens where fragments fall below legible size; long reading text, which needs the flat carrier and leaves the method nothing to do.
Signature (do not carry together): broken-tile mosaic with a glazed rim · leaning, bone-like catenary forms · a rainbow of stained glass.

**Josef Hoffmann.** *Take one square as the unit of everything, leave the planes blank, and let a thin line on every edge and a cluster of small squares at every opening do all the describing.*
Process:
1. Choose one square module and make every dimension a whole multiple of it. Use the same square at two or three scales at least (the cell, the opening, the plane), down to the smallest object in the set.
2. Keep the plane blank and flush: one flat ground on 80–85% of the surface, with nothing projecting, receding or shaded.
3. Trace every edge and corner of every volume with one thin line in the tone furthest from the ground, one weight throughout (about ¼–⅓ of the module). The line, not a fill, states the form.
4. Open the plane only with clusters of module squares (2 across by 3–4 down for a small opening, up to 8 across for a field), holes wider than the bars between them (bar ¼–⅓ of the pitch). Pattern exists only at an opening, a band or a threshold, never on the plane of step 2.
5. Compose the whole symmetrically; one element may rise above the rest (about 1.5× its neighbours).
6. Allow one exception of each kind per object: one round part (a foot, a knob, a hoop) and one filled panel in the single precious tone (up to half of one face), bounded by the edge line of step 3.
Why it works:
- The same unit at every scale groups parts by likeness, so things of different size and purpose read as one family with no shared ornament. One dimension off the module reads as a mistake.
- An outline is the cheapest sign of a volume. With the fill and the shadow removed, mass is read from its edges alone, so a heavy thing looks like a folded sheet. Fill the planes and it becomes a stack of boxes.
- Pattern is rationed, so texture means "something opens here" (salience by oddness), and one round part among squares is found at once. Spread the lattice everywhere and both signals are lost.
Colour relations: near-binary: light ground ≥80%, the opposite tone ≤15% as lines and small squares, both leaning to the same temperature, with no stepped mid-tones between them; one precious accent, either on edges or as the single fill panel; no gradients.
Space and line: one module; one line weight; hole to bar about 3:1 to 2:1; clusters counted in whole cells; flush planes; right angles except the one round part; symmetry with at most one taller element.
Type requirement: even-stroke, squarish letters with no contrast between thick and thin, in capitals spaced to fill a square or double-square box; a monospaced or tabular cut where figures must sit in cells; small plain reading text on the blank plane, never on a lattice or checker.
Motion and interaction: stepped and counted. Progress is cells filling once, one at a time (≥100ms each, a single pass, no blinking); a filled cell also carries a mark or label, so state is not told by tone alone. A control's hit area is a whole multiple of the module that reaches 44px or more, whatever the size of the visible square.
Fails when: the content has no unit size (photographs, long prose, text of unpredictable length that a square must truncate); small lattice cells are made into targets; a fine checker or lattice lies behind text (it vibrates and breaks reading contrast); the interface needs more than one accent to code categories.
Signature (do not carry together): black-and-white checker and punched square lattice · a white cube bound in dark or gilt edge bands · ball feet.

**Charles Rennie Mackintosh.** *Raise tall plain bays in a strict repeat, spend all the ornament at one height and all the irregularity in one narrow bay.*
Process:
1. Choose the register for the whole room before anything else: all dark or all light, one near-neutral ground on 90% or more of it.
2. Divide the field into tall bays (1:2.3 to 1:3) with thin full-height verticals, and repeat the identical bay at a strict interval (for example 4 + 4). The bays stay plain.
3. Break the repeat in exactly one narrow bay (about 1/8 of the width), set off the axis: asymmetric, denser than the rest, holding the way in.
4. Put the ornament on one horizontal line: a small cluster of squares (3 × 3, about 1/8 of the bay's width) at the head of each tall member, at the same height in every bay, 1–5% of the area in total.
5. End a long straight with one tight curve or one small mark, at that line and nowhere else (straight to curve at least 10:1).
6. Choose one colour mode per room: either small notes at the nodes of step 4 (under 5% of the wall), or one object covered whole in a saturated tone (about a third of its own area, in a neutral frame). Never both.
7. Enclose with height: let the members beside a group stand 2–2.6× taller than what they flank, so the group becomes a room inside the room.
Why it works:
- A tall plain bay makes emptiness read as intended, and its height carries the eye up to the one place where something happens. Scatter the marks down the bay and the climb has no end.
- Against a strict repeat a single irregular bay is found without being marked (salience by oddness). If every bay varies, there is no entrance.
- Marks at one height in every bay join into a band across the whole wall (grouping by alignment), so a frieze exists with almost no ink. One saturated thing among neutrals gains by contrast; add scattered colour and it loses it.
Colour relations: one register; neutral ground ≥90%; line in the tone furthest from the ground; a second neutral, lighter than the line, for secondary emphasis; colour either as notes under 5% in two or three low-chroma hues, or one saturated object or surround (≈8% of a view); nothing in between.
Space and line: bays 1:2.3–1:3; identical repeat with one narrow off-axis exception; cluster of 3 × 3 at the head; one ornament line; long ruled verticals, one closed curve; flanking members 2–2.6× the height of what they enclose.
Type requirement: tall condensed capitals with straight stems and crossbars shifted off centre, the same move as a long vertical with one detail near the top; display and labels only, placed on the ornament line; reading text in a plain face in its own wide column, never squeezed into a bay.
Motion and interaction: within a room only the node responds (the cluster, the head of the member); the bays hold still. A marked or selected node changes shape or fill as well as tone, and its hit area is 44px or more however small it is drawn. Moving between a dark and a light room is the one large transition (≈500ms cross-fade; a hint, since the dark-to-light threshold was not seen).
Fails when: the content is wide (tables, maps, charts) and cannot be cut into tall bays; long text is set in 1:3 columns (the measure is too narrow to read); the screen fits a single bay, so there is no repeat to break; categories need several colours at once.
Signature (do not carry together): the stylised rose in a square · the ladder-backed high chair · condensed capitals with high crossbars and ligatures.

### 4.8 MINIMALISM / LIGHT & SPACE: the serial, the specific, perception itself
The movement: no composition, only order, interval, material and light. Perception is the medium.
*The problem it answered:* How can a work hold attention with no composition at all (no hero, no balance, no depiction), so that the order itself is the experience? The answer at the top rung: fix a rule or a condition in advance, execute it plainly, and let interval, repetition, light and the viewer's own slow perception do the work that arrangement used to do.

**Donald Judd.** *Abolish ranking: one stated rule sets both the units and the gaps between them, so the interval counts as much as the thing and the run is read as a whole.*
Process:
1. Fix one unit before any content: one outer envelope and one orientation, or one constant spine spanning the whole run. The count may change to fit the container; the unit never rescales.
2. Derive the interval from the unit. Default: gap = unit (1:1), so empty and full alternate as equals. Nothing is clustered and nothing is centred.
3. If sizes must vary, state one ratio and apply it to units and gaps in opposite directions (e.g. units ×0.55 while gaps ×1.75 per step; over five units this gives the observed ≈11:1 for units and ≈1:9 for gaps), or hold the units constant and step only the gaps by a fixed decrement (each about 25% less). Keep one constant element beside the series so the rule can be read.
4. Put every difference inside the envelope: a split at half, a shelf, a diagonal, a doubled inner wall. Outer size and position never signal anything.
5. Assign colour by role, not by item: one saturated colour on one whole face of each unit (the front or the recessed inside, 20–40% of what is visible, the same face in every unit); the shell stays neutral and shows what it is. Or: constant element neutral, variable units saturated.
Why it works:
- When gap and unit are equal, figure and ground are balanced and the gap becomes countable as an element. Shrink the gap and the units fuse into one block (a list); widen it and each becomes an isolated object.
- With every envelope identical, grouping by likeness is total, so the eye stops ranking and reads the series; the only salient thing is a real difference inside a unit. Enlarge one unit and it becomes a hero with a supporting cast.
- Colour on a whole inner face reads as a property of the unit, and neutral outside with colour inside gives depth with no shadow. Put on small parts it turns into a signal and brings ranking back.
Colour relations: neutral ground and shell 60–80%; one saturated colour on whole faces only, 20–40% of each unit, identical placement in every unit; a second saturated colour only as a second role, never as a second rank; no gradients except the shell picking up its surroundings.
Space and line: gap:unit 1:1 (equal mode) or one ratio running opposite ways; one envelope; no drawn lines, every edge is two planes meeting; low, even density; no unit under 44px on its short side.
Type requirement: sizes taken from the same series as the layout (three steps, no in-betweens); labels read as specifications (plain, even, tabular numerals, at most two weights); text sits inside a unit and never bridges a gap.
Motion and interaction: serial and identical: a constant stagger (about 90ms per unit), near-linear, no overshoot; pointer or press changes the light on a face, never its size or height; on resize units are dropped or added, never scaled.
Fails when: one item truly must outrank the rest (an alert, a primary action among peers); content is dense or the screen short, because gap = unit halves capacity; items have very unequal content length, so one envelope truncates or pads.
Signature (do not carry together): cantilevered boxes stacked up a wall · raw metal shell with a coloured translucent face · factory-sharp right angles.

**Agnes Martin.** *Hold every difference just above the threshold of notice: one even division and near-equal fills, so the surface is a single tone from far away and a patient structure up close.*
Process:
1. Take one simple format and decide its status: a panel on a page (the division floats, inset 11–15% on every side) or the surface itself (the division runs to all four edges). Never a mixture.
2. Divide it by counting, not by content: either 8–14 full-width bands of equal height, or a fine all-over grid of cells that are never square (1:2 to 1:5; 40 or more divisions on the long count). Pitch comes from the container. No cell is larger, emptier or placed specially.
3. Fill within one narrow value band: every fill within about 9 L of every other and low in chroma, so fills differ by temperature only. Order them as a short phrase of 2–4 terms and repeat it strictly (every second, every third, or the phrase stated twice about the centre line).
4. Draw the division last, with one thin line weight only 8–10 L from the ground, and let it keep the small irregularity of being made by hand. The line must be weaker than any fill difference is wide.
5. Set two reading distances: at overview the division must fuse into tone; content becomes legible only at the near scale, sitting on the lines.
Why it works:
- Fine, low-contrast detail fuses into tone. The eye gets one calm value at a distance and a countable structure up close, so hierarchy is carried by viewing distance instead of size. Raise the line contrast and it becomes a table; make the cells square and it becomes tiling.
- At equal value nothing advances or recedes, so there is no figure, no depth and no rank; differences of temperature read as light, not as objects. One darker band at once becomes a stripe on a background.
- Near-threshold differences slow the viewer: the eye has to adapt before it can see them, and that waiting is the calm. Any strong accent resets adaptation and the rest goes blank.
Colour relations: one ground at a value extreme, 45–60%; 1–3 fills within about 9 L of it and of each other, chroma low (roughly a tenth of full), 40–55% in strict alternation; division line 8–10 L from the ground; no accent at all. Text is a separate ink at reading contrast (4.5:1 or more), and it is the only strong contrast present.
Space and line: one format, centred; equal bands (two heights near 3:5 with strips of about 2% as a variant); one line weight under 0.15% of the side; margins 11–15% or none; no focal point, no cluster.
Type requirement: light and even in colour so a paragraph reads as another ruled texture; hierarchy by a small size step (about 1.25×) and by position on the lines, never by weight; small plain labels on the lines; must hold reading contrast at light weight.
Motion and interaction: opacity only, 1s or longer, row by row; zoom is the main interaction (collapsed band = tone, opened band = ruled detail); selection deepens a fill by one step and adds a mark or label, since a near-equal-value change cannot carry a state alone.
Fails when: content has priorities, alerts or a primary action; categories must be told apart (equal-value hues vanish for colour-blind users and in greyscale, so each needs a label); small or sunlit screens and low-vision users, where the division is invisible, so nothing essential may depend on it.
Signature (do not carry together): pencil-ruled grid on a six-foot square canvas · pale pink, blue and peach washes on white · the trembling hand line.

**Dan Flavin.** *Colour is thrown, not applied: thin sources on the edges light an empty field, and zones are made by whose light reaches them.*
Process:
1. Fix a kit before designing: one source shape of constant thickness in at most four lengths at 1:2:3:4. Nothing custom; all invention goes into count, placement and orientation.
2. Empty the field and give it a neutral value with headroom (at least 30 L away from the source's value). No panels inside it. Place sources only on structure: boundaries, folds, thresholds, the seam between two zones.
3. Split colour into two roles. The source is the thinnest thing present, at the value extreme and nearly without chroma. The wash it throws carries the peak chroma and the area: half strength within about 3 source widths, gone by 8–10.
4. Give each zone one hue. Order the hues along the main route from cool at the entry to warm at the goal, and make the goal the smallest, warmest and brightest zone, seen framed by the largest cool one.
5. Where two washes meet, let them overlap and mix toward a paler, more neutral blend. Never draw a divider there.
6. Barrier mode (to stop movement while showing what lies beyond): repeat the source side by side with gaps no wider than one source, two hues alternating 1:1, covering the passage.
7. Switch on in one direction only: the source rises in a single monotone fade, then its wash spreads. Never blink, pulse or flicker.
Why it works:
- A large soft gradient is read as illumination and discounted, so content keeps its own colours while the zone is still felt. A hard-edged filled panel in the same hue would read as an object competing with the content.
- A thin pale source avoids a glare point, and sitting on the edge it leaves the centre free. Saturate the source instead and it becomes a neon sign: the eye goes to the line and the field goes dark.
- A seam that is paler than both of its sides reads as a join, not as a wall, so zones stay one space. With a fixed kit, every difference is read as arrangement, which is the only variable left.
Colour relations: neutral field 70–85%, mid value; sources under 3% at the value extreme, lowest chroma; washes 15–30% carrying peak chroma, at most four hues, one per zone, ranked cool and large to warm and small; overlaps paler than either parent; text only where the wash has fallen low enough to keep 4.5:1, or the wash is capped under text.
Space and line: sources 0.3–0.5% of the short side, four lengths at 1:2:3:4 (stepped 1:2:3:4:3:2:1 when grouped); all on edges; centre empty; the visible source is never the hit area (control targets 44px or more around it).
Type requirement: monoline and narrow, of even stroke like the source; set on the neutral field, not on the peak of a wash; every zone carries a text label, since hue alone may not name it.
Motion and interaction: on = one fade of the source (about 150ms, opacity 0 → 1, monotone), then the wash spreading over 400–600ms; repeated units follow at about 40ms each; off reverses (wash first). Reduced motion: the final state appears with no transition.
Fails when: the ground sits at the source's own value extreme (no headroom for a wash); long text or data colours must sit inside a wash; more than about four zones; meaning (status, category) would rest on hue alone.
Signature (do not carry together): bare fluorescent tubes · pink, green, yellow and blue catalogue light · a glowing corner or leaning barrier in a white room.

**James Turrell.** *Hold one field and one opening still, and change what the opening looks like only by changing its surround, more slowly than the eye can follow.*
Process:
1. Clear the view to one continuous field of a single tone, 70–85% of the area, with nothing on it: no chrome, no second surface.
2. Cut one aperture and put all content inside it (a quarter to a half of the width, wider than tall, 2:1 to 3:1 when rectangular). There is only ever one.
3. Choose its edge once per view. Cut: two flat fills meeting with no thickness, no outline, no shadow. Or dissolved: a soft edge, corners rounded to about 15% of the height, and a step of only 4–8 L from the field.
4. Give the chroma to the field and keep the aperture's own fill fixed and near-neutral. The aperture will appear tinted toward the field's opposite; choose the field's hue to get the appearance wanted, and never repaint the aperture.
5. For depth with no object (entry and empty views): nest 3–5 concentric steps of 4–9 L each, with one larger jump (about 19 L) at the outermost boundary, each band a soft inward gradient ending in a crisp step.
6. Put time in the field only: it moves between states too slowly to be seen moving, while the content stays fixed. Any meaning is stated inside the aperture in words or an icon first; the field may then agree with it.
Why it works:
- Simultaneous contrast: a neutral patch takes on the opposite of its surround, so one fixed fill yields many apparent colours and the viewer catches their own perception changing. Colour the aperture directly and it is just a coloured panel.
- An edge with no thickness removes the cues that separate surface from depth, so the opening flips between a flat patch and a space, and attention stays on it. Add an outline or a shadow and it settles into a card on a background.
- Change below the threshold of notice is found only after it has happened (adaptation), which makes the viewer wait. With a single aperture nothing competes; a second one turns the field into a page.
Colour relations: field 70–85% with the highest chroma present; aperture 10–25%, fixed, near-neutral, 4–8 L from the field (dissolved) or at a clear value step (cut); field and apparent aperture colour are near-opposites; nested steps stay in one hue; content ink is checked at 4.5:1 or more against the aperture in every field state.
Space and line: no lines at all; one aperture, centred or just below centre; edge either cut or dissolved, never both; concentric nesting only; focus indicators remain visible (3:1) as the one permitted outline.
Type requirement: thin and quiet, large rather than heavy, held inside the aperture; soft in value but at reading contrast; no text on the field.
Motion and interaction: ambient drift is a named class (`ambient-field-drift`), 30–120s per state, linear, pausable, with a static reduced-motion state that holds one field/aperture pair. Interaction is 600–1200ms fades of value only; drill-down enlarges the aperture in stages while the field dims; nothing slides or scales with bounce.
Fails when: content needs several panels or side-by-side comparison; colour-coded data, photographs or product colours sit in the aperture (the surround shifts them); long reading against a saturated field (afterimages, fatigue); urgent states, which slow change cannot deliver.
Signature (do not carry together): a razor-edged oculus open to the sky · saturated violet or crimson emitted light · nested glowing ellipses.

**Sol LeWitt.** *State a rule, run it to exhaustion, and show every result in an identical cell in sorted order: completeness replaces choice.*
Process:
1. Write the rule as one sentence before drawing anything: the unit, the variables, the constraint. The sentence is displayed with the result, in plain small text.
2. Choose a deliberately plain unit and one line weight for everything (frame to opening between 1:4.5 and 1:8), so that only the variable can be looked at.
3. Enumerate every valid result of the rule. No curating, no top three, no duplicates. If a result is valid it is shown.
4. Give each result an identical cell on a visible lattice, sorted by one stated key (how many parts it combines), and a code built from that key and its index. Let each row be as long as its count: the ragged edge is information.
5. Derive every visual property from the index, never per item: singles take one of a small fixed set (four); a combination is its singles overlaid, so its colour is their mixture, each added part makes it one tone darker, and figure differs from ground by direction alone.
6. Size the lattice so the whole set fits one view. Where it matters, state the same set a second time in another representation, in the same order.
Why it works:
- Completeness can be checked. A viewer who sees every case, in order, trusts that nothing was hidden, and an empty cell becomes a fact. A curated subset asks to be trusted and invites the question of what was left out.
- With unit, cell and line identical, grouping by likeness is total and the single varying property is all that remains to see; small differences become obvious. A decorated or varied unit makes the differences unreadable.
- Derived colour and tone can be decoded: a mixture points back to its parts, and more parts means darker, so the sort key appears as a density gradient and gives order without any element being larger.
Colour relations: neutral ground and one line colour at maximum value contrast carry the structure; at most four index colours of similar weight, none ranked; every further colour is an overlap of those, never a new swatch; tone steps of about 2 L per added part at overview; no accent, no signal colour.
Space and line: equal cells on a visible lattice; one line weight; frame to opening 1:4.5–1:8; margin about 5%; rows of unequal length allowed; no hero, no merged cells; each cell at least 44px.
Type requirement: mechanically regular with tabular figures, one weight per role; a code in the same corner of every cell; the rule sentence set small and plain. The code, not the colour or the direction, is what identifies a cell.
Motion and interaction: the system is seen running: cells appear in sorted order at a constant interval (30–40ms, linear), lines draw at one speed; changing a parameter regenerates the whole set in order; a toggle shows the rule behind any view. Reduced motion: the set appears complete.
Fails when: the valid set is unbounded or too large for one view (beyond roughly 150 cells), or has one member; the user wants a recommendation, not the catalogue (a checkout, an emergency action); the content is narrative and has no variable to enumerate.
Signature (do not carry together): white open-cube lattices · pencil lines in four directions on a wall · a typed instruction certificate.

### 4.9 OP & KINETIC ART: the eye and the moving viewer complete the work
The movement: color and movement happen in perception and in the user's own motion. Apply the guardrails strictly here.
*The problem it answered:* How can a still, flat, untouched surface make an event that happens in the viewer (in the eye, and through the viewer's own movement and hand) and not a picture of one? Each answer removes the author's touch and the focal hierarchy, fixes a system of identical parts, and leaves perception or participation to produce the result.

**Bridget Riley.** *Repeat one unit over the whole field, lock every property but one, and move that one along a single axis on a composed tempo: long calm, geometric build, off-centre climax, shorter release.*
Process:
1. Fix the unit and the lattice. One shape, tiled edge to edge with a constant count per row (12–20 rows, or 40–110 lines), in a format with no direction of its own (near 1:1). Margin, colour pair and weight are locked from here on.
2. Choose ONE variable and ONE axis: the unit's width, height, curvature, tilt, value step or chroma. A second variable is allowed only on the perpendicular axis, on its own curve. Counts never change, so every difference the eye finds is that variable.
3. Place the climax (the extreme value of the variable) off-centre on that axis: at 30–35% or 65–70% of it, or at the far edge. Never at 50%.
4. Compose the tempo around the climax: hold the resting value over 25–45% of the axis; build in geometric steps (each about 0.8 of the last) over 30–40% of it, to a total ratio between 3.7:1 and 18:1; release over a shorter run (about 0.85 of the build) and stop short of rest (≈75%), or omit the release when the climax is at the edge.
5. If the variable is colour, change chroma only: hold lightness within about 5 L from full strength down to neutral, alternate two opposed hues, and keep full chroma inside the climax band (≤15–20% of the axis). In a two-value field keep the dark share at 46–50%.
6. Mode for interface use: where the units are content (rows, columns, items), every unit is a flat surface and only the intervals follow the curve. Where the field is a pattern, it is decoration: it carries no text and no control, covers a bounded zone, and has a flat substitute the user can switch to.
Why it works:
- A regular gradient of size is the cue the eye uses for a surface turning away, so a smooth change in one dimension reads as a fold or as speed. Because the count per row is constant, the change is read as one surface bending, not as different objects. Vary two things on one axis and it reads as noise.
- Adaptation: the long calm stretch sets the rate the eye expects, so the build is felt as acceleration and the climax as a place. Centred, the climax becomes a symmetric target and the field its frame; with build and release equal, it becomes a mirrored ornament and stops being an event.
- Chroma changed at constant lightness alters intensity without altering depth or weight, so the colour peak stays in the same surface as the neutral and does not punch a hole in it.
Colour relations: two values at 46–50% dark and nothing else; or one opposed pair plus a neutral of the same lightness, with full chroma on ≤15–20% of the axis; or many hues (≈12), none above 16%, with a light interval of about a third of the area between them. Every colour is judged beside its real neighbour, never as a swatch.
Space and line: full bleed, margin under 1%; constant count; calm 25–45% : build 30–40% : release shorter than the build; climax at 30/70 or at the edge; extreme ratio 3.7:1–18:1; hard edges, no outline, no shadow, no trace of the hand.
Type requirement: one weight; rhythm from width or tracking, stepped on the same curve (a family with a continuous width range of at least 1.6:1); anything the user must read stays at the wide end, at full size, on a flat panel.
Motion and interaction: the tempo is already in the layout, so nothing has to move. Entrances stagger on the curve (intervals shrinking 40, 30, 20, 10, 5ms). A pattern field may shift phase by up to one pitch with scroll or pointer, in cycles of 2s or longer, never passing from light to dark more than 3 times a second; under reduced motion it is still. A compressed row may be DRAWN 16–20px high, but its pointer target stays ≥44px: rows drawn smaller share one target that reopens them, and keyboard focus always opens a row to full height.
Fails when: fewer than about 12 repeats lie on the axis (no curve can be read); the units are unequal (mixed cards, media); every row must be read or compared at the same size (tables, forms); text or controls would sit on the pattern; a full-contrast periodic field with a 6–16px period fills the viewport (pattern glare).
Signature (do not carry together): black and white only · a checkerboard or waves squeezed to slivers · a vermilion and turquoise pair dying into grey.

**Victor Vasarely.** *Write a closed alphabet of two-layer units, fill the field with it without a gap, then bend the whole lattice with one function, so that volume and focus come from distortion and never from shading.*
Process:
1. Fix one cell that tiles in both directions (equal sides) and give it exactly two layers: a ground, and one inset figure 55–90% of the cell wide. Fix a short list of figures (3–6) and an indexed colour set. From here on every element is a permutation: figure × ground index × figure index.
2. Fill the field edge to edge with cells that touch. Space comes only from shrinking the inset, and calm only from lowering the contrast between the two layers. No cell is left blank.
3. Colour by ramp, not by pick: one or two hue families per field, 4–6 value steps in each. Inside a cell the two layers contrast strongly; between neighbouring cells the same layer moves one step. Run one ramp along each axis.
4. Apply ONE displacement function to the whole lattice: cell size depends only on the distance from a peak, 2.2–2.9× at the peak, 0.5–0.7× in a ring at the rim, 1× beyond it. The cell count and the connections between cells never change. The lens may cover up to 85% of the field.
5. Mark exceptions inside the alphabet: swap figure and ground, or exchange the figure for another from the list. Keep them few (one to a handful per field), and never add an outline, a shadow or a new shape.
6. Two-value mode: with only a dark (43–68% of the area) and a light, draw the figure as stripes of the light that taper about 3:1 and bend, and separate overlapping parts only by a change of stripe direction.
Why it works:
- Texture gradient: cells that swell and then crowd together at a rim are what a regular lattice looks like on a sphere, so the eye chooses "regular grid on a curved surface" over "irregular grid on a flat one". Enlarge the centre without compressing the rim and it reads as one big tile; break the lattice's continuity and it reads as collage.
- In a closed alphabet every unit is predictable, so a single permutation is found before reading starts. Add an outside form or an outline for emphasis and nothing is the odd one any more.
- One-step neighbours group by likeness into a continuous surface, while the strong step inside each cell keeps the unit legible: the field shimmers and does not clash.
Colour relations: one or two hue families, 4–6 values each; layer to layer, high contrast (an inversion must survive with hue removed, ≥3:1); neighbour to neighbour, one step; the two inset tones near-equal in share (22% : 24%); a two-value field splits about 40 : 40 with a mid tone at the edge; no blank ground.
Space and line: one cell size before distortion; inset 55–90%; peak 2.2–2.9×, rim 0.5–0.7×, lens ≤85% of the field, centred or off-centre, optionally paired with a pinch (0.4×); no drawn lines, since every edge is two flat colours meeting.
Type requirement: letters as units: capitals at an even advance (fixed pitch, or tracked 0.2em or more) so a word is a row of cells; a second fixed-pitch voice for the code that names each permutation; text sits on a cell's flat ground at 1× or larger, never in a rim cell.
Motion and interaction: change travels through the lattice; objects do not travel across it. Focus moves the peak to the chosen cell and the neighbours rescale by distance over 300–500ms; under reduced motion the lens is fixed (the illusion is complete when still). Size the base cell so that an interactive rim cell is still ≥44px (base ≥88px at 0.5×), or keep controls out of the rim. A state is an inversion plus a second cue (a figure swap, the peak, a label), never the two colours alone.
Fails when: items differ in aspect or length (prose, wide tables); the field has fewer than about 9 cells per side (no rim to compress); a narrow screen pushes rim cells below a touch target; several things must be primary at once; the content has no natural unit.
Signature (do not carry together): a checker bulging into a sphere · discs in squares in saturated primaries · zebra stripes.

**Carlos Cruz-Diez.** *Do not paint the colour you want: set narrow bands side by side so that it arises between them, and make which colour arises depend on where the viewer is.*
Process:
1. Fix one band direction and one pitch for the whole field: a band is 0.4–0.7% of the field's length where neighbours must fuse, and up to 1.5% where one must induce a colour in the other; a repeat is 2–3 bands. All bands are equal in width, the darkest and the lightest included. There are no separators.
2. Run the bands edge to edge, across the direction of travel. Any emptiness is one solid block (up to 55% of the format), never scattered.
3. Choose 2–3 band colours per zone as generators. The colour meant to be seen is the one produced between them, by fusion or by induction; it is not painted (if it appears at all it is ≤15%, as a band among bands).
4. Make every figure and every level by zone: inside a region, swap one generator for another, shift the phase by half a pitch, or slant the bands. Keep to about 3 × 3 zones and draw no outline. At most one counter-form crosses the bands, about half the field wide.
5. Add a second layer whose alignment with the first depends on the viewer's position (the sides of a relief, a slower layer, a tilt). Seen frontally the field is at its quietest; intensity appears off-axis. When the viewer stops, the colour stops.
6. Mode for interface use: the band field is decoration and carries no text and no control; reading and acting happen on the solid block of step 2. The induced colour never carries meaning by itself.
Why it works:
- Below the eye's colour resolution, neighbouring bands fuse into a mixture; a little above it, each band pushes its neighbour toward its own opposite. Either way the seen colour exists only in the viewer, so it changes with distance, angle and light. Widen the bands past about 1.5% of the field and they read as two paints in stripes; paint the target and it becomes a surface.
- An inducer needs area: a dark band as wide as the light one shifts it, while a hairline only draws an edge. That is why the dark is a full band or the whole ground.
- With one pitch everywhere, a change of sequence or phase is the only event, so a zone reads as a figure with no contour and the field keeps a single skin.
Colour relations: generators near-equal in share (20–30% each); dark and light inducers as wide as the hues; the target colour absent, or ≤15%; frontal state low in chroma (≤17 in the one relief measured), off-axis state saturated; a void is the darkest value on the surface; no meaning is assigned to an induced colour.
Space and line: one direction, one pitch, straight parallel bands only; formats made to be walked along (up to 1:14); zones ≈3 × 3; one counter-form ≈50% of the width; void ≤55%, in one block; relief depth tiny against the format.
Type requirement: never on the bands. Tall, narrow letters with even vertical stems at a regular advance, so a line of type repeats the band rhythm at a larger pitch; one large size and one small; set on the solid block.
Motion and interaction: colour is a function of position input (scroll, pointer, tilt) and never of a clock: two layers at 1.0× and 0.85×, or a phase offset of up to half a pitch. Where scroll-linked animation is unsupported, or reduced motion is asked for, the field rests at one composed phase: declare that static state first, then add the scroll timeline inside a support check (`@supports (animation-timeline: scroll())`). Thresholds between zones blend over 20–30% of a screen, and are travelled, not timed.
Fails when: a colour must be exact or coded (brand swatches, charts, status); the output is a screenshot, a print or a low-density display (the induced colour vanishes, or the bands alias into moiré); the page is mostly text; full-contrast inducer bands fill the viewport.
Signature (do not carry together): red, green and blue blades · a circle cutting across vertical stripes · rooms flooded with one colour of light.

**Jesús Rafael Soto.** *Hang a sparse layer of identical strokes a small gap in front of a dense one, draw no shape, and let form exist only as density, as one hue, and as the interference the viewer's own movement makes.*
Process:
1. Lay the ground: identical parallel lines at a pitch of 0.3–0.4% of the field (1.7% in the light variant), with a dark share of 50–58% (≈28% in the light variant). Confine it to a zone of 36–60% of the face. The rest is flat: a dark frame, or blank board.
2. Hang the second plane a small gap in front: 1–40 identical strokes, as thin as the ground lines, parallel to them or crossing at under 10° (a few up to 20°). Keep it sparse, so that the ground shows through everywhere.
3. Draw no outline. A body is the region where the strokes change one property (hue along a segment, finish, density), and its edge is only where that property stops.
4. Use one hue per work; everything else is white, grey or black. The hue is carried by the strokes of the body (a few % of the area) or by one flat half of the board (≈50%), never by trim.
5. Leave it still. The only motion is the viewer's: moving shifts the planes against each other, and a touch displaces the strokes, which settle back by themselves.
6. Allow two readings by distance: from far away the strokes fuse into one mass; close up the mass comes apart into strands that can be counted or entered.
7. Mode for interface use: the two-plane zone is decoration, with no text and no control on it; they sit on the flat part left by step 1.
Why it works:
- Two near-identical line systems a small offset apart beat against each other: a shift of a fraction of a pitch moves the interference by many pitches, so a tiny movement of the viewer gives a large and exactly proportional change. Thicken or multiply the front strokes and they occlude the ground and become objects before a backdrop.
- Strokes that share one property group by likeness, and the eye closes the group into a volume. An outline would make it a flat shape; several hues would make the strokes a pattern and not a body.
- Fine lines fuse into tone with distance, so the same marks are solid from afar and air up close. Presence becomes a function of how far away the viewer stands.
Colour relations: achromatic ground and frame; one hue, either ≤5% as strokes or ≈50% as one flat half; no second hue (a second emphasis is a change of finish, on one row in four); inactive strokes are a lighter neutral, not a tint of the hue.
Space and line: ground pitch 0.3–0.4%, 50–58% dark; zone 36–60% of the face and the rest flat; 1–40 front strokes at under 10°; gap about 1–3% of the width (from catalogue dimensions, not seen); no outlines; depth from interference, not from shadow.
Type requirement: monolinear, low-contrast letters in light weights, evenly tracked so that a label is a row of equal strokes; the heaviest mark on the surface is the hue field, never a word; all text on the flat part, at full reading contrast.
Motion and interaction: user-driven only. Pointer, scroll or tilt moves the front plane 2–6px (or at 0.9× of scroll); a touch parts the nearby strokes, which return on a damped spring in 800–1200ms. No autoplay. Under reduced motion the planes sit at one composed offset. A selection changes the body (which strokes carry the hue) and also a label or mark on the flat part, never the hue alone.
Fails when: text or controls must cover most of the surface (no flat part is left); several categories each need a colour (status, charts); a low-density or scaled display aliases the ground; the device gives no continuous input (no pointer, scroll or tilt).
Signature (do not carry together): lemon yellow · thin rods or tubes hanging before hairline stripes · a sphere that dissolves as you approach.

**Julio Le Parc.** *Close the set of parts and the order of colours, state one rule, and let the rule, chance and the user's hand run every variation, with no variation and no part ranked above another.*
Process:
1. Close the set: one unit (two alternating at most) on an even lattice of 7 × 7 up to about 29 × 29, and one ordered colour sequence of fixed length (14 in the works measured) at full strength, every step the same width. Nothing enters from outside the set, and no step is tinted or shaded.
2. Never show a colour alone. Colours appear as runs in sequence order (the whole run, or a mirrored one). Order the sequence so that value rises to one light step in mid-run and falls to dark at both ends: a run then reads in value as well as hue, and where it crosses itself the dark edge separates the layers.
3. Write one generating rule (advance one step per cell, rotate by a fixed angle, nest, mirror) and apply it without exception. Number the outputs as a series and show the rule.
4. Give each unit one degree of freedom and hand it to something outside the author: air, light, or the user's hand or weight. Keep everything else about the unit neutral.
5. Frame the running system: the active lattice takes 44–50% of the face, on a neutral ground lower in chroma than any step.
6. Series mode: carry one band of the full run (15–19% of a panel's side) through a row of equal panels. It enters and leaves every panel at the same point on the same axis; between the seams it takes a different path each time (ground 23–54%), and it may split lengthwise at its lightest step and rejoin. The row has no centre.
7. Make every state reversible: one action scatters the arrangement and one restores it.
Why it works:
- A closed set with a visible rule lets the viewer predict the next state, so any arrangement is a legitimate variation and none is an error. That is what makes handing over control safe. Hide the rule and the same output reads as randomness or as a trick.
- Equal steps in a fixed order give no step more area or a special place, so the eye follows the sequence and not a hero. A colour used alone becomes a signal, and hierarchy returns.
- Fixed seams carry continuity from panel to panel (good continuation), which frees everything between them to vary; the value wave keeps the band one object through its own overlaps.
Colour relations: n ordered steps, equal in area; value is a single wave across the run (lightness measured L* 22 → 88 → 33), so the steps are far from equal in contrast; hue intervals uneven; ground neutral, 20–54%; no tint, no blend between steps, no lone step; type takes no colour from the sequence.
Space and line: even lattice, 7–29 units per side; active area 44–50% of the face; band 15–19% of the side, seams at one height; curves are constant-radius arcs joined to straights; hard edges; no centre across a series (a single panel may be concentric or mirrored).
Type requirement: a fixed-pitch voice with tabular numerals for series codes and counts ("07/14"), and a flat, even-width display voice; type is monochrome; labels sit beside a run and never on it, because contrast changes at every step.
Motion and interaction: idle units drift at low amplitude on unsynchronised periods of 6–11s, but only under a named idle state (a class such as `is-idle`, set while nothing in the lattice is focused, hovered or pressed), with a visible pause control. Under reduced motion the idle state is static: each unit frozen at a fixed, staggered tilt. Sequences advance in discrete steps, one per action, with no flashing. A state is a position in the sequence plus its number, never the colour alone.
Fails when: one thing truly outranks the rest (a critical alert, a single purchase action); colour must mean something fixed (status); the items are not interchangeable; rearranging has a real cost that cannot be undone.
Signature (do not carry together): a rainbow-ordered striped band tied in loops · small mirrored squares hanging on threads in a black box · concentric rings of 14 colours.

### 4.10 POSTMODERN & NEW WAVE: irony, collision, layered depth, type as voice
The movement: against "good taste" functionalism. Personality, density and noise as message. Keep functional UI legible.
*The problem it answered:* Functionalism had made the designed thing anonymous and its reader passive: how can a surface carry a voice, an attitude and the evidence of how it was made, and still do its job? Each answer adds one kind of noise on purpose and pays for it with one thing kept blunt and still (an axis, level text, a quiet ground, a single host shape).

**Ettore Sottsass (Memphis).** *Build the thing as a standing figure: separately treated parts joined in the open, mirrored about one axis, and balanced on a base narrower than its reach.*
Process:
1. Split the content into parts by job and give each part one whole-surface treatment (a flat colour or one printed texture). A treatment changes only at a joint, never inside a part. Count: 1–12.
2. Draw one vertical axis. Put everything closed (what stores, holds or acts) on it as a trunk, 4–35% of the silhouette. Nothing closed leaves the axis.
3. Branch open members from the trunk in mirrored pairs, their outlines cut at two angles from one family (between 30° and 60°). Void stays at half or more of the bounding box (≈55%); the widest member, near mid-height, spans the full width.
4. Assign the treatments from step 1: twins across the axis match; every tier up the axis changes; no two touching parts share one. Past six treatments, split them about evenly into pale and saturated, and let one pair of the same hue at far-apart values touch (the one "wrong" meeting).
5. Stand the figure on a base 8–12% of the height and 40–55% of the full span, in the surface mode the body does not use: textured (≈15% cover) under a plain body, plain and darkest under a textured one.
6. Mark every joint with a small brace in the darkest value and leave it visible; no part merges into the next.
7. Mode for a single tool: one treatment covers ≥85%, and two small accents (≤3% each) go only on the parts the hand moves.
Why it works:
- Mirror symmetry about a vertical axis, spread wider than its footing, is what the eye reads as a body keeping its balance. Widen the base to the full span and it is a block (a container); break the mirror and it is a heap.
- One treatment per part, meeting hard at joints, segments the object by job before any label is read, and matched twins group across the axis by likeness, so twelve treatments read as pairs on a spine and not as noise. A gradient, or a colour that crosses a joint, fuses the parts and the "assembled" reading is gone.
- The only closed mass in an open frame is the odd one, and it sits where weight is expected, so the main thing can stay small. A base in the other surface mode reads as ground, not as one more part.
Colour relations: colour is the identity of a part, never its rank; touching parts always differ in hue or in mode; pale to saturated ≈1:1 by count; the darkest value only at joints, legs and base (≤10%); the smallest closed part carries the most saturated treatment; texture counts as a treatment and never lies under reading text; no gradients, no shadows; the ground behind the figure is neutral and empty.
Space and line: one vertical mirror axis, with all variety running up it; angles only on the outlines of members (text and controls inside them stay level); void ≥ half; crossbar ≈1.9× the base; no drawn outlines, since an edge is two treatments meeting.
Type requirement: letters built like the parts: a display face of one uniform stroke weight, set level on a single-treatment block; a fixed-pitch label voice (one character, one cell, ≥12px), each label on the part it names.
Motion and interaction: a state change is an assembly move, not a lift. A part travels along its own member and stops dead at its joint (120–180ms; instant under reduced motion); nothing tilts, fades or casts a shadow, and textures never move. Focus enlarges the part's joint brace and adds a ring; selection swaps the part's treatment AND changes its brace shape and label, so the selected part is the one asymmetry in the mirror. Hit areas ≥44px even on a thin member.
Fails when: the content is n equal items (a feed, a table, a long form), so nothing can be trunk or arm and per-part colour becomes a rainbow list; more than about twelve parts are in view; reading text has to sit on texture; the format is one narrow column with no room for mirrored pairs (run the trunk alone: tiers changing up the axis, over the base).
Signature (do not carry together): black squiggle laminate · pastels beside primaries in flat blocks · a totem of slanted shelves.

**April Greiman.** *Compose in depth, not on the sheet: stack planes that each keep the grain of the tool that made them, tilt the planes, and keep the words level.*
Process:
1. Start from an open ground at one extreme of value. 25–60% of the surface stays ground.
2. Sort the content by source, not by importance: captured (photo, video, scan), drawn (flat shapes), set (text), measured (data and metadata). Each source gets its own plane and keeps its own grain: one plane coarse (cells 6–10× the fine grain), the rest sharp. 4–6 planes.
3. Stack by overlap. Each plane crosses at least two others and at least one edge of the frame. Tilt the large planes 9–21°, no two at the same angle. Depth comes from overlap, size and hard offset only; nothing is blurred.
4. Keep every line that must be read at 0°, whatever plane it lies on. Text sits on a tilted plane and does not tilt with it.
5. Double the main text with a hard copy in the palest tint that is not the ground, displaced ≈1% of the width along one diagonal, the same direction everywhere. The copy is decorative and hidden from assistive technology; the main text carries the content and meets 4.5:1.
6. Load one hot flat plane, 9–13% of the area, the only fully saturated flat. It enters from an edge and carries the thing to act on.
7. Cluster density beside the hot plane, and run the measured text level along plane edges, at least 20× smaller than the largest element.
Why it works:
- Overlap is the strongest depth cue a flat surface has. With planes that are not parallel to the frame and are cut by it, the sheet stops reading as boxes on a page and reads as a volume seen through a window. Tilt without overlap is only a crooked layout.
- Grain is scale information. A coarse-celled image beside sharp type reads as a different origin and a different distance, so sources stay apart with no boxes round them (grouping by texture). Equalise the resolution and the planes collapse into one flat collage.
- Level text gives the eye one stable frame while everything else leans, so the disorder is felt and not paid for in reading. Tilt the text too and the page is simply hard to read.
Colour relations: ground 25–60% at one extreme of value; one hot flat 9–13% at peak chroma; two or three smaller flat planes at mid value; captured imagery reduced to one or two inks so it never out-saturates the hot plane; the offset tint is used only as a double, never as text colour; one deep full field (≤30%) is allowed for a single immersive panel, not as the default; reading text sits on ground or on a flat (≥4.5:1), never on the coarse grain.
Space and line: planes enter from the edges; a long format (3:1 to 3.8:1) reads as a sequence; thin leader lines in one weight may tie items across planes; a fragment of a regular dot lattice in one quadrant gives scale; scale jump ≥20×.
Type requirement: one neutral sans with a wide width range for the level headings; one fixed-pitch or coarse-celled voice for measured text, never under 12px. Smallness is a ratio: reach the 20× jump by enlarging the largest element, not by shrinking the smallest.
Motion and interaction: depth is the thing that moves. Planes shift at different rates on scroll (far ≈0.3×, near 1×; fixed under reduced motion). An image resolves from coarse cells to sharp once, in one monotone pass of 400–600ms, with no flicker. Focus widens the offset from 1% to 2% for as long as it lasts and adds a level rule; a selected item moves onto the hot plane. Controls sit level on flat planes, ≥44px.
Fails when: the content needs aligned columns (tables, forms), because overlap hides cells; reading runs long, because planes cut the measure; the screen is too small to keep 25% ground under four planes (drop to two); everything comes from one source (all text), so layers would have to be invented.
Signature (do not carry together): pixelated video stills and halftone dots · tilted wedges with airbrushed splatter · type doubled by a pale offset copy.

**David Carson.** *On a surface meant to be looked at before it is read, let one display element carry the feeling of the piece as an image, against a quiet ground and square small text (expressive surfaces only).*
Process:
1. Fix the scope first. Take only a decorative or editorial surface: a cover, an opener, a hero, a poster, an empty state, a share card. Navigation, forms, data, errors and running text are outside the procedure and keep the product's plain kit.
2. Read the piece, name its feeling in one word, and choose ONE disturbance to carry it: crop (the frame cuts the display word, losing up to a third of its letterforms), collide (the word lies on the focal area of the image), turn (one word at ≈20° or 90°) or compress (lines nearly touch, gap ≤0.1 cap height). One at full strength, a second at most, and not the one the previous piece used.
3. Quiet the field: reduce the image to one ink or bleach it until 25–60% of the surface reads as ground.
4. Set the display word at the scale of the image's subject (30–100% of the width), touching or overlapping the image. No margin between them.
5. Hold everything else still: text that must be read stays at 0°, at least 10× smaller than the display word, gathered in one or two corners, with the opposite region left empty.
6. Add one spot ink on 2–5% of the surface (one word or the title). Where pale type crosses a pale image, give it a hard copy offset ≈0.5% of the width.
7. Supply the plain duplicate: the disturbed word also exists as real text (the surface's heading for assistive technology, and a legible line in the corner cluster).
Why it works:
- A cropped or collided word is taken first as a shape and then completed by the reader (closure), and that small effort is the engagement. It works only for a short, familiar or duplicated word; on a long unfamiliar sentence closure fails and the content is lost.
- Against a quiet ground and square small text, one disturbance is the odd thing and reads as meant. Disturb everything and no single move reads as a decision: it is noise.
- Type that overlaps the image fuses with it into one figure. A margin turns them back into a picture and its caption, which is the template the reader has learned to skip. Repeating the same move on every piece does the same thing by habituation.
Colour relations: ground 25–60% at one extreme of value; the image in one ink, sharing the ground's value at its edges; display type at the other extreme of value, or reversed out; spot 2–5%; type crosses the image only at display size and at ≥3:1 where it lies (or with its offset copy); small text never lies on the image. Collage mode: two saturated inks at 15–20% each round a monochrome centre.
Space and line: no shared template between pieces, but each surface has a corner cluster, an empty counter-region and one frame edge that cuts something; turned type is display only.
Type requirement: two to four faces that differ in kind, not in family size (one compressed heavy, one high-contrast serif, one plain small face); the display face must stay recognisable with a third of its letterforms gone; the small face is plain and ≥12px.
Motion and interaction: the disturbance is composed, not animated. Moving from one piece to the next is a cut; nothing jitters, flickers or scrambles. The disturbed layer is not a control and has no hover behaviour; links and buttons on the surface come from the plain kit, level, ≥44px, outside the collision zone. Each breakpoint gets its own crop.
Fails when: it reaches navigation, forms, checkout, settings, errors, tables, dashboards or body text longer than a paragraph; the display word is long, unfamiliar or translated (a crop tuned to one string breaks in the next language); a feed repeats one disturbance on every card; the layout reflows freely and the crop is left to chance.
Signature (do not carry together): a masthead cut by the frame over a bleached photograph · distressed mixed faces and overprinted wood type · torn-paper collage.

**Paula Scher.** *Fill the frame with words at the volume they would be spoken, and hold the crowd with one blunt structure: one flat ground, one host shape and one rule about angle.*
Process:
1. Fix the ground: one flat ink from edge to edge, 38–66% of the surface. Two or three inks in all (ground, one image ink, one type ink).
2. Choose one host and set it first: a single cut-out figure printed in one ink, one giant word, or a frame of cells. It takes 35–60% of the surface and touches or is cropped by at least two edges.
3. Rank the text by loudness, not on a scale: 3–6 sizes with a gap of at least 2× between neighbours, the largest ≥6× the smallest (a display word may fill a third of the height). Inside a line, the quiet words go light and narrow and the noun goes heavy.
4. Pour the remaining text into the spaces the host leaves, or along its contour, and resize each block until it fills its hole from edge to edge. Bare ground survives only as the host's own silhouette.
5. Keep one angle rule per surface: either everything at 90° in ruled cells, or one cluster off-axis (17–30°, or quarter turns) with all else square. Box each off-axis block in a rule ≈1.3% of the width, so it reads as one object.
6. Anchor the identity along one edge, the same words and weight on every surface, and close with a level full-width band or a perimeter of cells for the facts (dates, place, price).
7. Mode for many voices: when the blocks are quotations or categories, give each its own ink (up to ≈8), so colour separates the speakers while size still ranks them.
Why it works:
- Size reads as loudness. With gaps of 2× or more there is no doubt about rank, so even a full frame has an order of entry: largest word, host, blocks, band. A gentle modular scale would turn the crowd into a murmur.
- One host gives the crowd a figure. Blocks fitted to its contour group by proximity, and the host's outline is drawn by where the type stops. Without a host, a full frame is a wall with no way in.
- Few flat inks and one angle rule leave size and weight as the only variables, so the noise runs on one channel. The level fact band is the one place where the eye is promised plain information, which is why the rest can shout.
Colour relations: ground 38–66% in one flat ink at any value (lightest, darkest or fully saturated); the image as one ink at 18–36%; the type ink at maximum value contrast to the ground (≥4.5:1 below display size); a mid-value second type ink for the quiet lines; nothing graded. Voices mode: up to ≈8 hues at similar saturation on a neutral ground, of which ≈37% stays bare.
Space and line: full bleed, no outer margin; display type meets or is cut by the edge and never shrinks to fit; one rule weight for boxed blocks (≈1.3% of the width) and hairlines for fact cells; the identity runs along an edge, upright or lying; the fact band is ≈10% of the height.
Type requirement: one heavy family with a true light weight and a real width range, so a word can be squeezed or widened to fit its hole without distortion; a plain face for the facts, level and ≥12px. Off-axis text is display size only and is repeated level in the band.
Motion and interaction: what changes when content changes is the fit. Selecting a block makes it louder (heavier and wider), its neighbours give up the width (120–180ms, stepped), and a rule closes round it, so state is never colour alone. Entrances arrive in order of loudness as hard cuts, all at once under reduced motion. Controls live in the level band or cells, ≥44px.
Fails when: the content is sparse or quiet (one sentence and one button leave nothing to pour); reading runs long; the screen is a form or a table beyond the fact band; strings are translated without a width axis or a per-language fit (each block was fitted to its hole); the format is so small that a 6× jump pushes the smallest text under 12px.
Signature (do not carry together): heavy condensed wood-type capitals · street-poster inks, saturated ground with black · tilted ruled title boxes beside a stacked logo.

### 4.11 GROWTH & FORM: the rule, not the shape
The movement: form is the trace of a rule run to completion (a count, an interval, an equilibrium, one cut, a seeded growth), so you design the rule and its boundary conditions and accept the shape it gives. Keep text off the generated field and give every generated structure a plain list equivalent.
*The problem it answered:* How do you make a layout that is ordered but not gridded, and varied but not arbitrary, when the content is a population of related things and not a fixed set of boxes? The answer here is to design a rule and its boundary conditions (a count, an interval, a set of supports, one cut, a seed and a gradient) and let the form be whatever the rule produces, so every instance differs and all are kin.

**Ernst Haeckel.** *Order by counting: build every body from one unit turned a whole number of times, double the count at each ring outward, and seat the bodies in mirrored slots round one axis.*
Process:
1. Fix one vertical axis and one gutter `g` (2–4% of the frame width). Every slot off the axis gets a mirror partner equal in size, height and value weight; the partner's content and outline may differ.
2. Seat the hero on the axis, centred or slightly high, at 45–50% of the frame width. Satellites are 1/4–1/3 of its diameter. No size exists between the tiers.
3. Pack. Bodies cover 50–73% of the frame and stop `g` from each other and from the frame; the central ninth is the densest (≥75% covered). Nothing floats in empty ground.
4. Build each body from ONE unit rotated `n` times about a centre, `n` an integer from 3 to 10. `n` is the body's kind: same kind, same `n`, whatever the unit looks like.
5. Grow outward by doubling: each ring holds twice the count of the ring inside it (4 → 8 → 16), new units interleaved between their two parents, or subdivide so that cells stay near-square (count ≈ 2πr ÷ cell width). When a unit would fall below 44px, cut the count, never the size.
6. Alternate two values of one hue family round a ring, so 2`n` sectors read as `n` pairs. Spend chroma last and inversely to area: the most saturated tone covers ≤6% (≤4% on an achromatic ground) and sits inside a body, on the part that is live, always with a shape or label.
7. Break the symmetry once at most: one long free line passing behind the bodies.
Why it works:
- Mirror symmetry about a vertical axis is detected before attention, so paired slots are read as one figure and the eye inspects half the page. That is what lets a field packed to 50–73% stay calm. Unequal partners (in size or height, not in content) turn the same density into clutter.
- A count is a category code that needs no colour, and doubling per ring holds the arc length of a unit constant as the circumference doubles. With equal wedges instead, cells thin to slivers at the core and bloat at the rim, and size starts to signal a rank that does not exist.
- One free element among strict repeats is salient by oddness. Two free elements are a second system, and the symmetry stops reading.
Colour relations: one hue family in many values does the drawing (a view is effectively monochrome); ground 40–45%, palest tint of the body hue ≈25%, near-neutrals ≈15–20%, mid chroma ≈10%, peak chroma ≤6% and enclosed; ground and figure may swap ends of the value scale with nothing else changing.
Space and line: axis first; hero 45–50% wide; tiers at 1 : 1/3; constant gutter 2–4%; cover 50–73%; hairline outline with value made by mark density, the finest detail at the margin of each body; a thin frame, with the caption apparatus outside it and centred.
Type requirement: two quiet voices on the axis (a name line and a smaller running line), strokes no heavier than the hairline drawing; labels are short keys (numbers), not prose; no text inside a patterned body.
Motion and interaction: replication, not travel. A body appears as one unit copied round its centre (40–60ms per copy); a level opens by doubling, the new units entering between the old; only the free line may drift. Reduced motion: the finished body at once.
Fails when: the items have no natural hero or cannot be paired (an odd, ranked or chronological list); the format is a narrow single column (no room for slots beside the axis); counts above ~16 per ring at touch size; long text has to live inside the bodies.
Signature (do not carry together): engraved marine organisms with stippled shading · a ruled plate with a Latin caption · curling tentacles and spines.

**Karl Blossfeldt.** *Subtract until only the repeating module is left, enlarge it to fill the frame on its axis, and let the interval between repeats, never the module, carry time.*
Process:
1. Prune. Remove every part that does not recur in every instance until one module remains, and say in the caption what was removed.
2. One subject per frame, its centroid on the frame axis (0.49–0.51 of the width), spanning 70–90% of the short side. State the enlargement factor.
3. Set a plain, untextured ground at the opposite end of the value scale from the subject (medians at least half the value range apart). The subject takes the other end and the middle; no second hue and no cast shadow.
4. Repeat the module with constant shape and constant internal count. Only the interval changes, geometrically, ×1.5–3 per step counted from the growing end; cross-size settles within three steps (about +25%, then constant).
5. Let the frame cut the growth axis: the old end runs off the edge, and the growing end (the head, the current item) stays whole inside.
6. For a radial set, place units on a spiral, each turned a fixed angle from the last (near 137.5°), with size growing 3.5–4× from core to rim, so no unit sits directly behind another.
7. Where units of like value touch or overlap, give every unit a rim one value step toward the ground's end of the scale. No gap, no shadow, no change of fill.
Why it works:
- A single thing at 70–90% on an empty ground of the opposite value leaves the eye one variation to read, which is the repetition itself, so structure becomes the figure. Shrunk to a third of the frame, the same thing reads as an object in a space and the counting stops.
- The eye judges intervals by ratio, so a geometric series reads as even growth and position reads as age. Equal spacing claims every item is the same age; changing the module along with the interval confounds two variables and neither is read.
- An edge cut across the growth axis is completed by the viewer as "this continues", while the whole head gives the place to start. Crop the head and there is no entry point; float the whole column and the sequence has ended.
Colour relations: value only. Ground one flat value at one end; subject 3–5 values of one family at the other end and the middle; rims at the extreme opposite to the units they separate; the ground inverts when the subject's value does, never mid on mid.
Space and line: one subject, axial, 70–90% of the short side; interval ratio ×1.5–3; no drawn line, since an edge is two values meeting; comparison is 2–3 equal-height panels side by side with a narrow gutter (≈2%), one module each; light even and directionless.
Type requirement: letters that read as one repeated module (even stroke, near-equal widths, upright), set as a single column on the axis; captions small, in the margin, giving name, part and factor; never on the subject.
Motion and interaction: modules arrive in order at delays that grow by ×1.3–1.6 (60, 90, 140, 210ms); zoom is the main interaction, in labelled doublings, the subject staying centred. Reduced motion: all modules at once and zoom by cut.
Fails when: the items are heterogeneous (nothing recurs, so pruning leaves nothing); several things must be compared at a glance beyond three panels; the sequence has no direction of growth; a user must reach an old item fast and the widening intervals have pushed it far off screen (cap the interval, or offer a jump).
Signature (do not carry together): magnified plant parts in monochrome photogravure · a pale grey studio ground · a plate number with a Latin name and "×25".

**Frei Otto.** *Place only the supports and say which edges are free; everything between them is found by relaxing one continuous surface to equilibrium, so every change is shared by the whole field.*
Process:
1. Fix 3–7 anchors: the elements that may not move. Give each a height (its importance); no two equal, the most important highest.
2. Declare each boundary between neighbouring anchors pinned (to the frame or to a rigid edge) or free. Nothing else is positioned by hand, now or later.
3. Relax. Treat all remaining items as nodes of one net with a single rest length `m`, and solve for equilibrium (springs or force density, iterated until the largest move is under 0.5px). The solved positions are the layout.
4. Every free edge sags toward the interior as a concave arc, sag ≈ span ÷ 10 (1/12–1/8). Never straight, never convex. More load on an edge means more sag.
5. Alternate high and low: between two raised anchors put one pulled-down point, so each bay curves up one way and down the other. No bay is flat and no node lies more than one bay from a support.
6. Keep one mesh size `m` across the whole field. Where the surface curves the cells shear into rhombi; they never scale. Show the mesh as hairlines.
7. Cut one opening only, at the highest anchor. It is the lightest point of the field and holds the one live view.
Why it works:
- A boundary that curves inward between two points is what a pulled sheet does, so the eye reads the corners as holding the region. Anchors gain weight without gaining size, and regions need no border. A straight edge reads as a box that was drawn; a convex one as a blob that was inflated.
- A mesh of constant size is a scale reference: shear reads as slope, as any regular texture does on a curved surface. If the cells resize instead, size reads as rank and the slope is lost.
- When a change is spread over every node, each moves a little and keeps its neighbours, so the viewer's map of the screen survives. One box jumping a long way destroys the map and draws the eye to the wrong thing.
Colour relations: the continuous surface is ONE value of one neutral or one material hue, unchanged across bays (near-neutrals ≈90% of the view); supports are darker and thinner than the field; the opening is the lightest point; all chroma (≤10%) belongs to content seen through or under the surface, never to the structure.
Space and line: 3–7 supports and large spans; bays run into each other with no seams or gutters, told apart by rise and fall (a value gradient); only two kinds of line, straight thin supports and tensioned curves; mesh hairlines at 6–10% contrast.
Type requirement: one light, even weight with open counters and a wide rhythm; reading text sits only where the field is calm (near anchors, low curvature), on plain surface and never across the mesh at more than 10% contrast or along a steep bay.
Motion and interaction: relaxation. Any change re-solves the net and every node travels to the new equilibrium, critically damped, 300–500ms, no overshoot; moving one anchor shifts neighbours by an amount that decays with distance; a pressed anchor rises and the surface follows. Supports stay thin with ≥44px invisible hit areas. Reduced motion: cut to the new state with a short cross-fade.
Fails when: content must align in rows and columns to be compared (tables, forms, code); there are dozens of equal anchors (no spans left, the field becomes a quilt); long text has to follow a curved boundary; the layout must be identical for every user and data set (the solver gives a different answer per content).
Signature (do not carry together): peaked tent roofs on masts · translucent panels on a cable net · soap-film models on wire frames.

**Barbara Hepworth.** *Make one closed convex whole, then open it once, off-centre; the opening, finished as the inside of the same surface, becomes the subject.*
Process:
1. One mass per view: a closed, convex, smooth outline with no tab, notch or protrusion, the dominant shape on a plain ground. Seat it on one strict rectilinear base, which holds the only corners.
2. Cut ONE opening into the mass (two at most, and then joined as one passage). Choose its size by its job: about 1/7 of the mass's width for a hollow that only marks a place, up to 2/3 for a window that holds the content. The mass keeps at least 40% of its own area.
3. Displace the opening from the centre of the mass: a wide one by about 1/10 of the radius, a small one out to 0.6–0.75 across and 0.25–0.45 down. The surrounding wall then varies continuously between 0.05 and 0.15 of the diameter. The thinnest lip is the way in, and the control goes there.
4. Finish the inside as the same surface in a second state: value stepped 30–40 L away from the mass and chroma cut to a quarter or less, with no line at the lip. In a one-value system, model the hollow by shading on one side only (10–20 L).
5. Whatever enters the view from outside the mass's own palette (one accent, the live content) goes inside the opening, nowhere else.
6. If the opening is wide, span it lip to lip with 3–6 thin straight lines that show a relation between the two sides. They are the only straight lines on the mass.
7. Nest at most twice: each inner opening is about 1/4 of the one that holds it (2/3 → 1/6).
Why it works:
- The edge of a hole belongs to the shape around it, so what shows through is framed by the object and not placed on it. A badge on a card is a second object competing with the first; a hole is the same object pointing at its own content.
- A centred hole makes a ring: symmetrical, static, read as a symbol. Off-centre, the wall has a thick side and a thin side, which gives the mass a direction and the eye an entry at the lip.
- A convex outline has no feature of its own, so the only event on the mass is the opening. One protrusion adds a second focus on the outline and the opening loses.
Colour relations: outside the mass is one family, 2 values (lit and shadowed), the larger share (≈40% of the object) and the higher chroma; inside is the quieter state, about 15% of the view, opposite in temperature; one added colour at most, always inside; base darker than or equal to the mass.
Space and line: one mass; opening 1/7–2/3 of its width; offset ≈0.1 R, or toward an upper outer third; wall 0.05–0.15 of the diameter; no line anywhere on the mass except the 3–6 spanning ones; curved mass over a rectilinear base.
Type requirement: soft-shouldered letters with open counters, one weight per view, set as a compact block with a quiet closed outline on the solid part of the mass or on the base; never in the opening's rim zone and never across the spanning lines.
Motion and interaction: the opening dilates in place from the hollow size to the window size (400–600ms, ease-out); pointer, scroll or tilt turns the view a few degrees so more of the inside shows; nothing slides or bounces. The lip is the target: ≥44px wide, with a focus ring that follows its curve. Reduced motion: cross-fade to the open state.
Fails when: the view needs several peers (a grid of pierced cards is a tray of rings, and the single event is gone); the content is wider than 2/3 of any convex container (tables, long text); more than two levels of disclosure; a dense toolbar must sit on the mass.
Signature (do not carry together): carved wood or stone with a pale painted hollow · taut strings across the opening · an ovoid on a plinth.

**Nervous System.** *Design the rule and not the object: a seed, an environment and one gradient, stepped locally until the space is full, and shown as a family of outcomes.*
Process:
1. Declare three inputs and nothing else. A seed (start points, taken from the item's ID). An environment (the boundary, the targets to reach, and keep-out zones for text and controls). ONE gradient: the direction along which growth runs faster or density rises.
2. Grow in small local steps, each 1–2% of the width, toward the nearest unreached targets. A branch stops one step short of the boundary, a keep-out zone or another branch. Nothing crosses.
3. Where two branches meet, merge them, so the result has closed loops and every region is reachable by two routes. Halve the width at each branching level (4 : 2 : 1).
4. Stop on coverage, not on time: structure fills 16–21% of its area (voids about a quarter in the tiled mode), and density at the far end of the gradient is about 25% above the near end.
5. Grade one secondary variable along the same gradient and no other: opening size from small to large, unit size from large to small, or a second wave at 1/3–1/5 of the first wavelength and 1/3 of its amplitude.
6. Set the exceptions inside the field: about 3–5% of the units take a recognisable outline plus a label, each wholly surrounded by ordinary units.
7. Run the rule on 4–8 seeds and show the outcomes side by side, each with its seed printed. Publish the parameters.
Why it works:
- A local rule with collision produces gaps that are even without being regular. The field fuses into one tone at a distance, as a texture, yet no region repeats, so the eye finds neither a grid nor noise. Hand placement cannot hold the gaps even over hundreds of units.
- The gradient is the only source of hierarchy. Growth at the same rate everywhere wrinkles without order (the studio's own control case); a faster edge gives large lobes, then lobes on lobes, and a direction to read in with no hero.
- Several kin side by side let the viewer subtract what varies and see what holds (density, hierarchy of widths), so the identity is carried by the rule. A single output reads as an illustration somebody drew.
Colour relations: the grown structure is ONE value; the ground is the opposite end of the value scale; emphasis comes from density, not hue; if colour is needed it is one ordered ramp mapped to growth age or depth (never categories), with the same information in a label or in width.
Space and line: fill by growth to a coverage target; lines taper from trunk to tip, branch, merge and never cross; widths halve per level; density up 25% along the gradient; exceptions 3–5% and embedded; families of 4–8.
Type requirement: a neutral voice that stays out of the structure's way, plus a fixed-width voice for seeds and parameters; all text on plain ground in keep-out zones declared in step 1, never on the grown field; an indented list of the same structure for keyboard and screen-reader users.
Motion and interaction: growth is the animation and it runs once. The structure extends from its seed in the rule's own steps over 1–3s and stops at the stable state; new content extends from its parent along a path (300–600ms); "regrow" takes a new seed. Never loop growth behind text. Reduced motion: the final state at once, with a replay control.
Fails when: there are too few items to make a texture (under ~30 units the result is a doodle); exact position must carry meaning (rank, time, geography); the content is one fixed object with no family; users must find the same item in the same place on every visit (pin the seed, or do not use it for navigation).
Signature (do not carry together): white 3D-printed vein and coral forms · ruffled laser-cut or printed surfaces · a grid of numbered variants on black.
