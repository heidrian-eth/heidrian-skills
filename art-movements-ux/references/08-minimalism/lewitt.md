# Sol LeWitt — Minimalism / Conceptual

## Essence
LeWitt treated art as a specification rather than an artifact: he wrote the rule, and other people or circumstances produced the output. A deliberately dull unit (a line, a square, an open cube) is run through a system until every option is used up, and the beauty comes from that complete coverage, not from taste. He is art history's parametric component library.

## Key works examined
- **Wall Drawing 47 (1970)**: a wall split into fifteen equal parts, each with a different line direction. A grid of cells, each holding one variant of a single parameter. *(not obtained; its colour sibling Wall Drawing #49, 1970, was checked as the Tate diagram + certificate: fifteen equal vertical cells, four line directions in black, yellow, red and blue pencil and all their combinations, each cell labelled 1, 2, 3, 4, 12, 13, 14, 23, 24, 34, 123, 124, 134, 234, 1234, i.e. sorted by number of elements. The certificate prints the instruction in typewriter type beside the diagram)*
- **Wall Drawing #63, 1971 (AIC)**: four horizontal tiers of pencil line combinations (four singles, six pairs, four triples, one all-four). From across the room the drawing reads as four grey tones that darken downward (L* ≈81 → 75) inside a white margin of ≈5% of the wall. *(visually checked: AIC image)*
- **Wall Drawing 46**: "vertical lines, not straight, not touching, covering the wall evenly." The drafter supplies the jitter. *(not obtained)*
- **Serial Project #1 (ABCD), 1966 / Open Modular Cubes**: white open lattices built on a fixed ratio of beam to space (roughly 1:8.5). The module is the grammar. *(MoMA work not obtained. Checked: AIC Nine-part Modular Cube 1977, 9×9 front cells, beam 7 px on a 61 px pitch, beam:opening ≈1:7.7 (±1 px gives 1:6.7–1:9); Tate Two Open Modular Cubes/Half-Off 1972, far chunkier at ≈1:4.5 in perspective; AIC ABCD 5, 1971: four units on a white-ruled dark base grid, the four combinations of open/closed outer box × open/closed inner box)*
- **Incomplete Open Cubes, 1974**: 122 cube frames, each with 3–11 edges, always three-dimensional and connected, with no rotational duplicates. Shown on a matrix beside diagrams and photos: a complete enumeration. *(visually checked: secondary source, socks-studio.com: the 'Variations of Incomplete Open Cubes' chart, a 1974 installation photo, the book, and one drawing)* Seen: the chart is a ruled grid, one small isometric drawing per cell, read left to right in rows grouped by edge count: the first row holds 3 three-edge forms, the rows swell toward the middle counts and thin out, ending with a single cell; every cell is numbered. In the installation, white frame sculptures stand one per square cell of a white floor grid, in regimented rows, with the matching drawings and black-ground photos hung in rows on the walls. Each variant appears three times (object, drawing, photo) at three scales.
- **"Paragraphs on Conceptual Art," 1967**: "The idea becomes a machine that makes the art." All decisions are made beforehand, and execution is perfunctory.
- **Wall Drawing 1136 / 1144 "Broken Bands of Color in Four Directions" (2000s)** and **#729 Irregular Color Bands (1993)**: the same systematic logic in flat, saturated acrylic bands that meet edge to edge. *(#1136 seen only as a 133 px Tate thumbnail: a wave of parallel bands in red, yellow, green, blue and purple crossing a field of vertical stripes, wall-to-wall with no white gaps; #729 and #1144 not obtained)*
- **Wall Drawing #574, 1988 (AIC)**: fifteen equal arcs from the midpoint of the left side, in all combinations of four ink washes. Single washes: grey (dark, `#444143`), yellow (`#B7A11B`), red that dries to dusty rose (`#9D6261`), blue (`#274886`); overlaps give ochre-orange, olive, brown and violet bands. *(visually checked: AIC image, which looks like a printed reproduction; colours approximate)*
- **Six Geometric Figures (+ Two), 1980–1 (Tate)**: white crayon lines on black walls; the figures are filled with horizontal lines, the wall around them with vertical lines, line weight ≈1/9 of the pitch. Figure and ground differ only in line direction. *(visually checked: Tate image)*
- **Wall Drawing 1152 "Whirls and twirls" (2005)**: late curvilinear bands. Playful, still flat and unmodeled. *(not obtained)*

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Space** (Incomplete Open Cubes): one cell per variant on a visible grid; row order = sorting key (edge count); row lengths vary with how many variants each count yields, so the grid's ragged right edge is itself the data.
- **Colour relations:** white objects and lines on a neutral or black ground; no colour; the same information repeated in three media (object, line drawing, photo).
- **Line and edge:** even, thin lines in the drawings; the floor grid uses the same line as the frames.
- **The procedure:** enumerate every valid variant under a stated rule; sort by one visible key; give each variant an identical cell; show the set in full, and repeat it in more than one medium.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Sol LeWitt.** *State a rule, run it to exhaustion, and show every result in an identical cell in sorted order: completeness replaces choice.*
Process:
1. Write the rule as one sentence before drawing anything: the unit, the variables, the constraint. The sentence is displayed with the result, in plain small text.
2. Choose a deliberately plain unit and one line weight for everything (frame to opening between 1:4.5 and 1:8), so that only the variable can be looked at.
3. Enumerate every valid result of the rule. No curating, no top three, no duplicates. If a result is valid it is shown.
4. Give each result an identical cell on a visible lattice, sorted by one stated key (how many parts it combines), and a code built from that key and its index. Let each row be as long as its count: the ragged edge is information.
5. Derive every visual property from the index, never per item: singles take one of a small fixed set (four); a combination is its singles overlaid, so its colour is their mixture, each added part makes it one tone darker, and figure differs from ground by direction alone.
6. Size the lattice so the whole set fits one view. Where it matters, state the same set a second time in another representation, in the same order.
Why it works:
- Completeness can be checked. A viewer who sees every case, in order, trusts that nothing was hidden, and an empty cell becomes a fact. A curated subset asks to be trusted and invites the question of what was left out.
- With unit, cell and line identical, grouping by likeness is total and the single varying property is all that remains to see; small differences become obvious. A decorated or varied unit makes the differences unreadable.
- Derived colour and tone can be decoded: a mixture points back to its parts, and more parts means darker, so the sort key appears as a density gradient and gives order without any element being larger.
Colour relations: neutral ground and one line colour at maximum value contrast carry the structure; at most four index colours of similar weight, none ranked; every further colour is an overlap of those, never a new swatch; tone steps of about 2 L per added part at overview; no accent, no signal colour.
Space and line: equal cells on a visible lattice; one line weight; frame to opening 1:4.5–1:8; margin about 5%; rows of unequal length allowed; no hero, no merged cells; each cell at least 44px.
Type requirement: mechanically regular with tabular figures, one weight per role; a code in the same corner of every cell; the rule sentence set small and plain. The code, not the colour or the direction, is what identifies a cell.
Motion and interaction: the system is seen running: cells appear in sorted order at a constant interval (30–40ms, linear), lines draw at one speed; changing a parameter regenerates the whole set in order; a toggle shows the rule behind any view. Reduced motion: the set appears complete.
Fails when: the valid set is unbounded or too large for one view (beyond roughly 150 cells), or has one member; the user wants a recommendation, not the catalogue (a checkout, an emergency action); the content is narrative and has no variable to enumerate.
Signature (do not carry together): white open-cube lattices · pencil lines in four directions on a wall · a typed instruction certificate.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

LeWitt's procedure → a cocktail-bar menu in dark amber with photographs (phone, 390 × 844).
1. *Rule.* Printed at the top in 12px plain text: "Every drink that can be made from our four house infusions. 15 in all."
2. *Unit.* One glass photographed identically for every drink: same glass, angle and light, on a dark amber ground. A 1px cream line draws the lattice; cell 110px with a 14px frame band (1:7.9).
3. *Enumerate.* 4 singles, 6 pairs, 4 triples, 1 with all four. No "signature cocktails" section, no bartender's pick.
4. *Sort and code.* Three columns, rows grouped by number of infusions: rows of 3 + 1, 3 + 3, 3 + 1, 1, leaving empty cells at the row ends. Codes in the lower right of each cell: 1, 2, 3, 4, 12, 13 … 1234, with the drink's name beside the code.
5. *Derive.* Each infusion has one translucent tint over the photograph (four of similar weight); a pair shows both tints overlaid, so its colour is their mixture; each added infusion lowers the cell 2 L, and the page darkens downward from light singles to the one dense cell.
6. *Fit and repeat.* All 15 cells and the rule fit one 844px screen without scrolling (7 rows × 110px = 770px). Tapping the rule line restates the set as a text list in the same order with prices.
- *Type.* A tabular monospace at 12px for codes and prices, one weight; names in the same face at 14px.
- *Motion.* Cells appear in code order, 35ms apart. Removing an infusion (allergy filter) regenerates the set to 7 cells in order.
Result: a warm, photographic, dim menu. No white lattice, no pencil, no primary colours. What survived: the printed rule, the full enumeration, identical cells sorted by part count, and colour that is derived instead of chosen.

## Space & composition
- **Divide, then fill.** Every surface is first cut into equal cells (2×2, 3×5, 4×4), and each cell gets one value of one variable: line direction, color, or which edges are missing.
- **Show the whole set.** In *Incomplete Open Cubes* the 122 units sit in regimented rows, sorted by edge count (3 edges at one end, 11 at the other). The ordering key is visible, and density increases along one axis.
- **Fixed ratio module.** The open cubes keep beam to void at about 1:8.5 (measured ≈1:7.7 on the AIC Nine-part Modular Cube), so the structure is mostly air and the frame reads as a drawing in space. The ratio is a parameter, not a brand, and it is not universal: the 1972 Two Open Modular Cubes run ≈1:4.5.
- **Figure by direction only.** In Six Geometric Figures a shape is distinguished from its ground only by turning the hatching 90°; in Wall Drawing #63 adding line directions turns each tier one tone darker. Density and direction, not outlines, make the hierarchy.
- **Coverage over focal point.** "Covering the wall evenly" means no hero. Visual weight is distributed uniformly, and hierarchy comes from sequence, not size.
- **Scale for comprehension.** Size the grid so the whole system fits in one viewport.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

The early work uses graphite and black on white, and the logic is in the line; from around 1980 the same lines also appear as white on black walls. Later the palette moves to LeWitt's basic set of red, yellow, blue, and grey (in the #49 diagram: black, yellow, red, blue pencil). In ink wash these are muted, not primary: the grey is dark, the red dusty rose, the yellow ochre, and the overlaps turn brown, olive and violet (Wall Drawing #574). The late acrylics push well past four colours into green, purple and orange (#1136). These were first layered as translucent ink washes, so overlaps mix, and later laid as opaque, flat acrylic bands that meet edge to edge. Color is assigned by rule (band n gets color n mod 4), never chosen per element.

## Form, line & typography
LeWitt's line is uniform, unmodulated, and drawn to a ruler: one stroke weight across the whole wall. Shapes come only from a basic geometric vocabulary (square, circle, triangle, trapezoid, parallelogram, isometric cube). Typographically that calls for a monoline, mechanically regular face with tabular figures, because every element is labelled with a code like the cube variants ("7/29").
- **IBM Plex Mono**: instructions, captions, variant codes.
- **Space Grotesk** or **Archivo**: headings in one weight, set like stencilled labels.
One weight per role, never italic.

## Rhythm, motion & time
Time belongs to execution (a team drawing 10,000 lines). Motion should show the system running.
- Reveal a grid cell by cell in reading order with a constant 30–40ms stagger and linear easing. A machine has no ease-out.
- Draw lines with `stroke-dashoffset` at the same speed for every line, so long lines take proportionally longer.
- Changing a parameter regenerates the whole set, and every cell updates in sequence, so the user sees the rule apply to everything.

## UI translations
1. **Write the screen as a rule first.** Define the layout as a written spec ("3×4 cells, each showing one variant, sorted by X") and render the spec text in small mono at the top or bottom of the view.
2. **Enumerate, don't curate.** For any option space (themes, plans, filter combos), show every valid combination in a matrix rather than three "featured" picks. Exclude only true duplicates.
3. **Sort along one visible axis.** Order the matrix by a single increasing variable (edge count, price, density), and label row headers with the value.
4. **Make the base component deliberately boring.** It should be a bordered square with one label, no shadow, no radius. All interest comes from how instances vary.
5. **Use 1px rules on 8–9× cell spacing** (a 1:8.5 frame-to-void ratio, measured ≈1:7.7 on the Nine-part Modular Cube). For a heavier variant use 1:4.5 (Two Open Modular Cubes).
6. **Assign color by index.** `nth-child(4n+1..4)` maps to red/yellow/blue/grey. No per-component color picking.
7. **Do overlaps with multiply washes.** Selected or hovered states lay a translucent primary over the cell, and multi-select overlaps mix into new hues.
8. **Seed the variation.** Generated art, avatars, and empty states take a deterministic seed (user ID) and a rule like "lines not straight, not touching."
9. **Label every instance with its code** ("4/12", "B-3") in tabular mono, as a catalogue number. For combinations, use the component digits themselves ("1", "12", "123", "1234"), ordered by how many parts they combine, as on the #49 diagram.
10. **Give an "instructions" toggle.** Let users flip any visualization to see the rule that produced it.
11. **For bold modes, go flat and edge to edge.** Full-bleed bands of saturated primary color with no gutter, no gradient, and no shadow. Use crisp boundaries only.

## What this artist would reject
- One-off "hero" layouts with no generating rule behind them.
- Gradients, glassmorphism, drop shadows, and texture: surface effects that distract from the idea.
- Curated "top 3" choices that hide the rest of the option space.
- Ease-in-out bounce and spring physics on systematic reveals.
- Per-component color decisions made for "vibe" instead of from a mapping.

## Signature prompt seed
Design the screen as an executed instruction, not a composition. Start from a deliberately plain module, a hairline-bordered square with a mono catalogue code, and tile it in an equal grid that shows every valid permutation of one variable, sorted along a single labelled axis. Keep frames thin and cells airy (about 1:8.5). Assign color strictly by index from red, yellow, blue, and grey, flat or as multiply washes where states overlap. Reveal cells in linear sequence. Print the generating rule in small IBM Plex Mono beneath the grid.
