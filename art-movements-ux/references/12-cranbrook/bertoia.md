# Harry Bertoia — Cranbrook / organic modernism

## Essence
Bertoia builds a whole out of many identical thin units (wire, rod, small plate), joined at points and spaced so that roughly half of what you look at is air. The unit never changes much; what changes is where units bunch and where they thin out, which is how a flat field turns into a bowl, a cascade, a seed head or a chord. Light, wind, a body or a hand finishes the work: the screens glint, the chairs carry a sitter, the rods sound when touched.

## Detailed observations

**Manufacturers Trust screen, 1954.** The catalogue dimensions give a long band, 192 × 840 in: width is 4.4 times height, and only 24 in deep. In Stoller's frontal 1954 view the screen runs behind the tellers' counter from column to column and fills the full height between counter and the luminous ceiling; it reads as a textured wall, not an object. A row profile of that view shows about five horizontal seams (dips every ≈20 px over ≈102 px of height), so tiers of roughly 3 ft, and about 53 vertical seams over 380 px, which scales to roughly 70–75 plate columns over 70 ft, a pitch near 1 ft. The close-up (Sullivan) shows the unit: a tall narrow plate, height about 3.5–4 times its width, hammered and pitted, its edges slightly ragged, crossed by melt-coat beads and blotches. Plates hang in two planes: some sit in front of the rod lattice, some behind, and the lattice itself is a thin rod web of irregular polygons (hexagons and diamonds) that is visible only where a plate is missing. Plates in adjacent columns are offset vertically, so no horizontal edge lines up across more than two or three plates; the tiers are felt, not drawn. Here and there a plate is replaced by a different shape: a lens or leaf form with a split down its middle, a disc on a stalk, a triangle. Colour in the close-up: brass gold `#A1884F` (L 58, C 34) 26%, shadowed brass `#766037` (L 42) 25%, dark bronze `#4D3D24` (L 27) 22%, the near-black gaps between plates `#1C1814` (L 10) 15%, and the white ceiling seen through `#EAEEEE` (L 91) 6%: about 72% metal, 28% gap at this distance. In the 2016 view metal is `#4F4229`/`#32291B` (L 16–29, 85%) under a pale ceiling `#C2C8C7` (L 79). Absent: any figure, any frame, any single focal point; the screen has no centre and no edge treatment.

**Altarpiece, MIT Chapel, 1955.** Seen frontally (Harris photo), the oculus is a pale ellipse ≈450 px wide; the field of wires hangs from its rim and is ≈406 px wide, so the work is about 0.9 of the light opening it hangs from. The marble altar block below is ≈221 px wide, 0.54 of the field. I count 24–25 suspension wires across the front, at a pitch of 18–19 px in the middle and 13–14 px at the sides, which is what equal spacing on a curved plan looks like head-on (the List calls it curved; Knoll gives 20 wires). The plates are small horizontal slips, median ≈16 × 4 px in the photo (≈1/25 of the field width, roughly 5 in), each tilted at its own angle, so only some catch the oculus light at any moment. Counting lit plates in six equal height bands from top to bottom: 22, 35, 63, 87, 93, 102, a 4.6-fold increase, matching the List's account that the plates are sparser at the top and concentrated at human height. A bright-pixel share per band rises the same way, from 1.9% to ≈9%. Colour is almost entirely value: the dark brick and shadow `#0D0A08`–`#221B15` (L 3–11) take 77% of the frame; the lit plates and wires `#CCC6BF`–`#F3F0E9` (L 81–93, C ≤ 4) are 3–5%; the bronze patina reads warm only in the ArchDaily photo, where the brick is `#532F19` (L 24, C 25) and the plates `#E4EFEA` almost white. Nothing touches the floor: the plates stop above the altar's top edge, and the wires run unbroken from rim to bottom. Absent: any figurative sign, any frame, any base.

**Diamond chair, 1952.** The Met example measures 111.4 cm wide by 70.5 cm high: wider than tall by 1.58. Seen from above (MNAM), the seat is a single sheet of square-mesh wire whose outline is a rhombus with heavily rounded corners: two straight upper edges meet at the back in a rounded point; the side corners are rounded more tightly. The grid lines run parallel to the two back edges, so the grid is turned 45° to the sitter, and about 29–30 wires meet each upper edge. Cells are close to square at the rim and become rhombs and trapezoids where the sheet is pulled down into the seat bowl; the distortion is the shape. The rim is a heavier rod, each grid wire welded to it. Under the bowl a separate black rod frame (a horizontal ring, two struts) carries the shell on four rubber shock mounts, and the base is two V-shaped hairpin rods ending in floor sledges. The shadow on the white plinth draws the grid a second time, larger and sharper than the chair. In the MNAM photo the black wire `#1E1D1F` (L 13) is only 3.9% of the frame; everything else is the grey-white plinth and wall (L 40–88, C ≤ 5): the chair is almost entirely the air inside its outline. The AIC example is white wire with a yellow vinyl seat pad `#E3C844` (L 79, C 65, 9% of the frame), the pad's outline following the bowl's front lip, a solid patch laid inside the open grid. Absent: any solid surface on the bare version, any visible joint hardware on the shell's face.

**Bird chair, 1952.** The same welded mesh, but the shell continues from a rolled front lip down into a deep seat and up into a tall back that widens toward the top and flares sideways, like a raised wing. Knoll lists it at 101 cm high on a 98 × 89 cm footprint, seat height 38 cm: the back rises about 2.7 times the seat height. The upholstered cover follows the mesh exactly, so shell and cover share one silhouette; under it the white mesh shows its cells bending around the bowl. The base is again a separate sled of bent white rod with two struts, and the ottoman repeats the seat's outline on its own rod sled. Colour: white rod and wall `#E1E1DD` (L 89) 35%; orange cover `#D97204` (L 60, C 73) only 8% of the frame, the single saturated area.

**Wire-form chair study, c. 1950.** A single shell form photographed on the studio floor: two curved rim rods, one sweeping up into a pointed tip at the right, one rolling over at the left, with dozens of straight wires stretched between them (a vertical line through the right lobe crosses ≈50–70 wires, both layers). No wire is bent; the surface curves because successive straight wires change angle. Where front and back layers overlap they make moiré bands. The form touches the floor at one small foot.

**Textured Screen, Dallas, 1955.** A long rectangular frame of black steel, about 3.5 times as wide as it is high in the photo (≈870 × 250 px of the frame), hung from the ceiling on two rods. Inside, plates hang on vertical rods in about three loose tiers: mostly tall rectangles like the New York screen, but here many are tilted, and among them are triangles, small discs on stalks, diamond shapes with a cut-out, a few crescent and lens forms. The plates are rotated to different angles around their rods, so some show their face, some their edge. Coverage is lower than in New York: through the gaps you see ceiling lights, a dark window, the wall. Colour: copper and brass `#835C37`, `#AE8556`, `#B0753C` (L 42–60, C 30–45) together ≈55%, dark bronze `#5A4632` 22%, black gaps `#0D0B0D` 12%. The black frame is the only straight continuous line in the work.

**Multiplane panel (free-standing screen).** A rectangle of brass-coated plates, ≈475 × 705 px, raised on four thin rod legs with small square feet. Seven tiers of plates, ten to eleven plates per tier. Plates are tall rectangles with a median height/width of 2.6, hammered, with uneven edges. The rod lattice behind is a zigzag in plan (rods triangulate between front and back planes), so plates sit at slightly different depths and angles. Consecutive tiers are offset by about half a plate. Here and there a small square tab replaces a plate. Gold covers ≈48% of the body's bounding box: half metal, half air. Colours: `#BFA665` (L 69, C 37) 19%, `#A88C50` (L 59) 13%, `#D2BE84` (L 78) 11%, a darker `#866C42` (L 46) 5% in the creases.

**Sonambient sound sculptures.** The rod wall is about 110 rods side by side, read along three heights; pitch is ≈6.4 px with a ±1–2 px wobble, and rods fill 40–45% of the field: rod and gap are about equal. The whole is nearly square (w/h ≈ 0.92) and stands on a thin flat base bar. The top is not quite level: a slight step at mid-width shows it is two units butted together, and individual rods end a few pixels higher or lower. Colour: `#A19983`–`#8F866D` (L 56–63, C 12–15) brass against `#DFDEDD` wall; light passes through as fine vertical stripes. The capped-rod tonal is a square grid of rods (about 8 × 8 seen from the corner) topped by short cylinders ≈3.7 times the rod diameter, the caps' tops at slightly different heights, a ragged plateau; brass `#7F6851` (L 46, C 17) and `#AF9168` (L 62, C 28). In the barn, rod clusters of many heights stand in rows, a skyline of verticals with gongs hung among them; in the dark photo (`#040201` 51%, warm red-brown `#732A11`–`#A45530` L 27–46, C 43–47) the rods are silhouettes against a lit wall. The foundation says the rods are loose at the top and the sound is not known until the piece is assembled; Bertoia says he goes "beyond the visible portion... to the sounds". Absent: any bracing at the top, anything that stops the rods moving.

**Dandelion.** Hundreds of identical straight rods radiate from a small welded core, forming a disc-like sphere whose outline is soft because rod ends stop at slightly different radii. Head diameter ≈355 px against total height ≈729 px (0.49; the Whitney piece is 39 in wide by 75 in high, 0.52). The single stem below the head is ≈0.9 of the head diameter; the black stone base is ≈0.29 of the head diameter wide. Rods are densest and darkest at the core and thin toward the edge, where the white wall shows through: `#8D6C37` (L 47, C 35) in the core, `#BAAA92` and `#D2CBC1` toward the rim, then wall `#F3F3F3`. One heavy dark block at the bottom, everything else filament.

**Willow.** A bell of fine wires, ≈486 px wide by 676 px high (w/h 0.72), with a rounded crown that closes over the top and a hem that stays open just above the floor; a small flat plate base is visible through the wires. The wires fall almost straight; the outline is made by where they bend at the crown. Density varies across the width: darker bands where more wires overlap, light vertical streaks where few do. Colour is one metal in four values, `#716758` to `#C2BBAE` (L 44–77, C 8–10), against `#EFEEEA`.

**Monotypes, 1940s.** Three colour monotypes on the foundation page. (a) Dark field: a near-black blue-green ground `#12161C`–`#252D2D` (L 7–18, ≈35%) with grey-green mid-tones `#484F40`–`#666F5C` (L 32–46, ≈39%), three soft black biomorphic blots with no hard edge, a pale blue-grey floor zone `#ABBDB1` (L 75, 14%), a salmon disc and fan `#F09E71` (L 70, C 42, 4.3%) and a pale peach corner `#F8D9B6` (2.8%). Over the blots run about a dozen fans of very fine orange-ochre lines, each fan radiating from a point, plus translucent olive triangles `#6F683A` (L 44, C 26, 5.5%). (b) Triangles: tall overlapping translucent triangles in olive and khaki `#80795B`–`#CDC6AA` (L 51–80, C 14–18), with one dark triangle and a few ochre `#BEA05C` (7%) and rust `#99633A` (2.4%) shards. (c) Warm drift: leaf- and lens-shaped blots in orange `#CE8C28` (L 63, C 60, 8%) and vermilion `#C05A25` (L 50, C 58, 6%) scattered in an arc over a cream ground `#DAC192` (51%), crossed by short dry hatch strokes. In all three, colour comes as a translucent stain and line comes as scratched hairlines; the two layers are independent. Bertoia described the drawing process as pressing paper on an inked plate and working blind from the back, "a monotype that's done in reverse".

**Jewelry.** A pendant: one convex hammered silver plate, shield- or pebble-shaped, with a single round hole near the top and a slit running from the hole to the bottom edge; `#A99F91`–`#BCB6AB` (L 66–75, C 6–9). A necklace of eight to ten small rounded square plates linked by short wire loops, each plate slightly cupped and turned. The plate as a unit, and the linking of many plates by short rods, are already here.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **One unit, multiplied** (all works). Each piece is built from one element repeated tens to hundreds of times: ≈30 wires per rim edge (Diamond chair), ≈110 rods (rod wall), ≈400+ plates (MIT), ≈70–75 columns × 5 tiers (New York), 7 × 10–11 plates (multiplane panel), hundreds of spokes (dandelion). The unit varies by a little (angle, length, a ragged edge), never by kind, except for a few odd shapes in the screens.
- **About half air** (multiplane panel, rod wall, Diamond chair, New York close-up). Measured solid share of the body: 48% (multiplane panel), 40–45% (rod wall), 72% at close range in New York; the bare Diamond chair's wire is under 4% of the frame. Gaps are about the size of the unit. Bertoia: chairs are "mainly made of air, like sculpture" (Knoll).
- **Density as the drawing** (MIT, dandelion, willow, Diamond chair). Form is shown by where units crowd and where they thin, not by an outline: MIT's lit plates go 22 → 102 per band from top to altar (4.6×); the dandelion is dark at the core and see-through at the rim; the willow is banded by overlap; the Diamond chair's square cells distort into rhombs in the bowl.
- **Straight units make the curve** (wire-form study, Diamond chair, Bird chair, willow, MIT). Curves are produced by many straight pieces changing angle (ruled surface), or by a flat grid pulled into a bowl, not by bending a solid. MIT's straight wires on a curved plan read as tighter spacing at the sides (13–14 vs 18–19 px).
- **Joints are points, not seams** (chairs, screens, dandelion). Units meet at welds, brazed beads or a single core; there are no panels with continuous edges, no hinges or overlaps. The heavier element is reserved for the rim (Diamond chair rim rod) or the root (dandelion stem and core, rod-wall base bar).
- **Support separated and minimal** (Diamond, Bird, multiplane panel, dandelion, MIT). The open body sits on a different, much sparser structure: two hairpin sleds, four rod legs with tiny feet, one stem on a stone block, or hanging wires. In MIT and New York nothing reaches the floor.
- **Offset rows, no aligned grid** (New York, multiplane panel, Dallas). Tiers are offset by about half a unit; no horizontal edge runs across more than two or three plates. A strict grid exists only where the material demands it (chair mesh, rod wall pitch ±1–2 px).
- **Two planes, slight depth** (New York 24 in deep on 840 in wide, multiplane panel, Dallas). Plates sit in front of and behind a rod lattice, or turn on their rods, so a flat wall has a shallow, shimmering depth.
- **Colour is metal; one patch of hue at most** (all). The works are one metal family in 3–5 values (brass L 27–69, C 17–37; steel L 44–77, C ≤ 10). Saturated hue appears only as an applied, removable layer (a yellow seat pad 9%, an orange cover 8%) or on paper (monotype accents 4–8%).
- **Light and touch complete it** (MIT, screens, Sonambients, chairs). The rough or tilted surfaces exist to catch moving light; the rods exist to be struck; the chair mesh exists to take a body's shape. The foundation says light would "dance" through if the gaps were large enough.
- **Line layer over stain layer** (monotypes). On paper, soft translucent blots carry colour and mass; separate hair-thin line fans carry direction. The two never coincide.
- **The generative rule:** choose one thin unit; repeat it at a near-even pitch with gaps about equal to the unit; make the form by changing density and angle rather than by drawing an outline; join units at points; give the heaviest member to the rim or root only; carry the whole on a separate, much sparser support or hang it; keep colour to one material in a few values, with at most one applied patch of hue; leave it free to move, catch light or sound.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Harry Bertoia.** *Build the form as an open field of one thin unit with gaps as wide as the unit, and draw with crowding and thinning in place of outlines; weight goes only to a rim or root, hue only to one applied solid patch, and the viewer's movement finishes it.*
Process:
1. Fix the two limits the field runs between (an opening above, a thing it serves below, or two side limits) and fill the span between them. The field is about 0.9 of the opening it hangs from, and what it serves is about half the field's width (0.54). It has no frame, no centre and no edge treatment of its own.
2. Choose ONE thin unit and repeat it 30 to 400 times: a line of one weight, or a tall slip 2.6–4 times as high as wide. Units differ by a little (angle, length, a ragged end) and never by kind. A handful of units may be swapped for a different outline, each wholly surrounded by regular ones.
3. Set the gap equal to the unit. Solid covers 40–50% of the field's body, so whatever lies behind shows through and supplies the extreme value. Hold the pitch nearly even, with a wobble of 15–30% of the pitch.
4. Stack the units in 5–7 tiers and shift each tier by half a unit, so that no edge lines up across more than 2–3 units. Use a strict aligned grid only where the content is itself a mesh.
5. Draw with density. Run ONE gradient through the field, sparse at the far end and crowded where people act, rising about 4.5 times over six bands (22 → 35 → 63 → 87 → 93 → 102). Where a second form is needed, crowd or stretch the same units; never add an outline.
6. Spend weight once. Units meet at points, never along seams. One heavier member is allowed, at the rim or at the root. Carry the body on a separate support made of far fewer members, with a foot about 0.3 of the body's width, or hang it so nothing reaches the floor.
7. Split the units between two planes, in front of and behind a hairline lattice, with a depth of about 3% of the field's width, and give each unit its own tilt, so that only 2–9% of them answer the light or the pointer at any one position.
8. Keep the whole structure in one colour family at 3–5 values. If a hue enters, it is ONE solid patch of 8–9% of the view, the most saturated thing present, laid inside the open field along the field's own contour, and removable without touching the structure.
9. Leave the last step outside the work: something that moves (light, pointer, scroll, touch, live data) decides which units show, and a touched unit hands its motion to its neighbours.
Why it works:
- At a gap equal to the unit, neither solid nor void wins as figure, so the eye fuses hundreds of units into a tone. A change in crowding then reads as a change in tone, and a tone gradient reads as a turning surface or a gathering, which is why no outline is needed. Gaps much wider than the unit leave separate objects; gaps much narrower make a wall.
- Half-unit offsets stop edges from chaining into rows. Aligned tiers produce long lines, the eye follows the lines, and the field becomes a table.
- An outline closes the field into an object sitting on a ground. Without one, the ground runs through the field and supplies its lightest or darkest value for free.
- Likeness groups the units so strongly that one solid patch, or one odd outline, wins the glance at under a tenth of the area.
- A support as heavy as the body doubles the mass and the body stops reading as light. A sparse root makes the weight of the body a matter of count, not of thickness.
Colour relations: structure is one family in 3–5 values, ranked lit, shadowed, crease, and never the most saturated thing in view; the ground seen through the gaps is the extreme value (darkest or lightest) and takes the largest share, 28–60% within the field and up to three quarters of the whole view; lit units are the smallest share of the structure (3–5% of a dark view); one applied hue, 8–9% of the view, solid, highest chroma, a separate layer.
Space and line: field between limits, width to height anywhere from 0.5 to 4.4; solid 40–50%; gap = unit; 5–7 tiers offset by half a unit; one line weight plus one heavier rim or root; curves made from straight units that change angle, or from a square mesh pulled out of square; rounded corners on outer contours, a point kept where a form tapers; depth about 3% of the width; foot about 0.3 of the body.
Type requirement (derived; no lettering was observed): type is one more field of the unit. One weight per view, strokes close to the unit's weight, lines spaced so text rows and the gaps between them are about equal; labels sit on the heavier rim or root, or on the applied patch, and never across the open field; figures tabular so columns keep the pitch.
Motion and interaction: nothing moves by itself. Pointer, scroll or tilt changes which 2–9% of units are lit; a touched unit swings and passes a smaller swing to its neighbours, shorter units quicker than longer ones, and the motion dies out without a bounce at the end (about 150–250ms per unit, 600–900ms for the group); selection changes a unit's tilt or plane, not its hue. Pointer targets are the tier or the nearest unit, never the bare unit width. Reduced motion: lit states switch without the swing.
Fails when: there are fewer than about 30 units, because nothing fuses into tone; values must be compared along a line (tables, ledgers, forms), since offset and wobble defeat alignment; long text has to sit on the field; each unit needs its own label or a touch target at its own width; several categories each need a hue; a region must be sealed off (a destructive confirmation, a modal), since the field has no edge; the medium is static and unlit, so step 9 never happens.
Signature (do not carry together): hammered brass-toned plates hung on a welded rod lattice · a wire-mesh shell on a hairpin rod sled · a stand of upright metal rods on a flat base, or rods radiating from one core.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Test-run history for a build service, desktop 1440 × 900, light theme, brand navy with a coral accent, heavy geometric type (the works are metal, hand-welded and architectural).
1. *Limits.* The field hangs from the run selector, a control 1440px wide, and stops above the action bar. It is 1296px wide (0.9 of the selector). The action bar it serves is 700px wide (0.54 of the field). No card, no border, no title inside the field.
2. *Unit.* One slip, 12 × 40px (3.3 times as high as wide), is one test suite in one run. 204 slips in all. Five flaky suites are drawn as circles of the same area, each surrounded by slips.
3. *Gap.* Slips sit at a 24px pitch, 12px solid and 12px open, with 8px between tiers, so the fullest tier is 42% solid. Slip tops vary by up to 4px.
4. *Tiers.* Six tiers, one per run, each shifted 12px against the one below. No slip edge lines up across more than two tiers.
5. *Density.* The oldest run at the top keeps only the 12 suites whose result changed; then 18, 30, 42, 48, and the latest run at the bottom shows all 54 (4.5 times the top). The crowding at the bottom is the only thing that says "now"; no rule or heading does.
6. *Weight.* One 3px bar under the bottom tier carries the suite-group labels. It stands on two 1px stems that drop to the action bar, which holds three controls.
7. *Planes.* A 1px lattice of slanted lines runs through the field. Failed suites sit in front of it, passed suites behind it, one value apart. Each slip has its own tilt of up to 6 degrees, and at any pointer position at most 18 slips (9%) are lit.
8. *Colour.* Slips are navy in four values on the pale page, which shows through as the lightest value. Coral is one solid panel, 360 × 288px (8% of the view), laid over the right end of the field along its edge: the selected suite's log. Remove it and the field is complete.
9. *Completion.* Moving the pointer along the field relights slips under it; pressing a slip swings it and its two neighbours in each tier follow by half as much.
- *Motion.* 200ms per slip, the group settles in 700ms; reduced motion switches the lit state only.
- *Type.* One weight throughout. Labels on the 3px bar and on the coral panel; none on the field.

Result: a pale, navy-and-coral test history with nothing metallic, welded or mid-century in it. What survived is one unit at a gap equal to itself, offset tiers, form drawn by crowding, weight kept to one bar on a sparse support, and a single applied patch of hue.

## Space & composition
- **Field, not object** (New York, rod wall, MIT). The screens fill the space between architectural limits (counter to ceiling, oculus to altar) and have no internal centre; width-to-height runs from 0.5 (MIT, 121½ × 240 in) through ≈0.9 (rod wall) to 4.4 (New York).
- **Fits the opening it hangs from** (MIT): the field is ≈0.9 of the oculus diameter; the altar below is ≈0.54 of the field width.
- **Gradient from sparse to dense** toward where people are: 4.6× more lit plates at the bottom band than at the top (MIT).
- **Half open, half filled**: solid share 40–50% in the screens and rod walls; the unit and the gap are about the same width.
- **Shallow depth**: two planes a fraction of the width apart (24 in on 840 in).
- **Offset tiers**: rows shifted by about half a unit, 5–7 tiers in a screen.
- **Heavy small root, light large head** (dandelion): head ≈0.5 of total height, stem ≈0.9 head diameter, base ≈0.3 head diameter.

## Form, line & typography
The line is a thin, straight, even rod or wire of one gauge, with a slightly heavier rod at a rim or root. Curves come from many straight lines at changing angles, or a flat mesh pulled into a bowl; corners of outlines are rounded (Diamond rim), points are kept where a form tapers (Bird back, wire-form tip). Plates are tall rectangles (h/w 2.6 in the multiplane panel, ≈3.5–4 in New York), hammered, with irregular edges and pitted, beaded surfaces. On paper, line is a hairline in radiating fans, laid over soft-edged blots. Bertoia's own lettering was not examined.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- **Near-even pitch with small wobble**: rod wall pitch ≈6.4 px ±1–2; MIT wires 18–19 px centre, 13–14 px at the sides; New York columns ≈1 ft.
- **Rhythm by density change**, not by accent: 22 → 35 → 63 → 87 → 93 → 102 lit plates per band down the MIT field.
- **Moving light**: tilted plates (MIT, Dallas) mean only some catch light at any viewing position; walking past changes which ones glint.
- **Motion from outside**: Sonambient rods sway and strike each other when touched or moved by air; pin-balanced pieces may move in the wind, which Bertoia accepted. Shorter rods vibrate faster (higher pitch).
- **Body in the mesh**: the chair mesh gives slightly under a sitter and casts a moving grid shadow.

## UI translations
1. **Field between limits.** Hang a dense view from the control that filters it and stop it above the thing it serves; give it about 0.9 of that control's width and no card, border or centre.
2. **One unit, tens to hundreds.** Draw every item of a set as the same thin mark (a line of one weight, or a slip 2.6–4 times as high as wide) and vary only its angle or length.
3. **Gap equal to unit.** Space the marks so solid covers 40–50% of the field and the page shows through; the page colour is the field's extreme value.
4. **Offset tiers.** Stack 5–7 rows and shift each by half a unit, so the rows never chain into table lines.
5. **Density is the drawing.** Show where activity or attention is by crowding units there, about 4.5 times the sparse end across six bands, and use no outline, heading rule or tinted region.
6. **Weight once.** One heavier rule, at the rim or the root of the field, carries the labels; everything else stays at the unit's weight.
7. **Sparse support.** Controls for the field sit on a separate bar of a few members, joined to it by one or two hairlines, with a footprint near 0.3–0.5 of the field's width.
8. **Two planes.** Put one state in front of a hairline lattice and the other behind it, a value step apart, in place of two hues.
9. **One applied patch.** The only saturated colour is one solid panel of 8–9% of the view, laid inside the field along its contour, holding the selected item with a text label; closing it leaves the field whole.
10. **Lit by movement.** Tilt each unit a little differently so that pointer or scroll position lights at most 2–9% of them; a pressed unit swings and its neighbours follow by less. Targets are tiers or nearest-unit, at least 44px.
11. **Exceptions by outline.** Swap a handful of units for a second outline, each surrounded by regular units, and explain the outline in a legend.

## What this artist would reject
- A solid, closed surface where an open field of units would carry the form.
- An outline or border used to define a shape instead of density.
- Heavy bases, frames or supports equal in weight to the body they carry.
- Strictly aligned grids of identical tiles with no offset, angle or hand variation.
- Many colours on the structure itself; hue painted onto the metal rather than laid on as a separate pad or stain.
- Work that is finished without light, touch, sound or a body to complete it.

## Signature prompt seed
Build each dense view as an open field of one thin unit repeated tens to hundreds of times, with gaps as wide as the unit so the page shows through about half of it. Stack the units in five to seven tiers, each shifted by half a unit, and never draw an outline: show form and emphasis by crowding the units where people act, about four and a half times the sparse end. Keep one line weight, with a single heavier member at the rim or the root, and carry the field on a separate, much sparser support. Stay in one colour family at a few values, and let hue enter once, as a solid applied patch under a tenth of the view. Nothing moves until the pointer, scroll or data moves it.
