# Eero Saarinen — Cranbrook / organic modernism

## Essence
Saarinen treated each commission as one problem with one structural idea, then pushed that idea until it became the whole form: a single stem instead of four legs, a single arch instead of a frame, a single hanging surface instead of a ceiling grid. The members thin out toward where they meet the ground or each other and swell where the load gathers, so the structure reads as one continuous, flowing body. Colour is mostly withheld (white, grey concrete, steel, brick); one saturated warm colour is kept for the place where the body sits or walks.

## Key works examined
- **Womb Chair (Model 70), designed 1946–48, made c. 1959–63 (fibreglass-reinforced plastic shell, latex foam, wool upholstery, chrome-plated steel rod base; V&A W.35-2008 is 92.3 × 102.6 × 91 cm; Brooklyn Museum example photographed).** One upholstered shell that holds the whole body, floated on a few thin rods. It teaches one big enclosing mass on minimal support. *(visually checked: Brooklyn Museum photo via Wikimedia Commons; shell vs rod widths, leg share of height, palette)*
- **Tulip (Pedestal) Armchair, Model 150, 1955–56 (fibreglass-reinforced polyester shell, cast aluminium base, wool cushion; Brooklyn Museum c. 1970 example 81.3 × 64.8 × 58.4 cm; MoMA also holds one).** One shell on one stem on one disc. It teaches the single support that flares at both ends. *(visually checked: Brooklyn Museum photo via Commons; silhouette widths row by row, stem minimum, base width, red share, palette)*
- **Pedestal / Tulip Side Chair, designed 1955–57 (fibreglass-reinforced plastic, aluminium, upholstery; 32 × 19½ × 21 in; Cranbrook Art Museum ZO 1977.7, gift of Knoll International).** The armless version: the same stem, a narrower shell. It teaches that the system holds when a part is removed. *(visually checked: a museum example in Italy and a group of four with a pedestal table at the TWA Hotel, via Commons; Cranbrook record read, image not fetched; visual proportion only)*
- **Pedestal side table, 1955–57 (laminate or marble top on cast aluminium pedestal, Knoll).** The same stem logic under a round top. *(visually checked: TWA Hotel photo via Commons, in use with four side chairs; dimensions not obtained)*
- **General Motors Technical Center, Warren, Michigan, 1945–56 (steel frame, glass and porcelain-enamel curtain walls, glazed brick end walls, 22-acre lake, Design Dome).** A campus of long, low, gridded boxes around a long rectangle of water, punctuated by a few curved objects. It teaches the strict field with rare round incidents. *(visually checked: Library of Congress photo of the Design Dome and a 2021 aerial via Commons; lake aspect ratio, palette. - **MIT Chapel, Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1950–55 (brick cylinder about 50 ft diameter, 30 ft high, in a moat; Harry Bertoia altar screen; travertine floor; marble altar block).** A windowless drum lit from one oculus and from water light under low arches. It teaches darkness as the field and one shaft of light as the subject. *(visually checked: two interior photos via Commons; oculus/screen/altar widths, lit-plate count, palette)*
- **Kresge Auditorium, MIT, 1950–55 (thin reinforced-concrete shell, one-eighth of a sphere, on three points; glass curtain walls; 1,200 seats).** A single spherical triangle cut from a ball and set down on three points, glass filling the gaps. It teaches one geometric cut as the whole building. *(visually checked: 2017 photo via Commons; palette, roof share)*
- **David S. Ingalls Rink, Yale, 1953–58 (reinforced-concrete catenary arch about 300 ft long, steel cable net, timber roof; 75 ft maximum height).** One spine arch with a roof hung from it like a tent. It teaches a single backbone that carries everything and ends in a gesture. *(visually checked: Carol Highsmith photo and the Library of Congress longitudinal and transverse section drawing via Commons; section profile, cable intervals, palette)*
- **Gateway Arch, St. Louis, designed 1947–48, built 1963–65 (stainless steel Type 304 #3 finish over carbon steel and concrete; 630 × 630 ft; equilateral-triangle section tapering 54 ft to 17 ft; 142 sections).** The single gesture at its purest. It teaches taper as the only ornament. *(visually checked: two photos via Commons; height:width, crown vs leg thickness, face colours, arch share of frame)*

## Detailed observations

**Womb Chair.** The chair is one upholstered shell that wraps the sitter on three sides and tips back, set on a frame of thin chrome rods that end in small round glides. In the Brooklyn photo the dark shell is about 415 px wide while the rods are 4–7 px thick, so the support is about 1–1.5% of the width of what it carries. The rods take about 20% of the chair's height; the shell takes the rest. The shell's outline is one continuous closed curve from the head down through the arms; the arms are not separate parts but the shell's rim turned forward. Two loose cushions (seat and back) sit inside it, each a softer rounded rectangle, and a matching ottoman repeats the seat as a lower, flatter pad on the same rods. The upholstery is a near-black woven wool (`#19181C`, L 8, C 3, about 15% of the frame; the shadowed side `#323035`, L 21) on a white studio ground (`#F3F3F3`, L 96, 40%). There are no visible seams on the outer shell, no buttons, no exposed wood, no arm pads. The base is a bent rod loop, not four separate legs: the rod runs under the shell and turns down.

**Tulip (Pedestal) Armchair.** Seen from the front, the object is about 1,150 px tall. The shell takes the top 54% (rows 261–885), the stem about 35%, the base disc the last 11%. The shell is widest at the arm tips (893 px). The back's top edge dips in the centre and rises into two soft shoulders, so the silhouette from above reads as a petal. The stem narrows to 37–42 px, about 4–5% of the shell width, and the narrowest point sits a little below the shell's underside, not at mid-height. The stem flares in two places: up into the underside of the shell (111 px wide at the junction) and down into the base, widening from 42 px to 97 px and then to 248 px over the last ~15% of its length before merging into the disc, which is 549 px wide (0.61 of the shell width) in this slightly high view. The transitions are fillets with no visible joint line between stem and base or stem and shell. The only colour is the cushion: a flat round pad set into the seat, about 12% of the chair's silhouette, measured `#751B23` in this dim studio shot (L 27, C 40; it reads as a mid red). The white shell is `#EAE7DC` (L 91, C 5) with soft grey shading (`#B8B6AB`, L 74) on the inside where light falls off; the background is near-black green-grey (`#1B271C`, L 14, 56% of the frame). There are no screws, no legs, no armrest pads, no piping.

**Pedestal side chair and table.** The armless chair is the same stem and disc under a narrower shell that rises into a single rounded back with a slight central dip, with no arms. In the TWA Hotel group, four side chairs face a round white pedestal table of the same family: table top, stem and disc are one white body, the top a thin disc with a rounded edge. Every element in the group stands on one point, so the floor between them is open: the penny-tile floor runs uninterrupted under all five objects. Red seat pads (`#540507` in the shade of that room, L 17, C 41; about 16% of the frame together with the red banquettes behind) are the only colour on the white bodies (`#F6F6F6`, L 94, 7%). The Cranbrook record gives the side chair as 32 × 19½ × 21 in, so its width is about 0.61 of its height, against 0.80 for the armchair.

**General Motors Technical Center.** From the 2021 aerial, the original campus is a field of long, flat, rectangular buildings in parallel rows around one long lake. The lake measures about 2.6 : 1 in the oblique photo (Wikipedia gives 1,780 × 560 ft, which is 3.2 : 1). The buildings are low (generally three storeys or fewer per the sources), so the plan reads as horizontal lines. The only round thing visible on the whole site from the air is the Design Dome, a white hemisphere at the south end of the lake, plus a small water tower. In the Library of Congress photo, the dome is a shallow aluminium-clad dome, faceted into large panels, rising behind a long, low, dark brick screen wall that runs almost the whole frame width, with a hedge of clipped trees on top of the wall line. The dome is about 400 px wide and shows about 150 px of rise above the hedge; the wall is about 40 px high and about 920 px long, more than 20 times its height. The entrance is a flat white slab canopy on thin posts with a dark glazed front, cut into the wall at the centre under the dome. Colours here: dome and paving pale grey (`#C8CABA`, L 80, about 18%), wall and trees dark (`#182825`, L 14; `#294D36`, L 29), sky `#0488BF`. The glazed brick end walls in strong colours are reported in the sources (nine or eleven colours: reds, ultramarine, oranges, yellows) but were not seen in any photo obtained. Sources give the planning module as 5 ft (5 ft 2 in on centres in the offices, from the fluorescent fixture length).

**MIT Chapel.** The interior is mostly dark: in the frontal photo half the frame is `#1A1009` (L 6) and another 21% is `#30190B` (L 12), the brick in shadow. Light comes from one oval oculus at the top centre and falls onto the altar. Measured across the frontal photo, the oculus is about 325 px wide, the Bertoia screen under it about 275 px wide (0.85 of the oculus), and the marble altar block about 160 px wide (about half the oculus). The screen hangs from the oculus rim to the floor behind the altar, about 2.2 times taller than it is wide. It is made of hundreds of small rectangular metal plates on vertical rods; about 400–450 separate lit specks are counted in its lit band. The plates are scattered at uneven heights, denser in the upper half, so the screen reads as falling light, not as a grid. The altar is a plain white marble block (the press release gives about three feet high) on three low circular steps. The brick walls undulate in plan: in the side view the wall swells into at least three convex bays, and each brick course follows the curve. Low in the wall, square-latticed brick panels with open holes let sound through; below them a band of vertical wood slats wraps the room at waist height and curves with the wall. Between the wall base and the floor there is a gap of glass where reflected light from the moat enters. The floor is pale travertine in a large diamond pattern (`#B08F54` in warm light, L 59, about 8%). Seats are rows of ladder-back chairs with rush seats in a dark stained wood, a repeated vertical rhythm in front of the altar. Sky through the oculus is the only cool colour (`#9FD0E4`, L 80, 1%). The building has no windows at eye level. The light ladder is roughly: dark brick L 6–12, lit wall L 27, floor L 59, altar and screen L 80+.

**Kresge Auditorium.** The building is one thin curved roof: a spherical triangle whose three edges are arcs, touching the ground at three points. The visible edge rises from a small pad at the left, arcs over the glazed front and comes down to a second pad at the right. A raised edge beam runs along each arc, the only thickened line on the shell. Below each arc the opening is filled with a glass wall on a narrow vertical mullion grid, about five tiers of panes, with a single door group at centre. The roof surface is metal sheet with visible standing seams in parallel lines, pale green-grey (`#A1B4B8`, L 72, C 7, about 30% of the cropped frame). The glass reads dark (`#3E413B` / `#1D1F1D`, L 11–27). The roof meets the supports with no columns between the three points; the glass is non-structural infill hung under the edge. The ground is a flat brick paving plaza and lawn, so the dome sits on a plain plane.

**Ingalls Rink.** In the photo a single concrete spine (`#A89880`, L 63) runs up out of the ground on the left, carries over the building and continues beyond the roof as a cantilevered tip that curls up into a pointed light fitting, like a raised head. The roof drapes off both sides of the spine as a dark metal-and-timber skin (`#4D4E52`, L 32) that lifts again at the eaves, so its edge is an S-curve. The glazed entrance (wood-framed doors, `#68563A`) sits under the lifted edge, which overhangs it like a lip. In the longitudinal section the spine rises from the ground line (y 323) to a crown at y 190 over a length of about 735 px, a rise of about 0.18 of the length, then dips at each end and flips back up beyond the walls. The section shows about 50 evenly spaced vertical lines (one every ~9 px) along the middle, the cable lines seen in elevation. In the transverse section the roof falls from the central spine in a sagging curve: from the spine it drops 64 px over the first 90 px and only 37 px over the next 90, steep at the spine and flattening toward the eaves, the shape of hanging cable. The seating rakes down on both sides toward the ice, a valley under a tent.

**Gateway Arch.** In the river-front photo the arch is as wide as it is tall (about 690 px each, matching the 630 × 630 ft of the fact sheet). The leg is thick at the ground and thin at the top: the crown is 19 px deep (2.7% of the height), the legs about 45–49 px across near the base. The fact sheet gives the triangle side as 54 ft at the base and 17 ft at the top, a taper of 3.2 : 1. The curve is continuous with no visible kink at the crown; the top is rounder than a parabola would look and the legs come down almost vertically, as in a weighted catenary. The arch has no colour of its own: its two visible faces take the sky and the shade, `#8699AC` on the lit right face (L 61) and `#555D6D` on the shaded left face (L 39), against sky `#6CA4EE` / `#87B8F1`. The arch covers only about 2.6% of the frame, yet it organises the whole view; through it, the old courthouse dome is framed at the centre. In the second photo, taken from the approach path, the legs read as two faces meeting at a sharp edge, the triangular section visible as a hard ridge running up the leg; the steel skin shows a fine grid of panel joints. There is no plinth, no inscription, no base; the legs go straight into the grass.

**TWA Flight Center.** In the 2015 exterior, the roof is four shells: two large wing shells left and right and two smaller shells front and back, meeting at the centre with thin skylight slits between them. The shell span in the photo is about 910 px and its height from the ground to the wingtips about 173 px, a height of about 0.19 of the span. The two wingtips rise to sharp points; the centre front shell dips down to a ridge in the middle. The shells sit on four Y-shaped piers whose feet are 30–40 px wide at the ground (about 7–8% of one wing's width), spaced 318 px apart at the front (0.35 of the span). Each pier flares from a narrow foot into the shells with no joint line, the same move as the Tulip stem; between the two legs of the Y a rounded hole opens. The glass walls under the wings lean outward, with mullions slanting in a fan. In the close exterior view the wing's edge is a thick concrete lip that tapers to a sharp nose; the low side wings have their own rounded buttresses between doors at roughly even intervals. In low sun the concrete is warm cream (`#EAD2B2`, L 85, C 23, 28%); glass and shadow `#2F2D24` (L 18). Inside (restored, now the TWA Hotel), the balconies, stairs and railings are all white, continuous curves, with a red carpet (`#A90A07`, L 36, C 71, about 3% of that frame) running on the walking surfaces, balconies and ramp floors and nowhere else. The ceiling shells curve overhead in grey-cream; no right angle is visible except in later furniture.

**Dulles main terminal.** The ceiling is one plane that sags in a shallow curve along the length of the hall, a smooth grey concrete surface (`#858687`, L 56, 45% of the frame, and `#6E6E6A`, L 45, 23%) with only a few recessed lights in a small cluster. It fills more than half of the view in both photos. On one side the glass wall is held by tall piers that lean outward and flare at the top where they catch the roof edge; the window frames between them are long and narrow. The far end of the hall is all light: in the 1963 slide the end wall is a white glare band (`#FFFCED`, L 95, 5.5%). Under the ceiling, the floor is grey terrazzo and the only warm colour is the wooden counter fronts and panelling (`#A96D39`, L 53, C 41, 2.2% in the modern photo). In the 1963 slide a single round column with a flared top, the shape of a funnel, stands under the ceiling; the ceiling stays clear of all other columns.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **One structural idea per work, carried to the whole form** (Tulip, Gateway Arch, Kresge, Ingalls, Dulles). Tulip: one stem. Gateway Arch: one catenary, 630 × 630 ft. Kresge: one eighth of a sphere on three points. Ingalls: one spine about 300 ft long from which the whole roof hangs. Dulles: one suspended ceiling. In none of them is there a secondary structure that competes; the infill (glass at Kresge, Ingalls, Dulles, TWA) is visibly not load-bearing.
- **Support narrows to a point, then flares into what it carries** (Tulip, TWA, Dulles, Womb). Tulip stem 4–5% of the shell width, flaring to 0.61 at the floor and widening again under the shell. TWA pier feet 7–8% of a wing's width, flaring into the shells. The Dulles funnel column flares at the ceiling. Womb rods 1–1.5% of the shell width. The joint is a fillet, never a visible connection.
- **Taper as the only ornament** (Gateway Arch, TWA, Ingalls). Arch: 54 ft to 17 ft, a 3.2 : 1 taper; crown depth 2.7% of height in the photo. TWA shells: 7 in at the edges to 40 in at the junction, so the thinnest part is the visible edge. Ingalls: the spine continues past the roof and thins to a point.
- **The cut-out of a pure solid** (Kresge, MIT Chapel, Design Dome). Kresge is a sphere cut into one eighth; the chapel is a cylinder; the Design Dome is a hemisphere. Each is set on a flat ground (lawn, moat, paving) with nothing on the roof.
- **A field of strict repetition, punctured by one round event** (GM Technical Center, MIT campus pair). GM: long flat boxes on a 5 ft module around a 3.2 : 1 lake; the only round forms are the dome and the water tower. MIT: Kresge (sphere) and the chapel (cylinder) are the two curved objects in a rectilinear campus.
- **Dark field, single light source, many small repeated points within it** (MIT Chapel). Half the chapel frame is L 6; one oculus; under it ~400–450 small plates scattered at uneven heights, the screen 0.85 of the oculus width and the altar half of it. The ladder runs dark L 6 → wall L 27 → floor L 59 → light L 80+.
- **Neutral body, one warm saturated colour where the body touches** (Tulip, TWA, Dulles, MIT Chapel). Tulip: white shell L 91 C 5, red cushion ~12% of the silhouette. TWA: white and cream throughout, red carpet only on walking surfaces, ~3% of the frame. Dulles: grey concrete 68% of the frame, wood counters 2.2%. MIT: brick dark, the warm wood band and rush seats at body height.
- **Material takes the light, not a paint colour** (Gateway Arch, Kresge, Design Dome). The arch has no colour: L 39 and L 61 on its two faces from the same steel. Kresge's roof is pale grey-green metal; the Design Dome is bare aluminium panels. Colour is whatever the sky and sun put on the surface.
- **A continuous surface from structure to furniture** (TWA, Womb, Tulip). TWA balconies, stairs and railings run as one white surface out of the shells. The Womb arms are the shell's rim turned forward. The Tulip stem, base and shell look cast as one piece with no seam.
- **The end of a form becomes a gesture** (Ingalls, TWA, Tulip). Ingalls' spine curls up beyond the roof into a pointed tip. TWA wingtips end in sharp noses. The Tulip back dips in the centre and rises into two shoulders.
- **The generative rule:** pick one structural principle (a stem, an arch, a hanging line, a cut solid); make it the whole form with no secondary system; thin each member toward its edge or its contact point and swell it where loads gather, joining parts by fillets so they read as one cast body; set it on a plain, level ground; keep the body neutral (white, grey, bare metal, brick) and spend one warm saturated colour, a few percent of the view, on the surface the body touches or walks on.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Eero Saarinen.** *Find the one element that carries the view and make it the whole form: nothing else bears, supports gather to a few narrow contacts that widen into what they hold, thickness follows load, and the only strong colour goes where the user touches.*
Process:
1. Name the one carrier. Write the view's job in one sentence and pick ONE element that holds everything else: a spine, a single stem, one overhead plane, or one cut from a simple figure. Everything else hangs from it, rests on it or fills the gap under it. Test: take the carrier away and nothing else has a place; take anything else away and the carrier still stands. No second system: no frame around it, no extra supports under it.
2. Proportion it by its job. A carrier that stands alone as a mark is as wide as it is tall (1 : 1) and may be small, down to about 3% of the view. A carrier that shelters content is low and wide, rising 0.18–0.19 of its span; as an overhead plane it may fill 50–70% of the view.
3. Gather the supports. The carrier meets its ground at 1–4 points, never in a row. Each contact is 4–8% of the width of what it holds (1–1.5% when the support is a thin line), and several contacts stand about a third of the span apart. The ground runs unbroken between and under them. The carried mass takes the upper 55–80% of the height.
4. Narrow, then widen into the load. Each support is thinnest just under what it carries, not at its midpoint, and widens both ways: into the carried element and into its foot (a single foot spreads to about 0.6 of the carried width). The widening is continuous, drawn in the case's own geometry, and no line, gap or connector marks where two parts meet: they share one fill.
5. Let thickness follow load. Every member is thickest where things gather and thins toward its free edge by 3 : 1 to 6 : 1, so the thinnest part is the edge seen first. No member keeps one thickness and stops square: a free end tapers out or turns once, and at most one end per view runs on past the body.
6. Fill, do not build. Whatever lies between the carrier and the ground is infill: thin straight lines of one weight at one even interval, set back in value from the carrier and visibly hung under its edge. Even repetition belongs to the infill only (from 5 tiers to about 50 lines). The carrier is never repeated, gridded or boxed.
7. Clear the setting. One carrier per view, on a plain level ground, with nothing placed on top of it. In a larger system the setting is a strict rectilinear field on one module, and the carrier is one of at most two figures that do not conform to it.
8. Colour last, by touch. The carrier and its supports take no hue: low chroma, modelled by two values 15–25 L apart (lit and shaded). The single strongest colour, 2–12% of the view, goes on the surfaces the user touches or travels along, on all of them and on nothing else.
Why it works:
- A continuous form that nothing rivals is the only figure in the view, so it organises the view even at 3% of the area. Add a second bearing system and there are two figures: the carrier becomes a part and the eye has to work out which one holds the other. Infill at one weight and one interval fuses into a tone, so it reads as surface and leaves the carrier alone.
- A row of supports makes a texture at the base that competes with what stands on it, and it cuts the ground into strips. One to four narrow contacts leave the ground continuous, so the object reads as one thing placed on a field, and a large mass on a small contact looks poised (visual weight and leverage).
- Thickness that changes with load tells the viewer where things gather without a label. A member of constant thickness reads as a drawn outline; one that swells and thins reads as a body. A thin free edge makes a large mass look light, and a tapered end leads the eye off the form where a square end stops it.
- A joint is a line, and lines divide. With no line where support meets load, the two group by continuity into one object; one seam is enough to turn the form into an assembly of parts.
- Colour assigned by use is a reliable sign: on a body with no hue, the one strong colour is found at once (salience by oddness) and always answers "where do I act". Spread over the structure, or shared with decoration, it stops answering anything.
Colour relations: the body is the largest share, low in chroma, in two values 15–25 L apart that come from light and not from a second colour; infill is the darkest tone, 45–60 L below a pale body; one touch colour holds the highest chroma at 2–12% of the view (about 3% across a whole room, about 12% of a single object), in the works always warmer than the body and, on a pale body, about 60 L below it; one further tone at most, for the ground. Alternative mode, one work: a dark field over about 70% of the view, one source of light, the subject directly under it at half the source's width, a four-step ladder (about 6, 27, 59 and 80+ L), and several hundred small points scattered unevenly, denser toward the source, in a band 0.85 of the source's width.
Space and line: one carrier per view; 1 : 1 standing alone, rise 0.18–0.19 of span when it shelters; 1–4 contacts, each 4–8% of the carried width, about a third of the span apart; support thinnest just under the load; foot about 0.6 of the carried width; taper 3 : 1 to 6 : 1; straight lines occur only as infill, one weight, one interval; no line at any joint; plain level ground; in a system, a strict modular field with at most two non-conforming figures.
Type requirement: nothing typographic was observed, so this is derived from the infill rule. Text is infill: one family, one weight for running text, set in even lines on one interval and hung from the carrier's edge, with no boxes or rules of its own around headings. One display line at most, placed on the ground or on a touch surface and never across a joint or a tapering edge. The touch colour may set a label only where the label is itself the control.
Motion and interaction: movement follows the carrier's line and never crosses it; content hangs down from the carrier's edge when it opens and returns to it (about 300ms, ease-out, no bounce; durations derived); a focal view arrives by dimming in two or three stages before the subject is lit; press and focus change the touch colour's value and leave the carrier untouched; every touch surface is a full-size target (≥44px). Reduced motion: cut to the end state.
Fails when: the view is a set of equal peers with no single element that can carry them (grids of cards, dashboards); the content is dense tabular data that needs visible structure of its own, which is a second system; several states each need a colour, since there is one and it means touch; touch surfaces exceed about 12% of the view (long forms), where the colour stops being a sign; the carrier is too small to show a 3 : 1 taper.
Signature (do not carry together): a white moulded shell on one flared pedestal · a red cushion or red carpet on an all-white body · swooping concrete shells or a steel arch ending in upturned points.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Build-pipeline run page for a developer tool, desktop 1440 × 900, dark theme, monospace type, square corners, electric-blue brand colour (the works are pale, curved, cast in one piece and accented in warm red).
1. *Carrier.* The run's timeline: one band 1200px long from which the six stage logs hang. Without it the stages have no order; without any one log it still stands. No card around the page, no second rail.
2. *Proportion.* It shelters content, so it is low and wide: the band's top sits 216px above the base line (0.18 of 1200).
3. *Supports.* Two contacts, "commit" at the left end and "deploy" at the right, each 72px wide (6% of the span). The log area runs unbroken between and under them. The band and what hangs from it take the upper 70% of the height.
4. *Narrow, then widen.* Each foot is 72px just under the band and splays by straight 45° cuts to 120px where it enters the band and to 160px at the base. Foot and band share one fill, with no stroke between them.
5. *Thickness.* The band is 28px deep over the two feet and 8px at midspan (3.5 : 1). Past the deploy foot it runs on 64px and thins to a point, the one extended end.
6. *Infill.* Log lines hang under the band at a 20px interval in one weight, 50 L below the band; test ticks are 1px verticals every 12px. No boxes around stages.
7. *Setting.* A flat, level ground at L 10. Nothing sits on top of the band.
8. *Colour.* Band and feet are neutral, L 78 lit and L 58 on the splayed faces (20 L apart), chroma near zero. Electric blue appears only on what the user presses or follows: "re-run", the focused log row and the link trail, 4% of the view. A failed stage is shown by an icon, a label and an inverted row, since the one colour is taken.
- *Motion.* Progress travels along the band from the commit foot. A stage's log drops from the band's underside in 300ms and returns to it. Reduced motion cuts.
- *Type.* One monospace family in one weight for logs; the run title is the only display line, set on the ground above midspan.

Result: a dark, angular, monospaced pipeline page with nothing white, curved or moulded in it. What survived is one carrier with no rival, two narrow contacts that widen into it, thickness that follows load, even infill hung beneath, and colour kept for what is touched.

## Space & composition
- **One object, one gesture per view.** The arch is 2.6% of its frame yet organises it; Kresge and the Design Dome are single solids on a flat plane.
- **Width equals height when the form stands alone** (Gateway Arch 630 × 630 ft); low and wide when it shelters (TWA shells height 0.19 of span; Ingalls spine rise about 0.18 of its length).
- **Supports pulled to the centre or to a few points.** One stem (Tulip), three points (Kresge), four piers at 0.35 of the span apart (TWA), two leg feet (arch). The floor between supports stays open.
- **Strict field, rare curve.** GM's long low boxes on a 5 ft grid around a 3.2 : 1 lake; the dome the only round thing.
- **Light from one place into a dark volume** (MIT Chapel): a single oculus; the subject (altar) half the oculus width directly below.
- **Long, horizontal, continuous ceilings** (Dulles, TWA): the ceiling surface fills 50–70% of the interior views.

## Form, line & typography
The line is a continuous curve with no corners: a catenary (arch, Ingalls, Dulles), a sphere section (Kresge, Design Dome), a flared stem (Tulip). Members taper toward edges and tips; joints are fillets, not seams. Ends turn up into points (Ingalls tip, TWA wingtips, Tulip shoulders). Straight lines appear only as repeated infill: mullions, cables, the ~50 cable lines in the Ingalls section, the rods of the Womb base, the vertical rods of the Bertoia screen. Saarinen's buildings carry almost no lettering; at TWA the airline name is set in a red italic sans on the white balcony wall (seen in the hotel photo, possibly restored). No typography system was observed.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- **Movement follows the curve.** At TWA the body moves under rising shells, up white stairs and along balconies on a red floor, through tubes (232 and 272 ft long, red carpet) to the gate. Saarinen wanted the building to express the excitement of travel (Wikipedia, quoting him).
- **Approach and threshold.** At the chapel, the route goes through a grove and a narthex where the light dims in stages before the single shaft over the altar. At the Arch, the tram rides inside the leg 748 ft at 340 ft/min.
- **Even repeats in the infill, never in the structure.** Ingalls cables at even ~9 px intervals in the section; Kresge mullions in a regular grid; Bertoia plates deliberately uneven.
- **Light changes with the hour.** The arch's faces change from L 39 to L 61 across the same steel at one moment; TWA concrete goes cream in low sun; the chapel's moat light flickers on the wall base.

## UI translations
1. **One carrier.** Give each screen one continuous structural element (a single pedestal under the main panel, one arch over the content, one hanging header plane) and hang everything else from it. No sidebar frame, no second rail.
2. **Proportion:** A stand-alone mark is as wide as it is tall. A sheltering header or canopy rises 0.18–0.19 of its span and may fill 50–70% of the view.
3. **Pedestal, not legs.** Stand a card, a table or a dialog on one centred stem 4–5% of its width that flares to a base about 0.6 of the width, instead of four corner supports or a full-width footer.
4. **Fillets.** Join stem to panel and stem to base with a continuous concave curve; no border, divider or shadow line at any joint. Curves have no corners.
5. **Taper:** Thin every panel toward its free edge (3 : 1 to 6 : 1) and end one edge per screen in an upturned point.
6. **Infill:** Fill under the carrier with a regular grid of hairline verticals (mullions, cable lines) in one weight, dark behind a pale shell (`#2F2D24` under `#EAE7DC`).
7. **Color:** Keep the whole structure white or pale concrete (`#EAE7DC`, shading `#B8B6AB`; or cream `#EAD2B2`) with no painted colour on it.
8. **Color:** Put one saturated red (`#A90A07`) on touch surfaces only: buttons, the active path, input seats, at about 3% of a full screen and up to 12% of a single component.
9. **Material light:** For a metal or monochrome theme, show the carrier in two faces of one tone about 20 L apart (`#8699AC` lit, `#555D6D` shaded) and let it take no colour of its own.
10. **Field and event.** In a multi-screen product, keep long low rectilinear panels on one strict module and allow one or two round objects in the whole system.
11. **Chapel mode.** For a single focal screen, use a near-black field (`#1A1009`, about 70% of the view), one light source at top centre, the subject directly under it at half the source's width, and a fall of several hundred small unevenly scattered points between them.
12. **Ground:** Set the object on a plain, level, unpatterned ground with nothing on top of it and no plinth or caption block under it.
13. **Type:** No typography system was observed. For a strict study, set one plain sans in one weight as even infill under the carrier, with at most one display line.
14. **Motion:** Move content along the carrier's curve; open panels by letting them hang down from its edge (about 300ms, ease-out). Dim in stages before a focal reveal.

## What this artist would reject
- **A "slum of legs":** four separate legs under every chair and table, or many supports where one would do.
- **Secondary structure competing with the main idea:** columns under a hanging roof, a frame around an arch.
- **Visible joints and fasteners:** screws, seams, brackets where a fillet could make one cast body.
- **Colour spread over structure:** painted shells, multicoloured surfaces; colour belongs on the cushion or the walking surface.
- **Even, overall lighting in a sacred or focal room:** windows at eye level, a lit ceiling grid instead of one source.
- **Stopping a form with a blunt end:** members that keep the same thickness and stop square instead of tapering to an edge or a tip.
