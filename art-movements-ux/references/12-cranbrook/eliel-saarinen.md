# Eliel Saarinen — Cranbrook / organic modernism

## Essence
Saarinen designed whole places at once, from campus axis to the chair back, and tied the scales together by passing one geometric figure down through them (octagon in the courtyard paving → rug → table base → circle of the gilded dome). His buildings are warm, hand-laid masses (granite, brick, veneer, wool pile) that carry a rich surface pattern. The pattern is always ruled by a strict symmetry or axis, and he breaks that axis on purpose, by a small amount.

## Key works examined
- **Helsinki Central Station, competition 1904, built 1910–19 (red Hanko granite over brick and concrete; central hall ≈780 m²; Helsinki).** One giant arched block between low wings and an off-centre clock tower. It teaches how one big figure dominates a long, quiet frontage. *(visually checked: Wikimedia Commons, frontal photo by Aaaatu 2021, oblique photos by HartOve 2024 and Daderot 2012, Saarinen's perspective drawing (Finnish Architecture Museum scan); measured mass ratios, arch rings, window grid, palette)*
- **Hvitträsk, 1901–03 (with Gesellius and Lindgren; log, plaster, stone; Kirkkonummi, Finland).** The family studio-home before Cranbrook. It teaches the ryijy rug used as architecture (draped from floor over a bench), and painted ribs on plain vaults. *(visually checked: Wikimedia Commons, Markku Haverinen photos 2000, living room and "dining room"; palette)*
- **Chicago Tribune Tower competition entry, 1922 (second prize).** The stepped tower that brought him to America. *(not obtained: Commons file rate-limited on every retry; known only from text)*
- **Cranbrook School for Boys, 1925–29 (brick, red tile roofs, copper; Bloomfield Hills, Michigan).** Courts ringed by brick halls, an octagonal tower, a barrel-vaulted dining hall. It teaches a different brick pattern on every façade inside one family. *(visually checked: Wikimedia Commons, tower and quadrangle (Portnoy 2005, Brossard 2006), quad gate and brick façade (Trevorwelsh 2022), dining hall (ellenm1 2011); measured tower ratio, window:pier ratios, gable niche counts and pitch, lamp module, palettes)*
- **Saarinen House, designed 1928, built 1929–30 (brick; interiors in veneer, gold leaf, wool; CAM 1992.25, Cranbrook Art Museum).** The family home and studio, restored 1988–94. It teaches the house as one designed object, with one geometric figure carried from paving to ceiling. *(visually checked: Cranbrook Art Museum, Balthazar Korab photo of the dining room; Saarinen House visitor guide PDF, photos of living room, studio, exterior, courtyard and the ground-floor plan drawing; measured chair-back staves, dome rings, dome/room ratio, palettes)*
- **Loja and Eliel Saarinen, Rug No. 2, Nov 1928–Feb 1929 (cotton warp, wool pile, ryijy knotting with ten picks between rows; 110½ × 39 in; CAM 1955.3; woven by Studio Loja Saarinen with Walborg Nordquist Smalley).** A runner made for Loja's studio, signed "ES" and "CA". It teaches bilateral symmetry with banded side zones and a central spine. *(visually checked: Cranbrook Art Museum collection image (photo Dirk Bakker); measured aspect, band widths, palette)*
- **Kingswood School Cranbrook, 1929–31 (buff brick, copper roofs, Pewabic-type glazed tile; on Kingswood Lake).** The girls' school: long low horizontal wings with tall chimneys. It teaches horizontal calm and a stair lit by a stepped grid of small windows. *(visually checked: Wikimedia Commons, lake view (Danielcausa) and stair detail "Girls school - detail" (Brossard 2006); counted window steps, palettes)*
- **Cranbrook Academy of Art, Art Museum and Library with peristyle, 1938–42 (limestone frame, brick wings; Orpheus Fountain by Carl Milles, 1936, in front).** A flat-roofed open portico of slender square piers at the head of a long stepped axis of pools. It teaches the last, most abstract stage of the campus. *(visually checked: Wikimedia Commons, frontal photo ajay_suresh 2021 and axis view "Cranbrook178" (Danielcausa); measured bay spacing, pier slenderness, palette)*
- **Hot water urn, 1934 (silver-plated, spun and stamped; made by Wilcox Silver Plate Co.; British Museum; prototype tea service at the Met, urn H. 14½ in, tray Ø 17⅝ in).** A sphere on a pierced cylinder. It teaches pure solids joined by one repeated slot motif. *(visually checked: Wikimedia Commons, Daderot 2024 photo of the British Museum example; measured part ratios; no palette, the surface is a mirror)*
- **Side chair, 1929 (black walnut, leather; 110.2 × 45.7 × 48.3 cm; Met 482516/482517).** From the dining room shown at the Met's 1929 exhibition. *(not obtained: Met image not open access; its sibling, the Saarinen House dining chair, was seen in the Korab photo)*

## Detailed observations

**Helsinki Central Station.** In the frontal photo the main block is one round-headed arch set inside a slightly flattened arch-shaped mass. The block is ≈1.41 times as wide as it is high (to the canopy) and ≈1.1 times (to the ground). Around the opening, ≈7 concentric stepped ribs recede like the orders of a portal; they run from the ground up and over without a break. Inside them lies a plain granite band, then an inner arch whose glazing spans ≈0.45 of the block's width. The glazed lunette is a two-level grid: 9 dark bronze mullions make 10 tall bays, each split again by a lighter grid of ≈3 × 13 small panes. A square clock face sits in the central bay, on the vertical axis, just above the canopy. The copper roof edge follows the arch, but it is scalloped: a run of small rounded humps with knobs, not a smooth curve. Wings left and right are low (arch height ≈1.8× the wing height) and long, with a regular row of window bays. The clock tower stands off to one side, not on the axis. It is ≈1.4× the arch block's height and ≈2.6× the wings', a square shaft that steps back in tiers to a copper cap. Two pairs of giant granite figures (Emil Wikström's Lantern Bearers, holding globe lamps) flank the entrance: they replace columns. Saarinen's perspective drawing shows the long wings ≈5.7 times as long as they are high, with a festooned parapet and vertical pilaster strips at even intervals. Colour (arch crop, k = 6): pink-buff granite `#CEAA8F` (L 72, C 21) 34%, its shadow `#45362B` (L 23) 28%, mid granite `#84755E` 17%; the copper is verdigris `#98C9B1` (L 76, C 21), only ≈5% of the view but drawn as a continuous outline along the roof edges and the canopy fascia; the glazing reads `#5B4F3B` (L 34). Absent: any column order, pediment or polychrome; the ornament is concentric ribs and the material's grain.

**Hvitträsk living room and "dining room".** Log beams on the ceiling (≈20 round logs at even centres), walls in saturated blue plaster, a broad plank floor, an arched opening framed in white. A ryijy rug is laid across the floor and carried up over a long bench as one continuous textile. In the second room the plaster vaults are plain, and only the ribs and the edges of the arches carry painted vine ornament, in dark blue on cream: decoration follows the structure lines and leaves the fields empty. Window glazing is a fine grid. Palette (living room, k = 7): dark timber `#4F3623` / `#775637` ≈39%, near-black `#2A201B` 18%, white plaster `#D1D0D4` 11%. The blue wall is a large area but splits across clusters in this photo.

**Cranbrook School for Boys.** The tower over the court is an octagonal brick shaft ≈3.75 times as high as it is wide (base to dome top). It steps in once at ≈0.76 of its height and ends in a verdigris copper dome `#93BAB7` (L 73, C 14). Its windows are single tall, narrow round-headed slots, one per face. Low halls run off it with steep red tile roofs and small gables. Brick reads `#AF896F` (L 60) in sun and `#56473C` in shade. The quad gate gable rises at ≈36°. Its face is a tapering field of small round-arched blind niches: tiers of ≈6–7, ≈5 and 2 per side, shrinking toward the apex, around one tall central pointed niche that holds a bird sculpture. Brick courses alternate between projecting and recessed rows, and a soldier course follows the rake. The piers carry inset stone panels, ≈1:1.2 to 1:2 rectangles, stacked vertically. Gable palette: `#977E6F` 27%, `#795B4C` 24%, `#B4866E` 20%, niche shadow `#30221B` 13%. On one hall façade two six-window ribbons stack: the upper square-headed, the lower round-headed. Windows are ≈3.2× the width of the stone piers between them, and a band of blind arches in brick fills the zone between the rows. The visitor guide says each building on Academy Way has its own brick, door and window patterns. Dining hall: a white barrel vault on tall round-headed windows, a dark wainscot below ≈0.2 of the wall height, and in each window reveal a column of ≈5 small tile insets of rotated squares. The end window is a 5-light grid whose tiers shrink upward, with round-headed panes in every tier. The pendants hang on long rods: a repeated module of 3 stacked ribbed glass cones over one wide shallow bowl ≈1.6× the cone's width, repeated down the drop (by one account, Orrefors shades turned upside down). Hall palette: `#807769` 25%, `#665A4D` 21%, white vault `#9A989B`/`#D2DBEA` 31%, oak `#74543B` 4%.

**Saarinen House dining room (Korab photo).** The plan is square, made octagonal by four corner niches (guide). The rug is square with concentric octagons. The table has an octagonal base and a circular top. The chandelier is a hemispherical bowl hung from ≈7 twisted brass rods. The ceiling holds a shallow gilded dome in 3 stepped white plaster rings. The dome spans ≈0.77 of the room width in the photo. So the plan figure changes square → octagon → circle as the eye rises, which is what the museum text describes. Each chair back is a fan of 6 slightly convex pale veneer staves split by 5 black inlay lines. The back widens toward the top (≈1.33× the bottom width), and the lower edge is scalloped, one lobe per stave. The walls are veneer panels, ≈1:1.6 upright rectangles, joined by fine dark reveals. Each niche is a tall rectangle with a small stepped head that holds a lamp. The room is on an axis through the French doors to the octagonally paved courtyard and on through to the living room fireplace (both seen in the photo and the plan). Colour (k = 7): dark wood and shadow `#4E352C` 20% and floor `#171615` 14%, blue-grey rug `#334046` 18% with a lighter grey pattern `#6A7077` 14%, golden veneer `#A07D61` 15%, darker veneer `#76503C` 13%. Gold leaf reads `#917452` (L 51, C 24) over ≈47% of the dome crop. The Chinese red niche interiors `#541F23` (L 20, C 27) and the curtains `#723A35` are small areas; the niche lamps glow `#DE7850` (L 61, C 53), the most saturated point in the room. The red is held back into recesses and the brightest colour is light.

**Saarinen House living room, studio and plan (guide photos and drawing).** Living room: a long rectangle on one axis toward a fireplace faced in patterned grey-black Pewabic tile. A wall hanging is centred above it, with two torchères symmetric either side. The runner rug is cream with a fine fret border and a central spine of red brick-like blocks with 5 dark square "windows" stepped down its length. The guide says the rug echoes the outside brickwork and is set slightly off-centre on purpose, "making it seem less static". Palette (small scan, k = 7): dark floor `#553C2A` 23%, golden walls and veneer `#AC875C`/`#916237` 35%, cream rug `#C4AC81` 14%, rug red `#8E4A3A` 9%, lamp-lit orange `#C58844` 6%. Studio: a long, white, barrel-vaulted gallery with pendant bowls on the axis and a rug runner down the centre; palette pale `#EECF98` / `#FCECE0` ≈30%, ochre `#B19B48` 16%. Plan: a U of rooms wraps a square courtyard paved in an octagonal radial pattern of 8 sectors. A stepped walk enters on the main axis, and a covered walkway links the studio to the Academy.

**Rug No. 2.** The rug is 2.83 times as long as it is wide (110½ × 39 in; the image measures 337 × 950 px, which matches). It is mirror-symmetric left/right and also top/bottom about its mid-line. Across the width: a grey side band ≈16% each side, carrying 2–3 thin black lines; a beige field ≈64%; a pink central spine ≈21% wide. The spine is built from stacked figures with stepped outlines. Small triangular and stepped "trees" in teal and green sit on both sides, and zig-zag fields stand at each end. Teal end borders, with short combs of white teeth, close the top and bottom. A small "CA" monogram and "ES" initials sit in opposite corners, the one asymmetry. Palette (k = 8, rug only): beige field `#BDA37D` (L 68, C 23) 32%, grey bands `#756E5F` 21% and `#595144` 13%, tan `#A08662` 14%, grey-green `#7C8A7D` 8%, deep teal `#1A4250` (L 26, C 14) 6%, green `#496462` 5%. Pink spine sampled at `#B18362`–`#CB8563`. Chroma never rises above ≈25: the rug is low-chroma throughout, and the darkest teal works as the accent.

**Kingswood School.** From across the lake: long, low buff-brick wings under shallow hipped verdigris roofs. The roofs read `#B5CDC0` (L 79, C 9) with the sun on them, the lit brick `#E0BF6B`. Tall, slender chimneys stand at intervals, the only verticals. Building and reflection make a horizontal band ≈1/8 of the frame height. Stair (Brossard photo): treads, risers and the stepped stringer are clad in small square dark green-black glazed tiles (`#2A271C`, `#090D08` in shadow, together ≈65% of the frame). The stringer steps down in square notches, one per tread. Brass rails rise on paired square posts and end in a curled hook at the top. The upper wall is pierced by small square windows in deep plaster reveals, arranged in a stepped grid that climbs with the stair: rows of 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 4 from the top. Each window holds a leaded pattern of nested chevrons. Light enters only through these small squares (`#D6D4D1`, L 86, ≈3% of the frame), so the whole stair is lit by a pattern.

**Academy of Art, Art Museum and Library peristyle.** A flat-roofed rectangular frame in buff limestone (`#DBBDA0`, L 77, 24%) between two brick wings faced with stone pilasters. The front row has 4 square piers at equal centres (≈126 px apart, varying under 3%). Each pier is ≈13 times as tall as it is wide. The beam on top is ≈2× the pier width deep. A second row of piers stands behind, and the sky and trees read through all the bays (sky `#F7FAFE` 17%, shadow `#4D453E` 24%). The end bays are closed by solid stone walls, so the open middle is framed. In front, a round pool holds Milles's figures. Seen down the axis (Cranbrook178), the peristyle sits at the far end of a long, straight run of ≈4–5 shallow stepped pools and lawn, framed by clipped conical evergreens and rows of trees. It fills ≈14% of the frame width, a small, open, light frame closing a long, dark, planted approach. Absent: arches, ornament, the brick patterning of the earlier buildings.

**Hot water urn.** A near-perfect sphere (width:height ≈1.09 in the photo) on a short cylinder. The cylinder is ≈0.58 of the sphere's diameter, pierced all round by narrow vertical slots (≈14 visible on the front half). It stands on a thin flat disc. A cap seam cuts the sphere ≈1/5 down from its top. The finial is one tall upright bar, ≈0.6 of the sphere's diameter. Two straight square-section handles stick out sideways near the equator, and a lever spout sits low on one side. Every part is a primitive (sphere, cylinder, disc, bar), and the joins are plain seams, not mouldings. The slot rhythm of the base is the only ornament. The British Museum label notes that spinning and stamping made it, and that assembly was complex.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **One figure transformed through the scales** (Saarinen House dining room, plan; Helsinki). The octagon of the courtyard paving (8 sectors) returns in the rug's concentric octagons and the table base, then turns into the circle of the tabletop and the 3-ring gilded dome. The dome spans ≈0.77 of the room width. The figure does not change while you stay at one level; it changes as the eye moves up or along. At Helsinki, the arch figure repeats from the outer roof edge through ≈7 ribs to the inner window.
- **Strict axis, deliberate small deviation** (Saarinen House living room rug "slightly off center"; Helsinki tower off the axis; Rug No. 2's monogram in one corner only). Plans and rooms are symmetric on a main axis (dining room → courtyard → living room; Academy pools → peristyle). Then one element moves off it: a rug by a little, a tower by a whole wing.
- **Concentric stepping instead of a single edge** (Helsinki ≈7 receding ribs; dining dome 3 rings; Kingswood stringer notches; Cranbrook tower set-back at ≈0.76 height; Tribune entry stepped, by text). Edges are given as a sequence of parallel steps of equal small size, not one line.
- **Pattern fills structure, structure stays legible** (Cranbrook gable niches 6–7 / 5 / 2 per tier; brick bands between window rows; Hvitträsk ornament only on ribs). The pattern is in the material itself (brick courses, niches, knotted pile, leaded panes) and fits the field it fills, getting smaller as the field narrows. Each building gets its own pattern inside one family (guide).
- **Fine two-level grids** (Helsinki lunette 10 bays × ≈3 × 13 panes; Kingswood stair stepped window grid 2-2-3-4-4-4; dining hall end window 5 lights in tiers shrinking upward; veneer panels ≈1:1.6). A coarse structural grid always carries a finer one inside it.
- **Low-chroma warm field, small saturated points held in recesses or made of light** (dining room: fields L 24–57 with C ≤ 23; niche red C 27 inside recesses; niche lamp `#DE7850` C 53, the peak; Rug No. 2 all C ≤ 25 with deep teal as the dark accent; Helsinki granite C 21 with ≈5% verdigris outline). The strongest colour is in the smallest, most recessed or most luminous areas.
- **Verdigris copper as the one cool line on warm masses** (Helsinki roof edges and canopy `#98C9B1`, Cranbrook tower dome `#93BAB7`, Kingswood roofs `#B5CDC0`). Across 25 years and two continents, the cool colour is always copper at the top edge, L 73–79, C 9–21.
- **Primitive solids, repeated slot** (urn: sphere, cylinder, disc, bar, with ≈28 slots round the base; chair: 6 staves and 5 inlay lines; peristyle: 4 equal square piers). Later work strips the ornament down to repeated identical slots or members at an even pitch.
- **Long approach, small framed end** (Academy axis: peristyle ≈14% of view width at the end of ≈4–5 stepped pools; Helsinki: long low wings leading to the single arch; living room runner leading to the fireplace). Movement is directed along a long, quiet, repetitive run to one compact, richly treated goal.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Eliel Saarinen.** *Lay one axis to a compact goal seen from the first step, pass one plain figure down through every scale and change it by one step per level, rule every surface by symmetry and nested grids, then move exactly one element off the axis; the strongest colour goes to the smallest, most recessed places.*
Process:
1. Lay the axis and its goal. Draw ONE main axis through the view (and through the flow, when there are several views) and mirror the layout about it. Put the goal at its far end: compact, about 1/7 of the view's width when seen from the start of the approach, open in the middle and closed by a solid bay on each side, and visible from the first step. The approach is a long quiet run of 4–5 equal stages at one pitch (equal within 3%).
2. Rank the masses. One principal figure sits on the axis, about 1.4 times as wide as it is high. The flanks are low and long: the principal is about 1.8 times their height, and each flank is 5–6 times as long as it is high. At most one tall element exists, about 1.4 times the principal's height, about 3.75 times as high as it is wide, stepping in once at about three quarters of its height. Verticals are few and stand at intervals along the long horizontals.
3. Choose one plain figure and pass it down the scales. Take it from the case (the brand mark, the content's own shape). Use it unchanged across any one level, and change it by one operation between levels (add sides, round it, close it), so that three or four levels read as kin: site, room, object, detail. Centre the levels on the axis, stacked. The largest inner figure spans about 3/4 of its container.
4. Break the axis once. Move exactly ONE element off it: a small shift that is just visible (the works give no figure), a mark in one corner, or the one tall element set a whole flank away. Everything else stays mirrored.
5. Step the edges. Where the principal figure meets its field, replace the single edge with 3–7 parallel steps of one small equal size that follow the figure from base to base without a break. Lesser figures take 1–3 steps. No moulding and no added line: the step is the edge.
6. Nest the grids. Divide each surface into 4–10 equal bays on a coarse grid (openings about 3 times as wide as the members between them), and divide each bay again with a lighter, finer grid (about 3 across; many more along the long side). Where a field narrows, cut the count tier by tier (6–7, then 5, then 2) and keep the unit.
7. Make pattern from the unit the surface is built of, and put it on the structure. The pattern is the surface's own repeated unit arranged a different way, fitted to the field it fills. It goes on bands between rows, on ribs and edges and in narrowing end fields, and the large fields stay empty. Each section gets its own variant inside one family. Reduced mode (late works): no pattern at all, only one identical member or slot repeated at an even pitch (4 to about 28 times) on plain primitive forms.
8. Colour by inverse area. The field is one temperature family of 2–4 neighbouring tones at low chroma, within a band of about 27 L, with no pure extreme used as a field; the darks are the same family tinted, 13–35% of the view. Chroma rises only as area falls: a slightly stronger colour (about 2%) inside recesses; the peak (under 1%, about twice the field's chroma) only as a point of light inside a recess; a mid-chroma run of up to 9% is allowed only as a spine on the axis. One hue of the opposite temperature appears as a thin continuous line on outer or top edges, or as a border (5–6%).
9. Light from hiding. No source is bare: the brightest places are recesses that glow, and openings are small and patterned, so light arrives already divided (about 3% of a dark view can light it).
Why it works:
- A shape that returns at each scale groups the scales by likeness, with no line or label needed to say they belong together. Changing it one step per level also tells the viewer which level they are on (scale reference). Repeat it unchanged and it flattens into a stamp; use unrelated shapes and the view is a collection of parts.
- Symmetry makes a frame so regular that the eye stops reading it. Inside it a single departure is the one odd thing (salience by oddness), and it gives the composition a direction; the house guide says the offset rug makes the room "seem less static". Without the axis an offset is not legible as an offset. Two departures read as disorder, and none reads as a seal.
- One edge is a line. Several equal steps overlap like layers, so the boundary gains thickness (depth from overlap) and the parallel lines lead the eye inward to what the figure holds. Unequal steps would read as separate objects and break the gradient.
- A fine grid inside a coarse one fuses into tone at a distance, so the mass stays whole from afar and gives up detail on approach (fusion of fine detail into tone). Pattern made of the surface's own unit and placed on the structure lines makes the structure easier to read; the same pattern spread over the fields would hide it.
- Saturation and area trade off: a large calm field lets a very small intense point win. Placed in a recess or given as light, that point reads as depth and warmth in the room and not as paint on the surface. The thin line of opposite temperature outlines the mass against its ground without adding a second mass.
- A long even run adapts the eye to repetition, so the compact, richer goal gains by contrast with what came before (adaptation to the previous state). Because it is visible from the first step, direction is never in question; because its end bays are closed, the open middle is framed (figure and ground).
Colour relations: field family largest, one temperature, low chroma (the works stay at or under C 25), middle values in a band of about 27 L, the main mass about a third of a view and its shade about a quarter; darks tinted, 13–35%, pure black only in shadow; recess colour about 2%, dark, just above the field's chroma; peak chroma under 1%, about twice the field's, always light; axial spine up to 9% at mid chroma; one opposite-temperature hue as line or border, 5–6%, either the lightest cool note on a top edge or the darkest accent as a border.
Space and line: one axis, mirrored; goal about 1/7 of the view's width at the end of 4–5 equal stages; principal 1.4 : 1; principal to flank 1.8 : 1 in height; flanks 5–6 : 1; one tall element at 1.4 times the principal, set back at about 0.76; inner figure about 0.77 of its container; edges in 3–7 equal steps; coarse grid of 4–10 bays within 3%, opening to member about 3.2 : 1; fine grid inside every bay; counts fall as fields narrow; lines thin, of one weight, made by a change of surface and not drawn on top; panels about 1 : 1.6 upright.
Type requirement: text is the fine grid, so it must fuse into an even tone: one family of low stroke contrast and steady rhythm, running text in justified or mirrored blocks inside the bays. One title line only, in widely spaced capitals, set once across the full width of the principal figure and centred on the axis. The maker's mark is small and sits in one corner, where it may serve as the single departure of step 4. Tabular figures, so columns keep the coarse grid.
Motion and interaction: movement runs along the axis toward the goal and stages pass at one even pace; a stepped edge opens or closes one step at a time from the outside in (40–60ms per step; durations derived, not observed); moving between levels changes the figure by its one operation (300–400ms, ease-out); hover and focus raise the light inside the recess and add one step to the rim, with a focus ring that follows the steps; the ambient light may shift slowly with the hour. Reduced motion: cut to the end state.
Fails when: there is no single goal (dashboards and grids of peers have no end to the axis); the content cannot be mirrored (chat, feeds of uneven items, code editors); the screen is too small for 3 steps and a fine grid to resolve (steps under 2px merge into one line); several states each need a strong colour, since chroma is rationed to under 1–2%; the flow has one step, so there is no approach to adapt the eye; a dense table needs the bands between rows that the pattern would occupy.
Signature (do not carry together): hand-laid brick patterned with tiers of small arched niches · a verdigris copper line or cap on warm stone or brick · an octagon turning to a circle under a stepped gilded dome, or a giant round arch in concentric ribs.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Check-in flow for a budget airline, phone 390 × 844, stark cool white with cobalt ink and one yellow signal, fast and disposable (the works are warm, heavy and hand-laid).
1. *Axis and goal.* Vertical axis at x = 195. The goal is "Get boarding pass", a control 56px wide (0.14 of the width) at the bottom end of the axis, on screen from the first frame. It stands in an open bay 168px wide between two closed solid bands of 111px each. The approach is the 5 steps (passengers, seats, bags, documents, payment) as 5 rows at one 96px pitch, each mirrored about the axis: label left, value right, state mark on the axis.
2. *Ranks.* The goal bay is 172px high, 1.8 times a row; the rows are the long low flanks. The screen is too narrow for a third rank, so there is no tall element.
3. *Figure.* The brand's mark is a rounded bar. Level one, the screen's panels: corners at radius 0. Level two, the rows' state marks: radius a quarter of the height. Level three, the goal: radius half the height, a full round. The inner round spans 130px of the 168px bay (0.77). All three stack on the axis.
4. *Departure.* The running price tag sits 24px right of the axis on the rim of the goal bay. Nothing else leaves the axis.
5. *Steps.* The rim of the goal bay is 5 bands of 3px, each 3 L darker than the last toward the inside, running unbroken round the opening. Each row has 1 such step.
6. *Nested grid.* Five rows are the coarse grid. Inside each row a lighter 3-column grid holds its items as cells (three passengers, their seats, their bags).
7. *Pattern.* The unit is the item cell. The 16px bands between rows carry that cell rearranged, a different run for each step (single file, pairs, a stagger). The row fields stay empty.
8. *Colour.* Field: three cool whites, L 90–97, chroma under 6. Darks: cobalt-tinted ink, about 15% of the view. Recess: deep cobalt inside the goal bay, about 2%. Peak: yellow only as the glow behind the goal's label and on the current row's state mark, under 1%, each with a text label. Opposite temperature: one thin warm line along the top edge of the header and of the bay.
9. *Light.* Nothing is outlined in yellow. The current row is known by its glowing recessed mark and by its label, "now".
- *Motion.* On completing a row the next row's mark lights and the bay's rim closes inward one band at a time, 50ms each. Reduced motion cuts to the end state.
- *Type.* One grotesque in one weight for rows; the flight number once, in spaced capitals, across the full width of the goal bay.

Result: a cold, bright, throwaway check-in with no masonry, arch or warm tone in it. What survived is one axis to a small framed goal that is visible throughout, one figure rounding through three scales, a stepped rim, a single off-axis element, and chroma kept to the recess and to light.

## Space & composition
- Symmetric axial plans: the house is a U around a square court; the campus runs on axes between courts; the station is one central figure between equal wings.
- Mass hierarchy in simple ratios: Helsinki tower ≈1.4× the arch block, arch ≈1.8× the wings; wings ≈5.7× as long as they are high; Cranbrook tower ≈3.75:1, stepping in at ≈0.76 of its height.
- Enclosure then release: courtyards and quadrangles entered through gates; the peristyle as an open frame with closed end bays.
- Long horizontals with a few verticals: Kingswood's low wings with spaced chimneys; Academy pools with conical evergreens.
- Thresholds marked by sculpture (Lantern Bearers instead of columns, the bird in the gable niche, Orpheus in front of the peristyle), always on the axis.
- Interiors stack pattern on the vertical axis: rug figure → table → chandelier → dome, all centred.

## Form, line & typography
- The arch, the circle and the octagon are the main figures. The step (concentric ribs, set-backs, stepped niche heads, notched stringers) is the main edge treatment.
- Fans and staves: chair backs of 6 widening convex staves with scalloped feet; ribbed glass cones; slotted cylinders.
- Lines are drawn by contrasting material: black inlay between veneer staves, dark reveals between panels, copper outlines on stone. Line weight stays thin and even.
- Lettering: the Helsinki canopy carries widely spaced capitals in bronze ("RAUTATIEASEMA JÄRNVÄGSSTATION") in one line across the full width of the arch, on the axis. Monograms are small and in corners (Rug No. 2 "ES", "CA").
- Scalloped and festooned profiles soften long edges (Helsinki roof edge, the drawing's parapet, chair-back feet, gable rake niches).

## Rhythm, motion & time
- Even repeats at a steady pitch: peristyle bays equal within 3%; 10 lunette bays; Helsinki wing bays; 6 window lights per row at Cranbrook.
- Repeats that shrink toward a boundary or apex: gable niches 6–7 → 5 → 2; end-window tiers shrink upward; Kingswood stair windows widen downward 2 → 4.
- A modular stack repeated down a vertical (dining hall pendants: 3 cones + 1 bowl, then again).
- The body moves along axes: through a gate into a court, down a long runner toward a fireplace, up a stepped run of pools to an open frame. The goal is visible from the start of the approach.
- Light changes over the day are part of the design: the guide's book room for late-afternoon sun, French doors to the courtyard, torchères bounced off ceilings. Light is mostly indirect: glowing niches, uplights, small windows.

## UI translations
1. **Total design.** Derive every component from the page: one figure (octagon, arch or circle) appears in the page frame, the card, the control and the icon, changing one step per level (square → octagon → circle).
2. **Axis first.** Mirror the page about one centre line and stack hero, content and action on it; then set one element (a rug-like panel, a side tower) off the line.
3. **Long approach, framed end.** Lead to the primary action through 4–5 equal quiet sections; make the action compact (about 1/7 of the width), open in the middle and flanked by solid bays, and keep it in view from the top.
4. **Mass ranks.** A principal block about 1.4 : 1 between low flanks (1.8 : 1 in height, 5–6 : 1 long) and at most one slender tower-like column (about 3.75 : 1, stepped in at three quarters).
5. **Stepped portals.** Frame the main opening with 3–7 concentric equal steps of value instead of a border or a shadow; lesser frames take 1–3.
6. **Two-level grid.** A coarse grid of 4–10 equal bays, each subdivided by a lighter fine grid (like 10 bays of 3 × 13 panes); gutters about a third of the bay.
7. **Pattern on the structure.** Put pattern, built from the row or cell unit, in the bands between rows, on dividers and in tapering end fields, a different variant per section; leave the fields plain. Counts fall as a field narrows (6–7, 5, 2).
8. **Warm field, held colour.** Low-chroma warm mid-value surfaces (C ≤ 25); a deep red only inside recessed wells (about 2%); the brightest colour only as a glow (under 1%).
9. **One cool edge.** A thin light verdigris line along top edges and caps, or a deep teal border, at 5–6%, as the only cool hue.
10. **Lines by material.** Draw dividers as thin dark reveals or inlays between panels (about 1 : 1.6 upright), in one weight.
11. **Title on the axis.** One line of widely spaced capitals across the full width of the main figure; the product mark small, in one corner.
12. **Hidden light.** No bare bright element: states glow from inside niches and wells; focus adds a step to the rim. Steps open one at a time (40–60ms each); reduced motion cuts.

## What this artist would reject
- An object designed apart from its room, or a room apart from its site; nothing in Saarinen House is off the shelf except the Frigidaire, which was shown off on purpose.
- Plain, unpatterned surfaces on public masses where material could carry pattern (every brick façade has its own bond and niches).
- Large saturated colour fields; strong colour is kept in recesses, borders and light.
- A single hard edge where a few concentric steps could make the transition.
- Pure asymmetric free composition: there is always a governing axis to depart from.
- Direct bare-bulb light; sources are hidden in bowls, niches, stacked shades and torchères.

## Signature prompt seed
Mirror the view about one axis and lead along it, through four or five equal quiet stages, to a compact goal that is open in the middle, closed at the sides and visible from the start. Choose one plain figure and repeat it at every scale, changing it by one step from page to component to detail. Give the main opening a rim of several equal concentric steps instead of a border. Divide surfaces into a coarse grid with a finer grid inside each bay, and put pattern, made from the surface's own unit, only on bands, ribs and edges. Keep the fields low in chroma; let the strongest colour appear only in small recesses and as light, with one thin line of the opposite temperature on the outer edges. Then move exactly one element off the axis.
