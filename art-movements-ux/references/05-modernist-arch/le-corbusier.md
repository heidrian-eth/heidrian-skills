# Le Corbusier — Modernist architecture

## Essence
Le Corbusier treated a building as a measured system you move through: a body-based proportion scale (the Modulor), a structural grid that frees walls from loads, and a choreographed path (the *promenade architecturale*) turn white volumes into a timed sequence of views. Color and light are spatial tools that push planes forward, pull them back, or open a wall into a lantern.

## Key works examined
- **Villa Savoye, Poissy (1928–31).** White box on pilotis; a ramp rises from the hall to the salon, the roof terrace and a curved solarium. Teaches: one continuous path as the main structure. *(visually checked: Fondation Le Corbusier photos. North-west facade: the white box is 5.4:1; its ribbon opening is ≈16:1 (644 × 40 px), runs 87% of the facade width and is 29% of the box height. The recessed ground floor is dark green `#657E5E`, not umber, behind thin white pilotis; an interior corridor wall is deep blue `#01406C`. The roof solarium is one curved screen wall against the orthogonal box.)*
- **Five Points (1926).** Pilotis, roof garden, free plan, ribbon windows, free facade. Teaches: once structure is separate from skin, every partition is optional. *(pilotis, roof garden, ribbon window and free plan seen at Villa Savoye in the photos above.)*
- **Le Modulor (1948; Modulor 2, 1955).** A 1.83 m figure, 2.26 m with raised arm, 1.13 m navel; two golden-ratio series, red (navel) and blue (double the navel). Teaches: two linked scales instead of one linear scale. *(visually checked: Fondation Le Corbusier scan of the Modulor chart, and the 1951 Milan Triennale photo. Confirmed: figure 1829, raised hand 2260, navel 1130; the red series descends 1829 · 1130 · 698 · 432 · 267 · 165 · 102 · 63 · 39 · 24 · 15 · 9 · 6, the blue 2260 · 1397 · 863 · 534 · 330 · 204 · 126 · 78 · 48 · 30 · 18 · 11, drawn as two interlocking spirals of lenses beside a black silhouette `#110E1F`. The chart itself is printed in a pinkish red `#F72F56` and a clear blue `#0085DF` with one black rectangle, so the series are colour-coded, not just named.)*
- **Notre-Dame du Haut, Ronchamp (1953–55).** A south wall tapering from a point to about 3 m thick, pierced by irregular splayed openings with clear and red, green and yellow glass; a few-centimeter light slot under the heavy roof. Teaches: light as the event, mass as the frame. *(visually checked: Fondation Le Corbusier photos. Inside, the south wall reads dark; ≈13 splayed openings of unequal size, with bright white reveals, cover ≈15–20% of its area, and coloured glass is tiny: yellow `#DCC23A` ≈0.3% of the view, red glass `#A20302` and green `#43A007` only in individual windows. A thin light line separates wall and roof (under 1% of the frame height). Outside: rough white render `#E2ECF4` (sky-lit) under a dark brown concrete roof `#372E26`.)*
- **Pilgrims' house, Ronchamp (1950s)** *(added; visually checked: Fondation Le Corbusier photo)*: béton brut wall `#A09A9C` with window panels painted in flat blue `#0368BC`, yellow `#F1C539`, white and violet, several panels split on the diagonal into two colours. Teaches: Polychromie as flat panels set into raw grey, not white.
- **Polychromie Architecturale (1931, 1959).** 43 "purist" shades in 14 series, plus 20 stronger ones in 1959 (63 total), grouped into keyboards (*claviers*) to be played in combination. Teaches: a palette is a set of permitted chords. *(retry pass, visually checked: Fondation Le Corbusier scans of the Salubra Le Corbusier colour card (one strip of 14 chips, 32091 → 32080) and a Salubra keyboard page with its cut-out viewing mask. The chips run in families of 2–3 values of one hue, pale → deep: pink/red, three blues, three greens, three blue-greens, three oranges. The keyboard sets a band of 8 chips (greys, blues, an umber) between two large ground fields, and the mask isolates 2–3 chips against one field: a chord is "one big field + a few small values", not equal stripes.)*

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Le Corbusier.** *Measure everything on two interlocked scales taken from one body dimension, free the partitions from the structure, and string the views on one continuous path; colour then sets each plane's depth.*
Process:
1. Take one dimension from the user's body or reach (a target size, a line of text) and build two geometric series from it: ratio ≈1.62 per step, the second series exactly double the first, so each term of one falls between two terms of the other. The first sets internal spacing and type, the second structural gaps. No value outside the two series exists.
2. Lay a regular support grid (3–5 bays) and then place partitions without regard to its lines. The grid carries, the partitions only divide, so any partition may be moved or removed without touching the rest.
3. Detach the main mass from what carries it: a recessed band below it in the opposite value (main mass about 2× the band's height), or a thin line of light under the heaviest element (<1% of the height). Never a cast shadow.
4. Open each plane by 15–30% of its area, in one of two ways and never as an equal grid: one continuous strip (≈1:16, 85–90% of the plane's width) in a thin plane, or 5–13 deep openings of unequal size scattered in a thick plane of the opposite value.
5. Colour by depth, as a chord: one ground (≈50%) plus 2–3 small flat fields, or 2–3 value steps of one hue family. One flat colour per plane; cool and neutral planes go behind, the warmest and most saturated is nearest and smallest (from <1% up to ≈13%).
6. Order the views on one unbroken path that doubles back, so each turn reframes what was just seen from the other side. Stages are reached by travelling, never by a cut.
Why it works:
- Scale reference: when every size is a term of two series built on a body dimension, any two neighbours are in a ratio the eye has already met, so the whole reads as measured. A linear scale gives near-equal steps at the large end and arbitrary ratios between distant sizes.
- Depth from temperature, value and overlap: with structure and partition separated, a plane's position is free, and colour is the cheapest way to state it. Colour every plane equally and nothing advances; the plan goes flat.
- Continuity of path: a view reached by moving is located relative to the last one. Cut between views and the user holds a list of screens, not a building.
Colour relations: ground ≈50%, lowest chroma; a chord is the ground plus 2–3 small fields or 2–3 steps of one family (pale, mid, deep); saturated colour is rare by area (<1% to ≈13%); one flat colour per plane, no gradient across a plane; inside a thick opposite-value plane, colour shrinks to chips ≤1% set in the openings.
Space and line: two series, ratio ≈1.62, offset by 2×; support grid of 3–5 bays; main mass ≈2× its recessed base; strip ≈1:16 over 85–90% of the width, or 5–13 unequal openings; openings 15–30% of their plane; at most one curved partition, and only against an orthogonal frame (one work).
Type requirement: sizes are consecutive terms of the first series, so type and spacing are one scale (a size step is always ≈1.62×; intermediate sizes do not exist); one plain face that survives large jumps in size; stage labels numbered along the path.
Motion and interaction: movement follows the path (translate along a shallow incline, 500–700ms, near-linear) and is replaced by an instant change under reduced-motion; hover and focus add light to an opening and a visible outline, never size; light itself may drift over 10–20s, never in response to input.
Fails when: users must jump between views at random and at speed (the path becomes delay); the content needs fine, even gradations of size (dense tables, code: 1.62× steps are too coarse); the content is n equal items, which no opening rule or chord can rank; small openings are used as targets under 44px.
Signature (do not carry together): a white box on thin columns · one long strip window · primary-coloured flat panels set in raw grey concrete.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Le Corbusier's procedure → a CI pipeline run viewer in dark mode (desktop, 1440 × 900), dense, utilitarian, slate and green-grey.
1. *Scales.* Body dimension: the 20px log line. Series A (internal): 5 / 8 / 12 / 20 / 32 / 52 / 84. Series B (structural): 10 / 16 / 24 / 40 / 64 / 104 / 168. Nothing else is allowed; the linter rejects 14 and 18.
2. *Grid and partitions.* Four support bays of 336px with 24px gaps. The log panel spans 2.6 bays, the stage summary 1.1 bays; neither edge sits on a bay line, and the summary can be closed without the log moving.
3. *Lift.* The log panel (520px high) stands on a recessed band 260px high, one value step lighter than the panel (the scheme is inverted, so the base is lighter). A 40px gap in the ground value separates them. No shadow anywhere.
4. *Openings.* The run timeline is one strip across the top panel: 1240 × 78px (≈1:16, 86% of the width, 26% of that panel's area). The left rail is the thick plane, the lightest surface on the screen: 9 job tiles of unequal size (from 52 × 52 to 168 × 104px, all above 44px) cover 18% of it.
5. *Chord.* Ground: dark slate, 52%. Receding: a cooler blue-grey for the log panel and base. Advancing: one warm amber field, 3% of the screen, the failed stage, the nearest and smallest plane. Passed, running and queued are three value steps of one green-grey family, each also carrying a glyph and a word, so state is never colour alone. Inside the rail tiles, status is a chip ≤1% of the rail.
6. *Path.* Stages run left to right along the strip, then the path turns: the selected stage's jobs run right to left beneath it, then its log runs down. Selecting a stage travels along the strip (600ms; instant under reduced-motion) instead of swapping the panel.
- *Type.* 12 / 20 / 32 / 52 only. The stage number is 52, the job name 20, log text 12 on a 20px line.
Result: a dark, dense engineering console with no white, no columns-as-motif and no primaries. What survived is the double scale, partitions that ignore the support grid, a lifted mass, the two opening types, colour as depth, and one doubling-back path.

## Space & composition
- **Grid as structure, not as walls.** Villa Savoye sits on a regular column grid (cut from 5 m to 4.75 m to save money), while the walls move freely around the columns. A strict 4-column grid carries the content, and panels ignore column edges. One curved partition, like the solarium, meets the orthogonal frame.
- **The piloti lift.** The main volume floats above a recessed ground: a visible gap plus a darker, set-back base band, never a drop shadow. At Savoye that base is dark green, and the white box is roughly 2× the height of the visible green band.
- **Ribbon over punch.** Openings are long horizontal strips, not portrait holes. Measured at Savoye: ≈1:16, across 87% of the facade, about 29% of the box height (the earlier 1:6–1:8 was too squat).
- **Dual-scale spacing.** Red series in px (rounded): 6, 10, 16, 26, 42, 68, 110. Blue series: 12, 20, 32, 52, 84, 136. Red sets tight internal spacing and blue sets structural gaps. Never use a value outside these two scales.

## Form, line & typography
Orthogonal volumes cut by one free curve; thin columns against thick slabs. Le Corbusier's drawings and signage used stencil capitals (engineer lettering with broken strokes). Map it to a geometric sans for body and stencil caps for labels and numbers, tracked about +8%.
- **Jost** (Futura-like geometric sans) for body and UI.
- **Big Shoulders Stencil Display** or **Stardos Stencil** for section numbers and wayfinding labels.
Set type sizes from the red series (10/16/26/42/68), so the type scale and the spacing scale are the same scale.

## Rhythm, motion & time
Motion follows a ramp, not an elevator: translate on a 6–8° diagonal with near-linear easing (`cubic-bezier(.3,.1,.3,1)`), 500–700 ms. Each stage reveals a framed view, as the Savoye ramp turns back on itself to reframe the landscape. Light moves slowly. On Ronchamp-style screens, colored glints drift over 10–20 s as ambient motion, never as a reaction to clicks.

## UI translations
1. Build all spacing and type from two Modulor-derived px series (red and blue), stored as CSS custom properties. Ban all other values.
2. Run a fixed 4-column structural grid, but let panels sit off the column lines. Make exactly one panel a curved shape (a large `border-radius` on one side only).
3. Raise the primary content block on a recessed base band in dark green `#657E5E` (or ombre `#372E26`), with a blue-series gap of 32–52 px between them. No shadows.
4. Replace hero images with one ribbon at about 1:16 spanning ~87% of the panel width and ~30% of its height, framing a horizontal view.
5. Make navigation a promenade: one continuous scroll path whose sections switch back (left, right, left), each revealed by a ramp-angle transition.
6. Give each surface exactly one flat Polychromie color. Use blue or gray for anything meant to recede and a single vermillion plane for the nearest element.
7. Build a "thick wall" sidebar (about 30% width) in a dark tone, pierced by 5–13 openings of unequal size (thumbnails or CTAs) covering 15–20% of it, with bevelled light inner borders. Colour is a tiny chip inside a few openings (red, green or yellow, ≤1% of the sidebar), not a tint over all of them.
8. Separate the header from the body with a light gap only 4–6 px high in `#FDF5EE`, so the top bar seems to float the way Ronchamp's roof does.
9. Hover: increase light, not size. Brighten the tint of the hovered opening and leave its dimensions alone.
10. Use a free facade: each breakpoint gets its own composition based on orientation, not a stacked copy of the desktop layout.

## What this artist would reject
- 8-pt spacing systems with no relation to the human body.
- Gradients and glassy blur that leave a plane with no one color.
- Equal-weight card grids where nothing advances or recedes.
- Elevator UX: hard page cuts and modals that teleport the user with no path.
- Decorative drop shadows imitating mass, instead of real lift and void.
- Portrait-punched images scattered without a structural grid.
