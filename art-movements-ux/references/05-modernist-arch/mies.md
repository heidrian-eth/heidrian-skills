# Ludwig Mies van der Rohe — Modernist architecture

## Essence
Mies drew a strict, regular structural grid and then placed a few free-standing planes inside it, so that space flows around them and is never boxed into rooms. The minimal form doesn't come from being cheap. It comes from extreme precision, and from saving the luxury for a few large, perfectly finished surfaces: onyx, travertine, bronze, primavera wood. In his work the frame is the ornament and the material is the color.

## Key works examined
- **Barcelona Pavilion, 1929 (rebuilt 1983–86):** a regular grid of chrome-clad cruciform columns with freely placed planes between them. Content can float off the grid. *(visually checked: Fundació Mies van der Rohe photos of the reconstruction. Chrome cruciform columns read as mirrored vertical stripes ≈5% of the frame width; a white plaster ceiling `#F3F0E9` takes ≈30% of interior views as a luminous lid; one wall is lit milk glass that glows white; travertine floor `#E0D9C6` shows a fine grid of joints. Besides the onyx, walls of dark green-black veined marble `#424C51` (near-black `#1E2123`) frame the inner and outer courts.)*
- **Golden onyx wall, Barcelona Pavilion:** the single book-matched slab of Atlas Mountains onyx that the plan turns around. One precious surface anchors the whole composition. *(visually checked: Fundació Mies van der Rohe photos. The onyx is amber-brown `#AD7A3A` / `#7E5118` with cream-yellow veins, darker than the assumed golden `#C9A25E`; the slabs are book-matched so the veins mirror across visible seams (2 rows × 4 slabs). In the main interior view it is ≈44% of the frame width and ≈12–23% of the area, and it stands on a black carpet `#2E3234` that covers ≈12% of the view.)*
- **Farnsworth House, 1951:** a white steel floor slab and roof slab raised 5'3" above the floodplain, with an off-center wood core. The base is the hero, and the one opaque block sits off-center. *(visually checked: Edith Farnsworth House site photo. The house is two thin white bands (roof fascia ≈14%, floor fascia ≈11% of the house's visible height) with clear glass between, ≈4.6:1 overall, occupying only ≈54% of the frame width in a landscape that fills the rest; the lower terrace slides left of the house. The wood core reads warm brown `#5E4C33` through the glass.)*
- **Seagram Building and plaza, 1958:** a 515 ft tower set 100 ft back from Park Avenue behind a granite plaza. Spending space on emptiness is how prestige reads. *(visually checked: secondary source, archdaily.com AD Classics: Seagram Building, colour aerial view, Ezra Stoller b/w views, lobby photo)* Seen: a plain slab (≈1:3.5 in elevation) set back behind an open plaza with two symmetrical rectangular features, framed by taller, paler neighbours that crowd the street line. The tower is the darkest object in the view: brown-black glass and bronze (median tones L*≈11–37), against white and pale-blue glass neighbours. At night (Stoller) it reads as an even lattice of lit cells.
- **Seagram bronze I-beams:** non-structural, purely expressive mullions that stand in for the hidden frame. Structure is used as rhythm and ornament. *(visually checked: same ArchDaily photos, at building scale only)* Seen: the front carries ≈30 equal vertical modules across its width, running unbroken from the plaza canopy to the dark top band; no horizontal accent at all except the solid service floors at the crown. Blinds stopped at random heights make a scattered warm patchwork (≈9% light tan) inside a strictly regular grid. The I-beam profile itself is not resolvable at this scale, so the bronze hex stays unverified.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Space** (Seagram): one simple slab, set back, with an empty forecourt; contrast with the neighbours is by absence (the gap of the plaza) and by value (the dark tower among pale ones).
- **Colour relations** (Seagram): almost monochrome dark warm brown/bronze (≈90%); the only variation is a scatter of lighter tan cells (blinds, lit rooms, ≈9%) inside the regular grid; no accent colour.
- **Line and edge:** ≈30 equal verticals across the face, unbroken full height; edges of the slab perfectly straight; one dark band at the crown.
- **The procedure:** take one regular module and repeat it uniformly over the whole face; make the object darker and plainer than its context; give up the front ground as an empty plane; let any variation come only from the contents behind the grid, never from the grid itself.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Ludwig Mies van der Rohe.** *Make the system so regular that it disappears, so attention falls on the space flowing between a few free planes and on the one surface where all the richness is spent.*
Process:
1. Fix one module and repeat it across the whole format without exception (equal intervals, unbroken from edge to edge). Every gap is a whole multiple of it; the single permitted fraction is a quarter module, used only where an edge overhangs its last support.
2. Give up ground before anything is shown: in the first view the object takes about half the width (≈54%) and the rest is empty, plain surface with one name and one way in. The emptiness is spent, not left over.
3. Lay the two largest fields as a pair of plain, parallel bands at one end of the value scale (≈30% of the view each, the only texture a fine grid of joints on the module). Everything else stands between them as an island.
4. Stand 2–4 planes free between those bands: different lengths, each stopping short of the frame at both ends, sliding past one another so the way between them narrows and widens. No four planes ever close a box. Put the one dense, opaque block off-centre (about 45/55).
5. Spend the richness once: one plane (12–25% of the area, up to ≈45% of the width) carries the highest chroma and the only strong texture, mirrored across its seams. Seat it on a plain field at the opposite value extreme from the bands, about half its area (≈12%).
6. Let variation come only from the content behind the grid: cells may differ in what they hold (≈10% of cells at most stand out in value), but no line, interval or edge of the grid itself ever changes.
Why it works:
- Grouping by likeness: a perfectly uniform repeat fuses into a single surface and stops being read line by line, so the eye is released to the exceptions. Vary the module anywhere and the structure becomes the subject.
- Figure and ground with no enclosure: a plane that stops short at both ends is read as an object in continuous space, and the gap beside it as a way through. Close the corners and the space becomes a set of boxes with doors.
- Salience by oddness, bought with restraint: the rich surface is valued against ≈60% of plain field. A second rich surface halves the first and both read as decoration (stated in the texts; seen in one building).
Colour relations: no applied accent; each surface quality has one job. Plain bands ≈60% (two fields, one value extreme, lowest chroma) : rich plane 12–25% (peak chroma, mid value, strong texture) : opposite-value plain field ≈12% : translucent or reflective planes take the rest. As an object in a context, be the plainest and furthest in value from the neighbours (one work).
Space and line: one module; overhang exactly 1/4 module; object ≈54% of the first view's width; lid and floor ≈30% each; free planes of unequal length; core at 45/55; one line weight, thin, constant and straight, crossing at right angles with no joint detail; reflective supports ≈5% of the width.
Type requirement: one face of constant stroke with no flourish and tabular figures, in few sizes; text hangs between the grid lines with baselines on the module, so a table shows the module without drawing it; labels may be capitals with slight extra spacing.
Motion and interaction: the planes stay and the viewpoint moves (lateral translation, 400–600ms, no scaling, no overshoot; planes at different depths move at 0.3–0.5× the scroll rate, switched off under reduced-motion). Content is revealed by sliding a plane aside. A component's states are positions on the module, never free sizes; focus is a full-weight outline, not a colour shift.
Fails when: content lengths are irregular and cannot sit on one module (user-written text, mixed media feeds); the format is too small to give up ground or to let planes stand free (a phone column turns every plane into a box); the first view must be dense (operational dashboards); several items each claim the rich surface.
Signature (do not carry together): a veined amber stone wall on a black carpet · mirrored cross-shaped columns · a dark metal-and-glass tower lattice behind an empty plaza.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Mies's procedure → a transit departures board in saturated line colours on near-black (wall display, 1920 × 1080), dense, public, read at 5m.
1. *Module.* 60px. 32 intervals across, 18 down, drawn nowhere but obeyed everywhere: every row is 60px, every column edge sits a whole number of modules in, and the board's right edge overhangs its last column by 15px.
2. *Give up ground.* The departures table takes 1020px (53% of the width). The remaining 47% is empty near-black, holding only the station name and the clock: no adverts, no weather, no ticker.
3. *Two plain bands.* A header band and a footer band, each 300px high (28%), both at the dark extreme, plain, with the 60px joints visible at 4% contrast. The table and the map stand between them.
4. *Free planes.* Three: the table (17 modules long), the service-notice plane (7 modules), the platform map (11 modules). None touches the left or right frame; the notice plane slides 2 modules past the table's end, so the gap between them narrows from 4 modules to 1. The table is the dense core, centred at 45% of the width.
5. *Richness once.* The platform map is the only surface with saturated colour and texture: every line colour appears here and nowhere else as a field, 18% of the area, its track pattern mirrored about the station's centre line. It sits on a pale grey slab (the opposite extreme) of 9%.
6. *Variation from content.* All 9 rows are identical in height, type and rule. Only delayed services differ: their time cell is one value step lighter and carries the word "delayed" and the new time, 1 row in 9 on a typical board. Line identity in the table is a letter in a square cell plus the colour, never colour alone.
- *Type.* One constant-stroke face, two sizes (120px times, 60px destinations), tabular figures, 7:1 contrast.
- *Motion.* A departed row slides out sideways over 500ms and the rows above step down by exactly one module; under reduced-motion the list simply updates.
Result: a dark, colourful public sign. No stone, no chrome, no luxury. What survived is the exceptionless module, the spent emptiness, planes that stop short, richness in one place, and variation only inside the cells.

## Space & composition
- **The grid is a grid of supports, not of walls.** At Farnsworth, eight columns at 22 ft centers make three bays; the roof cantilevers about 5.5 ft (a quarter bay) past the end columns. Let the outer edges overhang the grid by a quarter column. *(Confirmed in the Farnsworth photo: column spacing 148 px, roof end 37 px past the last column = 0.25 bay.)*
- **Offset planes, not enclosures.** At Barcelona the walls slide past each other, so the circulation path narrows and widens. Panels never close into four-sided boxes. In the photos the onyx wall stops short of the glass on both sides, so space passes around it.
- **Lid and floor.** The flat white ceiling and the travertine floor are the two largest surfaces (≈30% each in interior views); walls are islands between them.
- **One opaque core, off-center.** The Farnsworth core (13 × 25 ft) sits slightly north of center in a 55 × 28 ft room. Put the dense element (nav, editor, data table) at about 45/55, never dead center.
- **The plinth.** Both houses sit on a raised travertine base, reached by a few steps. Give the page a distinct base band that the content "stands on."
- **Setback as luxury.** The Seagram plaza gives up ground-floor value for an empty forecourt. Give the first 30–40vh to a quiet surface with a single name and one entry point.
- **Measured void.** Every gap is a multiple of the module, never leftover.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

Mies used no applied color. Color comes from materials, and each material has one job. Hexes sampled from Fundació Mies van der Rohe (Barcelona) and Edith Farnsworth House photographs; travertine and white shift with daylight (cool `#CED1CE` outdoors, warm `#E0D9C6` inside).

| Hex | Role |
|---|---|
| `#E0D9C6` | Travertine. Base canvas and plinth (Barcelona floor, interior light) |
| `#F3F0E9` | White: Barcelona ceiling / Farnsworth enamel (sky-cast `#D1D9DE` outdoors). Structural lines, frames, the lid |
| `#AD7A3A` | Onyx (amber-brown, veins cream-yellow, shadow `#7E5118`). The single feature surface (one per screen) |
| `#424C51` | Verde antico marble (reads almost black-green-grey, near-black `#1E2123`, white veins). Deep secondary panel or the selected state |
| `#5B4632` | Seagram bronze (unverified; nearest seen: Farnsworth wood core `#5E4C33`). Mullions, rules, active text |
| `#6B7A6C` | Grey-green tinted glass. Translucent overlays (with backdrop-blur) |
| `#2E3234` | Black (Barcelona carpet). Text, and one large dark floor field |

Logic: about 60% stone and white (floor plus ceiling), one veined onyx hit of 12–25% (book-matched, mirrored veins), one dark field (carpet, ≈12%), glass and chrome for the rest. Two onyx surfaces read as decoration.

## Form, line & typography
Mies's line language is the steel profile: thin, constant-width, perfectly straight, with crisp right-angle crossings like the cruciform column and no visible joints. The type should feel engineered, with even strokes, open apertures, and no flourish. Use a strict scale (14/18/28/56) and all-caps labels tracked +0.08em.
- **Inter Tight** or **Archivo** for UI and headings (neutral, grotesk, steel-like).
- **IBM Plex Mono** for numerals and data, where the grid needs to show.

## Rhythm, motion & time
The experience is a walk past planes, not a series of screens. Motion should feel like your viewpoint shifting while the architecture stays put: panels translate slowly in parallax (0.3–0.5× scroll), and nothing scales or bounces. Easing: cubic-bezier(0.4,0,0.2,1), 400–600ms. Reveal content by sliding a plane aside, as though a wall moved, never by fading in from nothing. On hover, a rule warms from bronze to onyx and does not grow.

## UI translations
1. Lay the page on a visible but faint column grid (1px `#5B4632` at 8% opacity). Hang content between the lines, not on them.
2. Build at most three free-standing panels per view. Give each a different width (for example 5, 3, and 7 columns) and offset them vertically so the gaps between them become paths.
3. Make exactly one surface per screen onyx-textured (a hero card, primary CTA field, or featured record). Everything else is stone or glass.
4. Run thin vertical bronze rules every 64px in headers and tables as non-structural "mullions." Do the same for rhythm in lists.
5. Raise the main content on a plinth: a 1px top border plus a 24px-taller travertine band below.
6. Put the dense functional core (sidebar, editor) off-center at 45/55. Never split the screen in two equal halves.
7. Limit component states to a few fixed positions (e.g. closed, half, open) so the composition always holds. *(unverified as a Seagram rule: the photos show blinds stopped at random heights, not three states; this is a prescription, not an observation.)*
8. Use translucent grey-green glass (`backdrop-filter: blur(12px)`, `#6B7A6C` at 30%) for overlays, so the plane behind stays legible. One panel may instead be "milk glass": an evenly lit near-white `#F1EFE6` surface with no texture.
9. Navigate by sliding planes horizontally, not by modal pop-ups. Space should flow from one area to the next.
10. Treat the empty hero as the plaza: one wordmark, one entry action, and 40vh of quiet stone. Don't put cards there.
11. Give corners `border-radius: 0` and hairline borders of 1px. Use a 2px rule only on the single structural element.
12. Set the onyx surface on a dark field: a near-black band `#2E3234` under or beside the feature panel (≈half its area), as the pavilion's black carpet sits under the onyx wall.
13. Mirror the onyx texture across one vertical seam (book-matching) so the pattern is symmetric while the layout is not.

## What this artist would reject
- Rounded, shadowed cards stacked into equal boxes. These are enclosures, not planes.
- Gradients and accent colors used for "brand energy" instead of real material differences.
- Ornament that hides the system: illustrations, decorative blobs, icon confetti.
- Centered, symmetrical hero blocks with three equal feature tiles underneath.
- Components with unlimited visual states that break the facade's uniformity.
