# Luis Barragán — Modernist architecture (Mexican)

## Essence
Barragán builds emotion from a few plain things: a high blank wall, one saturated colour, a slot of light, the sound of water. His spaces shut the world out and then ration what gets back in, so moving through them becomes a slow sequence of thresholds (dark, then coloured light, then open sky). Modernist abstraction is pushed toward ritual, and what you feel is serenity: in his words, "in my fountains, silence sings."

## Key works examined
- **Casa Luis Barragán, Mexico City (1947–48; UNESCO 2004)**: entry by compression. A bare, dim foyer leads to a high vestibule with yellow light and one fuchsia wall, then past a low threshold to a double-height white living room. The roof terrace walls frame only the sky. *(visually checked: Barragan Foundation photos. Roof terrace prints show lilac-pink #D5AFC7, red-violet #874A7C and orange #D59B7A walls against white and a grey concrete plane; no blood red seen. The stair hall print shows pale pink walls #D7C3B9, basalt steps #5B5D5C and a gold-leaf square (Goeritz) at about 1/4 of the wall width; the "fuchsia" vestibule wall reads pale in this old print, so its saturation is unverified.)*
- **Capuchin Convent Chapel, Tlalpan (1954–c.1963)**: light you never see the source of. Yellow glass turns orange walls hotter, and an orange cross painted the colour of the wall seems to dissolve. *(visually checked: Barragan Foundation photos. The ochre-orange wall #C08047 fills about 40% of the nave view; a gold altar triptych (#FAF398 where lit) is the only bright element at about 10%; a backlit lattice of yellow glass ramps from #F4BB3D to #B45D3B on the walls.)*
- **Cuadra San Cristóbal, with Andrés Casillas (1966–68)**: one long pink wall splits the site roughly 1:3. Portals are sized to a horse and rider, and a purple wall, a pink granary and a water spout make a composition of planes at landscape scale. *(visually checked: Barragan Foundation photos. The long walls are a pale pastel pink #F1D2D2, not fuchsia; the strongest colours are the smallest planes: terracotta fountain wall #BC512D ≈5% and violet trough wall #754268 ≈2% of the view below the sky. The pool doubles every plane in reflection. The 1:3 site split could not be measured from the photos: unverified.)*
- **Casa Gilardi (1975–77)**: a 10 × 35 m lot organised around an existing jacaranda. The corridor has yellow walls, ceiling and glass and opens onto an indoor pool with a blue back wall and a magenta freestanding plane lit from a hidden skylight. *(visually checked: Barragan Foundation photos. Pool room: plaster 44%, cyan-blue wall #0492D1 18%, magenta plane #E74889 8%, floor 25%. The magenta plane is 18% of the frame width, set at 38–56% across, and rises into the skylight slot in front of the blue wall. The corridor is not flat yellow paint but light: a ramp from #FBDA41 at the ≈12 glass slots through #D87A2A to near-black #2C1715, ending in a small blue-and-pink portal about 13% of the frame wide.)*
- **Torres de Satélite, with Mathias Goeritz (1957–58)** *(added; visually checked: Barragan Foundation photos)*: five triangular-plan concrete prisms of different heights (tallest ≈2× the shortest) standing free on an empty plaza. Colours seen: terracotta red #9B5236 (the tallest), blue #4C92BB, yellow-ochre #D1A157 and two pale green-white towers #BDDEC1. Board-marked horizontal texture on every face. Teaches: several saturated planes can coexist when each is a separate, free-standing object with open space between.
- **Pritzker acceptance speech (1980)**: a vocabulary of beauty, silence, solitude, serenity, joy, myth and the garden "poetic and mysterious."

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Luis Barragán.** *Shut the world out, then ration what returns: one colour, one light and one opening per room, each felt against the room before.*
Process:
1. Enclose. Remove everything that belongs to another room (global chrome, cross-links, status). What remains is 4–6 large planes, and nothing small carries colour.
2. Sequence before composing. Order the views as rooms and set each to compressed (about 1/3 width, dim, low) or released (full, bright, tall). Alternate them, and make the step extreme: at least 2× in width and in luminance.
3. In each room assign the quiet field (35–45%, lowest chroma) and a ground plane (≈25%, the darkest and the only textured one).
4. Add one or two colour planes, each a whole surface edge to edge, in different hues: the farther one larger and cooler (15–20%); the nearer one narrow (5–10% of the area, ≤1/5 of the width), the warmest and most saturated, overlapping the far plane and standing on the seam between colour and quiet field.
5. Light it from a source the viewer cannot see, from one direction: let the light grade across a plane, spill a faint tint of the near colour onto its neighbours, and split a plane into two values of one hue instead of adding a hue.
6. Cut one opening sized to the hand in a plane at least 10× its size. End each passage with a small view (about 1/8 of the width) of the next room, in the opposite temperature.
Why it works:
- Perception adapts. After a dim, narrow, colourless stretch the same room reads brighter, larger and more coloured, so the feeling is made by the difference between consecutive states, not by any single screen.
- Saturation and area trade off. A large saturated field tires the eye and goes flat; a narrow one beside a large quiet field and a cooler plane stays vivid. Warm, narrow and overlapping in front of cool and large stacks three depth cues with no shadow.
- Colour that covers a whole plane, and light with no visible source, read as properties of the space and not as objects in it. The only object left is the opening, so it reads as the way through.
Colour relations: quiet field 35–45% (lowest chroma) : dark textured ground ≈25% : far plane 15–20%, cool, mid chroma : near plane 5–10%, warm, peak chroma; adjacent planes clash in hue and meet at a hard edge with no line; second tones come from value (shadow), never from a new hue; a gradient exists only as light falling off from its source.
Space and line: no drawn lines; every edge is two planes meeting; divisions off-centre near 1:3; near plane ≤1/5 of the width; opening to plane ≥1:10; compressed to released ≥1:2.
Type requirement: small, calm, low-detail letters set wide apart, placed like the opening (text height ≤1/40 of its field); one size tier for labels; reading text in a narrow measure on the quiet field; the face must stay legible small and letterspaced.
Motion and interaction: slow and directional. A transition is a threshold (≈200ms narrow and dim, then 700–900ms widen and brighten); ambient light drifts ≤3% in value over a minute; a state change recolours or relights a whole plane; nothing small moves.
Fails when: the task needs everything at once (dashboards, comparison tables); users jump non-linearly at speed, so thresholds become delay; colour lands on small components; more than three coloured planes or equal saturation everywhere; the compression is timid.
Signature (do not carry together): rough pink or magenta stucco walls · yellow light through glass · courtyard water and framed sky.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Barragán's procedure → a dark-mode music player (phone, 390 × 844). Unchanged from the approved pilot, section C2.
1. *Enclose.* No tab bar, no global search on either screen. Two rooms: Library and Now Playing.
2. *Sequence.* Library is compressed: content column 72% of the width, rows dense, text at L 55 on a ground of L 10, small type. Now Playing is released: full bleed, peak luminance at least 2.5× the library's, type in one small tier inside large fields.
3. *Fields (Now Playing).* Quiet field: a charcoal with a green cast, 40%, top of the screen, carrying only the track title in small tracked capitals. Ground plane: the darkest value, 25%, at the bottom, the only textured plane (queue and lyrics as fine text).
4. *Colour planes.* Far plane: deep muted teal, 18%, holding the album art desaturated to about 40% and tinted toward the teal so it cannot become the saturation peak. Near plane: a narrow amber slab, 18% of the width and 8% of the area, the most saturated thing on screen, overlapping the teal plane by 15% and standing on the seam between teal and charcoal. It holds the transport.
5. *Light.* The amber slab is lit from above the screen edge (its top 20% is two steps lighter, no glow object). It spills an 8%-opacity amber wash across the nearest 15% of the teal and charcoal. Paused, the slab drops to the shadow value of the same amber, not to grey.
6. *Opening and preview.* One control sized to the thumb (60px) sits in the slab, alone. In the Library, the mini-player is the preview: a strip 13% of the height, the only warm thing in a room lit by a dim cool gradient that falls off from the top.
- *Motion.* Library → Now Playing: 180ms in which the list narrows and dims, then 800ms in which the preview strip widens and brightens into the room. Over a track, the slab's lit edge descends by 3% in value.
Result: a nocturnal charcoal, teal and amber player. No pink, no plaster, no daylight. What survived is enclosure, compression before release, whole-plane colour with the most saturated plane the narrowest and nearest, and sourceless light.

## Space & composition
- **The wall is the subject.** Façades show almost nothing to the street. Each room is a box of 4–6 planes. Usually one or two carry colour, and when there are two they are different hues (Gilardi: blue wall plus magenta plane).
- **Compress, then release.** Low, dark and narrow first, then tall, bright and open. The ratio between the two is extreme: Casa Barragán goes from a squat foyer to a double-height room.
- **Asymmetric division.** A wall cuts the field off-centre (San Cristóbal is about 1:3 by texts; *unverified* in the photos). Openings are rectangles cut into large planes, never grids of windows.
- **Freestanding planes.** At Gilardi a magenta wall stands in the pool and rises into the skylight slot, which hides the light source and creates depth through overlapping layers. It is narrow (18% of the frame width) and stands in front of the larger blue plane.
- **Shadow as a second tone.** On the roof terrace a hard diagonal shadow cuts the pink wall into two values of one hue (#D5AFC7 lit, #7C6290 shade). The light makes the second colour, not paint.
- **Reflection band.** At San Cristóbal the pool doubles the walls; the water strip takes about 20% of the frame height and repeats the planes upside down.
- **Void as programme.** The courtyard, the pool and the sky framed by walls are the main rooms. Density sits in tiny pockets: a chair, a gold panel, a spout.
- **Human or animal scale tied to monumental scale.** One opening sized exactly to the body (horse plus rider) sits next to planes much larger than the body.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

Paint is applied as a flat field across a whole plane, never as a highlight. Adjacent planes clash (pink beside violet beside terracotta, magenta beside blue) instead of harmonising. The most saturated plane is usually the smallest (Gilardi magenta 8%, San Cristóbal terracotta 5%, violet 2%), while the big field is a quiet tint (pastel pink, plaster, ochre). Coloured light (yellow glass at Gilardi and Tlalpan) is the one exception to flatness: it makes a glowing ramp from bright yellow to orange to near-black. All hexes below are sampled from Barragan Foundation photographs, many of them old prints, so pinks and yellows depend on light and print ageing.

## Form, line & typography
The forms are orthogonal blocks with sharp arrises and rough stucco texture (visible in every close photo: Gilardi's magenta plane, San Cristóbal's walls; Torres de Satélite shows horizontal board marks instead). There are no curves, mouldings or visible joints. The few lines are thresholds and shadow edges. The type should echo that: wide, sturdy, calm grotesques with almost no detail, set large with very generous tracking, plus a humanist serif for the rare poetic phrase. Suggested fonts: **Archivo** (wide or expanded widths for display, like a monumental inscription), **Work Sans** (body, plain and warm), **Cormorant Garamond** italic (one quiet line, used sparingly). Headings sit in the field like a portal in a wall: uppercase, small, widely letterspaced. Avoid stacked hierarchies.

## Rhythm, motion & time
Time is sunlight moving across a wall. Motion should be slow, ambient and indirect. A colour field can drift through a few lightness steps over minutes. A soft "light shaft" gradient can move across a plane as you scroll. Transitions work like doorways: the screen dims and narrows for about 200 ms, then opens to the next view over 700–900 ms with `cubic-bezier(.2,.7,.1,1)`. There should be no bounce, no stagger cascades and no parallax confetti. Water appears only as a slow, faint, low-amplitude ripple or reflection at the base of a coloured plane. Under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`, drop the ambient drift, light shaft and ripple and replace the doorway transition with a short plain cross-fade.

## UI translations
1. **Layout:** Make 35–45% of each screen a single flat, uninterrupted quiet field (plaster, pastel pink or ochre), and put content in one rectangular "portal" cut off-centre, around the 1:3 line (the 1:3 figure is from texts; unverified in the photos).
2. **Layout:** Start every flow with a compressed screen (narrow max-width of about 28ch, dim background, small type), then release into a full-bleed spacious view. Enforce the contrast between the two.
3. **Color:** Allow one to three saturated planes per viewport, each a full panel edge to edge in a different hue, and make the most saturated one the smallest (5–10% of the screen, e.g. a narrow magenta panel against a larger cyan one). Never use a saturated pill, chip or underline.
4. **Color:** Tint neighbours with light. Give the panel next to a yellow block a `#FBDA4114` wash so the colour seems to spill like light through glass. A corridor-like flow may use the one permitted ramp: `#FBDA41` → `#D87A2A` → `#2C1715`, as light, never on a painted plane.
5. **Layering:** Use a freestanding plane, an offset card with no border, radius or shadow, that overlaps the hero by about 15% and hides the image's edge, the way Gilardi's magenta wall hides the skylight.
6. **Type:** Set headings in Archivo Expanded, uppercase, `letter-spacing: .25em`, at 12–14 px inside huge fields. Let quiet type in a vast plane do the monumental work.
7. **Light:** Light a key panel from an unseen source: an off-canvas `radial-gradient` that brightens one edge, with no visible icon or glow object.
8. **Motion:** Slow down navigation by default: a 700–900 ms crossfade-and-widen between sections, and an ambient lightness drift of no more than 3% on the hero over 60 s. Under reduced motion: no drift, and a ≤200 ms cross-fade.
9. **Interaction:** Hide the next section behind a threshold, a full-height colour plane the user has to cross, with the next content revealed only through a narrow vertical slot that widens on scroll.
10. **Interaction:** Hover and focus states change the colour of a whole plane (such as a row's full background) instead of adding borders or icons.
11. **Seclusion:** Keep the chrome walled off. No sticky header, no visible nav until the user asks for it. The screen is a courtyard, not a street.
12. **Scale:** Size one control exactly to the body, such as a 56 px tap portal, and place it in a field more than 10× larger.
13. **Light:** Split one coloured panel with a hard diagonal "shadow" into two values of the same hue (e.g. `#D5AFC7` / `#7C6290`) instead of adding a second colour.
14. **Sequence:** For a passage between sections, repeat narrow vertical light slots along one side and end on a small coloured portal (about 13% of the width) that previews the next room's colours.

## What this artist would reject
- Dashboard walls of equal cards, the opposite of a single framed void.
- Gradients, drop shadows and glassmorphism that blur the edge where two planes meet.
- Rainbow accent systems and colour as decoration on small elements.
- Everything visible at once: mega-menus, sticky banners, "open-plan" pages with no thresholds.
- Snappy, bouncy, attention-seeking motion and notification noise.
- Curved, bubbly, rounded-everything forms.

## Signature prompt seed
Design this screen as a walled Mexican courtyard. Make 35–45% of it a single flat plaster-white or pastel-pink field with rough, unshadowed edges. Cut one off-centre rectangular portal into it, around the 1:3 line, to hold the content. Let one to three saturated planes (magenta #E74889, cyan #0492D1, violet #874A7C or terracotta #BC512D) meet the quiet field at hard edges, with the most saturated plane the smallest, and yellow #FBDA41 appearing as light that spills onto its neighbour. Use small, widely tracked Archivo Expanded capitals. Enter through a narrow, dim screen before opening wide. Make transitions slow and doorway-like, and keep everything silent and secluded.
