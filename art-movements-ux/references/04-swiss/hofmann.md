# Armin Hofmann — Swiss International Typographic Style

## Essence
Hofmann made graphic design out of two forces set against each other: a hard, still geometric sign (a dot, a bar, a sans-serif letter) placed next to something soft and moving (a blurred halftone body, a gestural line), almost always in black and white. Unlike Müller-Brockmann's mathematical grids, his grid flexes. The tension between opposites does the communicating.

## Key works examined
- **Graphic Design Manual: Principles and Practice, 1965** *(retry pass, visually checked: Niggli edition cover and six spreads; the light-blue-on-black cover is the current edition's)* (Niggli, black and white throughout; revised 1988). Chapters run Dot, Line, Confrontation, Letters and Signs, and each moves from abstract exercise to applied work. It teaches that a whole visual language can be built up from the smallest units.
- **Giselle, Basler Freilichtspiele, 1959** (offset litho, 127.6 × 90.2 cm). A near-abstract halftone dancer against rigid type. The dot of the "i" rises above the letter height to echo her knee. It teaches the organic-versus-geometric pairing. *(visually checked: secondary source, peoplesgdarchive.org)* Seen: black ground ≈67%, paper-white ≈18%. The title runs up the left edge, rotated 90° to read bottom to top, its lowercase letters filling ≈32% of the width and the full height, set tight; the i-dot is detached and pushed out past the letters' outer line into the black, at ≈47% of the height, right where the dancer's blurred tutu reaches toward the type. The photo takes the right ≈2/3, a motion-blurred grey-white figure bleeding off the top, right and bottom edges. A five-line flush-left info block sits top left in small regular grotesk. No colour.
- **Junge Holländische Bildhauer, Kunsthalle Basel, 1960.** A huge hand-cut linoleum "h" over condensed wood type. It teaches a single glyph scaled until it becomes architecture. *(not obtained; the same move was checked on the Stadttheater 1960/61 poster below)*
- **Stadttheater Basel season posters, 1960s** (the 1968 sheet is a 128 × 90.4 cm lithograph). They carried a fixed blank box, upper right, where the weekly programme was pasted, so the poster lived all season. It teaches the permanent frame with a swappable data slot. *(retry pass adds the 1962 ear-and-eye and 1963/64 hands sheets, from the Lars Müller Publishers "Armin Hofmann" spreads; first pass: 1960/61 and 1965/66 sheets, Cooper Hewitt; the 1968 sheet was not obtained)* In **1965/66** the slot is not a closed box: one **L-shaped hairline** (a vertical from the top margin and a horizontal at 43% of the height) fences off the area from 62.5% of the width to the right edge, so the poster edges close it. In **1960/61** the slot is **inside a giant black "T"** cropped by the top and right edges (bar from 33% of the width to the edge, stem to ~60% of the height, black = 31% of the sheet), with the notice reversed white in the stem.
- **Stadt Theater Basel 1965, the "feets" poster.** A ballet slipper against a cowboy boot. It teaches that meaning comes from putting two cropped fragments side by side, without illustration. *(visually checked: the 1965/66 season poster, Cooper Hewitt)* Five cut-out photo fragments (boot with spur, pointe shoe, column base, cello, music stand), all cropped by the sheet or by each other, on bare paper; "Stadt Theater Basel / 1965 66" is set **flush-right** against a thin diagonal slash.
- **Added: Robert Jacobsen / Serge Poliakoff, Kunsthalle Basel, 1958; Stadttheater Basel 1962 and 1963/64; Schweiz kleines Land grosse Landschaft, c. 1965** *(retry pass, visually checked: Lars Müller Publishers spreads)*. Jacobsen/Poliakoff: a flat red triangle (≈45%) thrust into black (≈50%), condensed type on the red, dates top-left. 1962: an ear above an eye in coarse grainy halftone, white ground, "Stadt Theater Basel" small at the right edge. 1963/64: white hands on black (black ≈70% of the sheet), tiny rust "63 64" and title. Schweiz: a snow landscape reduced to black-and-white masses, title bottom-centre. All keep the type small (≈1/30–1/40 of the sheet height) against one dominant image mass.
- **Wilhelm Tell, 1963.** Univers 57 condensed in perspective, with the numeral "1" replaced by a capital "I". It teaches glyphs bent to fit the system. *(visually checked: secondary source, fontsinuse.com)* Seen: the date line reads 'I5.-3I. VIII I963', so the 1s are plain vertical strokes like a capital I, in a condensed grotesk; the info block sits top centre and 'Wilhelm Tell' top right. The image is a soft airbrushed apple filling the middle ≈60% of the sheet, grading from black to white, with its stalk at the top and a hard black arc (a head) along the bottom edge. TELL is cut out of the apple in white blocky letters that step back in perspective, each letter smaller and offset up and to the right. Hard type and soft grey are again the only contrast; no colour.
- **Dot/line studies** (Manual, *retry pass, visually checked*): a 6×6 field of dots whose diameter grows row by row (and the same with squares, and triangles that grow and turn); stochastic dot fields fading from solid to bare paper; a black disc cut into parts and recombined into nine figures; rows of dashes whose density accelerates left to right; a row of vertical lines stepping from heavy bars to hairlines; letter studies of crossing line bundles. Every study is black on white in a square frame, with the caption in a narrow column beside it. Thérèse Moll's knocked-out lattice itself was not among the spreads seen (*unverified*). They teach rhythm through gradation and interruption.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Space** (Wilhelm Tell): one soft central mass; hard elements only at the edges (text top, black arc bottom) and cut into the mass (TELL), so geometry and atmosphere meet inside the image.
- **Space** (Giselle): two vertical zones: a rigid type column on the left third and a soft photographic field on the right two-thirds; the one meeting point is the i-dot, pulled out of the type column toward the image.
- **Colour relations** (Giselle): black / paper only, black ≈2/3; the photo supplies the only greys; the brightest marks are the letters and the dot.
- **Line and edge:** type edges razor-sharp, the figure blurred and grainy: hard versus soft is the whole contrast.
- **The procedure:** split the format into a hard typographic band and a soft image field; rotate the title to run the full height of its band; let exactly one glyph part escape the band toward the image where the two meet.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Armin Hofmann.** *Oppose one hard, still sign and one soft, moving image at extreme scale, let them touch at exactly one point, and give changing information a fixed slot so the opposition never has to move.*
Process:
1. Reduce the surface to two values, a dark and a ground, and decide which dominates (dark 31–70% of the area). Every grey that follows must come from inside the image, never from a tint.
2. Cast two bodies of opposite character: one hard (a letter, numeral, bar or dot: flat, razor-edged, still) and one soft (a continuous-tone image: blurred, grainy or graded). Give them unequal zones, about 1/3 to 2/3, neither centred, each cut by at least two edges of the format.
3. Enlarge one body until it is architecture: 50–70% of the height or more, with all running text at 1/30–1/40 of the height. Nothing sits in between.
4. Make ONE contact: move a single part of the hard body out of its zone toward the soft one, or cut the hard form into the soft mass, at the place where the two come closest (about mid-height). Exactly one, and nothing else leaves its zone.
5. If information changes over time, fence a slot for it before placing anything else: about 1/3 of the width by 40% of the height in an upper corner, closed on two sides by rules and on two by the format edges, or cut as a window into the hard body with the text reversed. The slot's content swaps; its position never does.
6. Hue is all or nothing: none; or under 1% as a few small characters; or one flat plane at full scale (≈45% against ≈50% dark). Never a medium-sized accent.
Why it works:
- Contrast of kind beats contrast of amount: a sharp flat edge beside a blurred graded mass makes each more itself (edge contrast, figure and ground), so two values are enough. Make both hard and it is a diagram; both soft and nothing holds the format.
- One escaped part reads as intention because everything else obeys its zone (salience by oddness); it joins the two bodies into one event. Two escapes read as loose alignment.
- A slot whose place is constant is found by position, not by search, and the composition does not reflow when the data changes. A floating data block makes the big sign negotiate with it every week.
Colour relations: dark and ground only, in a 30:70 to 70:30 split; greys 0–25%, all inside the image and with highlights that are the bare ground; the brightest marks are the type and the escaped part; hue as step 6.
Space and line: two zones near 1:2; the large body reaches 60–100% of the height and 2/3 of the width; text 1/30–1/40 of the height; two rules at most, both belonging to the slot; edges of the format do the closing.
Type requirement: one bold, regular-width face that stays clear when set small and reversed, and whose letters are simple enough to work as shapes at 60% of the height; one line may run rotated along an edge, full height; figures that can be redrawn as plain strokes.
Motion and interaction: the hard frame is still and only the soft layer moves (drift or blur ≤600ms). The slot changes by hard cut. Selection is the escaped part re-used: a solid mark that leaves the text line, plus a weight change; the mark is 8–12px but the target is the whole row, at least 44px high. Progress is a row of bars stepping from thin to thick, moved one step at a time.
Fails when: there is no image and no single sign worth enlarging (settings, forms, long tables); several items need equal weight; the small text must sit over the soft image (contrast cannot be guaranteed, so keep text on the flat dark or the ground); colour coding is a requirement.
Signature (do not carry together): a giant black letter cropped by the sheet · a grainy black-and-white photograph · a detached dot.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Tram departures for one stop, in two greens (phone, 390 × 844; deep bottle green and pale mint, rounded sans).
1. *Two values.* Bottle green 62%, mint 38%. No third tint; the only mid-tones are inside the live street image.
2. *Bodies.* Hard: the line number "11", flat mint, in the left 34% of the width. Soft: a long-exposure image of the stop, mint-on-green, grain visible, filling the right 66% and cut by the top, right and bottom edges.
3. *Scale.* The numeral runs up the left edge rotated, 100% of the height; stop name and times are 22px (1/38 of the height). No mid-size headings.
4. *Contact.* The flag of the second "1" is cut loose and pushed 28px right, into the image, at 46% of the height, where the tram's light trail comes nearest. It doubles as the "tram approaching" mark.
5. *Slot.* Upper right, from 62% of the width to the edge and from the top to 40% of the height, fenced by one vertical and one horizontal 1px mint rule. Next three departures, reversed mint on the flat green (not on the image). It swaps by cut every 20s.
6. *Hue.* None on the normal screen. On disruption the whole soft zone is replaced by one flat amber plane (46%) with the notice set on it in green.
- *Selection.* Choosing a departure moves a 10px solid dot out past the row's left edge and sets the row bold; the row is 48px high.
- *Motion.* The image drifts 4px over 600ms on refresh; the numeral and slot never move.
Result: a two-green transit screen. No black, no paper, no theatre. What survived is hard against soft at 1:38 scale, one contact, and a slot that never moves.

## Space & composition
- **Two-body compositions.** One soft mass (photo, blur, gesture) against one hard type cluster. Neither is centred, and they meet at a tense, near-touching edge.
- **Flexible grid.** Columns exist but give way when clarity demands it.
- **Extreme scale jumps.** A single letter or dot can take 50–70% of the sheet height while the text sits at roughly 1/40 of that. Confirmed on the 1960/61 sheet: the "T" runs to ~60% of the height, its bar spans 67% of the width (≈4 of 6 columns), and it bleeds off two edges.
- **Bare paper is a material.** The whites in *Giselle* are unprinted paper: an active field the black pushes against.
- **The fixed data slot.** A permanent frame with one window for changing information, placed as part of the design.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

The theatre posters carry almost no hue: black (or a warm sepia-black) photo and type on paper, and the 1963/64 sheet adds only its season numerals "63 64" in a rust brown `#754835` (<1% of the sheet). But red is real: the Kunsthalle Basel poster *Robert Jacobsen / Serge Poliakoff* (1958, retry pass) is one huge flat red triangle `#DA042B` (≈45% of the poster) driven into a black field (≈50%), with condensed black type set on the red. So the hue rule is: none, a rust numeral, or one red plane at full scale, never a medium red accent. Hofmann reacted against the "trivialization of colour" in postwar advertising and said colour photography was too simple and too brilliant to be used correctly. His colour was precise, rare and treated as a language. The default is value contrast: black against paper, with halftone greys between.

| Hex | Role |

| `#070906` | Ink: type, solid dots and bars (the 1960/61 "T") |
| `#4A4B42` | Dense halftone: photographic shadows, secondary masses |
| `#8B877C` | Light halftone: disabled, meta, motion-blur trails |

## Form, line & typography
The vocabulary is point, line and plane: perfect circles, and uniform bars that are graded or interrupted. Type is a grotesque, set flush-left and ragged, and sometimes rotated. In the two checked theatre posters it is a **bold regular-width grotesque** (not condensed), set small; "Basel" runs rotated 90° under "Stadttheater" (1960/61), and the 1965/66 block is flush-right against a slash. Letters are shapes first, so a counter, a dot or a stem can be enlarged until it reads as an image.
Fonts: **Archivo** (variable width axis; condensed widths stand in for Univers 57), **Anton** (for the Schmalfette wood-type punch), and **Inter Tight** for small text.

## Rhythm, motion & time
The Manual's line studies are motion diagrams: bars that thicken, thin or space out to suggest speed. In UI, motion should look like gradation, not easing flourishes. On fast transitions, leave a trail of 2–3 grey ghost copies at falling opacity. Keep the geometric frame still while the "soft" layer moves, the way the type in *Giselle* holds still as the dancer spins. Swap the data slot with a hard cut, never a fade.

## UI translations
1. Build every key screen as two bodies: one large soft element (a blurred or high-contrast photo, a gradient-free grey mass) offset against one tight block of type. Never centre either.
2. Default to pure value contrast: ship the whole UI in `#0B0B0B` on `#F3F1EA` plus two greys, and earn any hue with a single spot accent no larger than 3% of the screen.
3. Turn one glyph into the hero: set a single initial or numeral at `font-size: 60vh`, cropped by the viewport edge with `overflow: hidden`.
4. Design a permanent shell with a fixed "programme slot" top-right where live data swaps in, so the composition never reflows. Fence it with one L-shaped 1px rule (left and bottom only, the viewport edges closing it), about 1/3 of the width by 40% of the height, or cut it into the hero glyph itself with reversed text.
5. Run a 6-column grid but let the hero span 4.5 columns and bleed off the opposite edge, and allow text to hang outside the grid when that reads better.
6. Render photos as grayscale cut-outs with highlights left as bare background (`filter: grayscale(1) contrast(1.4)`, subject masked, no rectangle). A coarse, grainy dot screen is confirmed on the 1962 Stadttheater sheet (ear and eye, retry pass); the earlier scans read as continuous tone.
7. Make loading and progress states into graded line rhythms: 12 bars whose weights step from 1px to 8px, animated as a travelling wave.
8. Mark selection with a solid black dot (8–12px) set slightly above the cap height, Giselle's oversized i-dot, instead of a filled pill or background tint.
9. For empty states, show Moll's lattice: a grid of black bars with the missing cells knocked out to `#D9D6CE`, so the absence of data is drawn.
10. Set headings in a condensed grotesque, flush-left with tight tracking (`-0.02em`) and leading at 0.9, and rotate one per view 90° along an edge.

## What this artist would reject
- Cheerful multi-hue palettes with no meaning behind them.
- Centred, symmetrical heroes on soft gradients.
- Rounded cards and drop shadows standing in for figure/ground contrast.
- Decorative illustration and literal stock imagery.
- Timid type scales where nothing is enormous and nothing is tiny.
- Choices justified by taste rather than correctness.

## Signature prompt seed
Design this screen as a Basel poster by Armin Hofmann: warm paper-white field, black ink, two halftone greys, and no hue except one tiny red signal. Compose with two opposing bodies: a huge cropped geometric sign (a single letter or perfect dot at 60% of viewport height) against a coarse, near-abstract halftone photo, offset asymmetrically on a flexible six-column grid. Use condensed grotesque type, flush-left, set tight and small, and keep one fixed rectangular slot for live data. Show motion only through graded line rhythms and ghost trails, with no rounded cards and no shadows.
