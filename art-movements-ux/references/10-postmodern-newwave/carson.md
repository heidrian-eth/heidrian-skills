# David Carson — Grunge / Postmodern typography

## Essence
Carson treats a page as something you look at all at once, like a painting, before you read it in lines. Type works as image and texture, and the layout responds to how a piece *feels*, not to a grid. He rejects the idea that legibility and communication are the same thing: a page can be hard to read and still say a lot, or very easy to read and say nothing. Each layout is built by eye from cropping, collision and found scraps.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**David Carson.** *On a surface meant to be looked at before it is read, let one display element carry the feeling of the piece as an image, against a quiet ground and square small text (expressive surfaces only).*
Process:
1. Fix the scope first. Take only a decorative or editorial surface: a cover, an opener, a hero, a poster, an empty state, a share card. Navigation, forms, data, errors and running text are outside the procedure and keep the product's plain kit.
2. Read the piece, name its feeling in one word, and choose ONE disturbance to carry it: crop (the frame cuts the display word, losing up to a third of its letterforms), collide (the word lies on the focal area of the image), turn (one word at ≈20° or 90°) or compress (lines nearly touch, gap ≤0.1 cap height). One at full strength, a second at most, and not the one the previous piece used.
3. Quiet the field: reduce the image to one ink or bleach it until 25–60% of the surface reads as ground.
4. Set the display word at the scale of the image's subject (30–100% of the width), touching or overlapping the image. No margin between them.
5. Hold everything else still: text that must be read stays at 0°, at least 10× smaller than the display word, gathered in one or two corners, with the opposite region left empty.
6. Add one spot ink on 2–5% of the surface (one word or the title). Where pale type crosses a pale image, give it a hard copy offset ≈0.5% of the width.
7. Supply the plain duplicate: the disturbed word also exists as real text (the surface's heading for assistive technology, and a legible line in the corner cluster).
Why it works:
- A cropped or collided word is taken first as a shape and then completed by the reader (closure), and that small effort is the engagement. It works only for a short, familiar or duplicated word; on a long unfamiliar sentence closure fails and the content is lost.
- Against a quiet ground and square small text, one disturbance is the odd thing and reads as meant. Disturb everything and no single move reads as a decision: it is noise.
- Type that overlaps the image fuses with it into one figure. A margin turns them back into a picture and its caption, which is the template the reader has learned to skip. Repeating the same move on every piece does the same thing by habituation.
Colour relations: ground 25–60% at one extreme of value; the image in one ink, sharing the ground's value at its edges; display type at the other extreme of value, or reversed out; spot 2–5%; type crosses the image only at display size and at ≥3:1 where it lies (or with its offset copy); small text never lies on the image. Collage mode: two saturated inks at 15–20% each round a monochrome centre.
Space and line: no shared template between pieces, but each surface has a corner cluster, an empty counter-region and one frame edge that cuts something; turned type is display only.
Type requirement: two to four faces that differ in kind, not in family size (one compressed heavy, one high-contrast serif, one plain small face); the display face must stay recognisable with a third of its letterforms gone; the small face is plain and ≥12px.
Motion and interaction: the disturbance is composed, not animated. Moving from one piece to the next is a cut; nothing jitters, flickers or scrambles. The disturbed layer is not a control and has no hover behaviour; links and buttons on the surface come from the plain kit, level, ≥44px, outside the collision zone. Each breakpoint gets its own crop.
Fails when: it reaches navigation, forms, checkout, settings, errors, tables, dashboards or body text longer than a paragraph; the display word is long, unfamiliar or translated (a crop tuned to one string breaks in the next language); a feed repeats one disturbance on every card; the layout reflows freely and the crop is left to chance.
Signature (do not carry together): a masthead cut by the frame over a bleached photograph · distressed mixed faces and overprinted wood type · torn-paper collage.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Carson's procedure → the monthly summary opener of a dark-mode personal finance app in navy and slate (phone, 390 × 844).
1. *Scope.* Only the opener panel: the top 46% of the screen (390 × 388px). The tab bar, the account switcher, the category table and every button below it use the plain kit, untouched.
2. *Feeling and move.* The month ran over plan: "tight". The disturbance is compression. No rotation and no crop; February used a crop, so it is not repeated.
3. *Field.* The spending curve as a filled silhouette in one slate ink (L 30) on a navy ground (L 10); 55% of the panel stays ground.
4. *Display.* "MARCH" over "OVER", two lines of 150px caps with a 12px gap (0.08 of the cap height), 96% of the width, laid across the peak of the curve.
5. *Still.* "€212 over plan, 1–31 March" at 13px and 0° in the bottom-left corner; the top-right third is empty. 150px against 13px is 11.5×.
6. *Spot.* A pale coral on the word "OVER" only: 4% of the panel. Contrast with the slate is 9:1, so no offset copy.
7. *Duplicate.* The panel's real heading is "March: €212 over plan"; the display layer is hidden from assistive technology.
- *Motion.* None. Swiping to February is a cut. The display word does nothing on touch.
- *Next month.* Under plan, "loose": the move changes to one word turned 20° on a wider empty field. The table below does not change at all.
Result: one dark, tense panel above an ordinary ledger. No white stock, no photograph, no distressed type. What survived is one disturbance chosen from the content, a quiet field, square small text and a plain duplicate.

## Space & composition
- **No grid, but a decision on every spread.** He has never used grids. In UI, keep a hidden 12-column grid and break it only on hero and editorial surfaces.
- **Collision instead of separation.** Type sits on the photo, not beside it ("Water" on the face, "David Bowie" on the neck). Photos covering copy and text running into the gutter were not seen in the checked pages, so the 10–25% overlap figure is unverified.
- **Uneven density.** Micro text clusters in one corner (the Bowie cover's caption at bottom-left) while large areas stay blank: 26–62% of the checked magazine pages are near-white. Aim for a few crowded spots in a lot of open ground, not even padding.
- **Crop past comfort.** Cut faces at the eyes and headlines halfway through the cap height. Let `overflow: hidden` cut a word in two at the viewport edge.
- **Rotated type.** The rotations seen are large: ≈20° on a display word (Beach Culture) and 90°/180° letters in the prints. A body column tilted 2–3° was not seen. Body text stays square.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

Color in Carson's work comes from print: white stock, duotone and bleached photos, small spot inks and offset copies. Grounds read clean white (#F9F9F8), not newsprint. Spot colour is used small (2–5% of the page: a blue masthead, a sage offset, an orange tracked word), while the collages and prints switch to saturated red, magenta and plum.
- `#F9F9F8` white stock ground (background; 26–62% of the pages checked)
- `#010103` toner black (type, drip lettering)
- `#A89C91` warm photo grey (duotone mid-tones, secondary text)
- `#C65930` rust/spot orange (letterpress ink; tracked accent word)
- `#264863` ink blue (Beach Culture masthead; no cyan seen)
- `#AFC2AF` sage offset (Ray Gun's ghost copy; no acid yellow-green seen)
- `#FA0001` collage red and `#C21765` magenta (nucollage only)
- `#65343C` plum ink (letterpress prints)

Logic: images are monochrome, duotone or bleached, and type sits on them with one pale offset copy. Use `mix-blend-mode: multiply` only where inks overprint (the prints), and keep editorial screens mostly white.

## Form, line & typography
Several faces per spread; sizes changed mid-word; reverse leading so ascenders and descenders collide; extreme letterspacing beside type squeezed to nothing; dingbats used as text. Scale jumps are huge: a 9px caption sitting against a 400px fragment of a letter.
Fonts: **Anton** (condensed, compressible, for jammed display type), **Special Elite** (typewriter-and-photocopy texture for captions), **Bodoni Moda** (high-contrast serif to set against the grunge, set huge and cropped).

## Rhythm, motion & time
The pace is jumpy, like flicking through TV channels, never smooth. Use hard cuts (`steps()` easing, 0ms crossfades) rather than 300ms ease-out slides. Have elements arrive slightly late and slightly off their final position (2–6px jitter, then snap). On scroll, let layers slide past each other at different speeds so overlaps re-form as you read. Each section gets its own entrance.

## UI translations
1. **Layout:** On editorial and landing screens, set the headline on the image and crop it mid-letter at the frame edge. Rotate one display word ≈20° and keep body columns at 0°.
2. **Layout:** Make one text block break its column by 1.5× and run into the gutter or off-canvas, with `overflow-x: clip` on the section.
3. **Type:** Stack display lines so they nearly touch (gap ≤0.1 of the cap height, about `line-height: 0.85`), with mixed sizes inside one headline (for example, 180px / 48px / 220px words).
4. **Type:** Put two to three faces on a hero, all sharing one weight-contrast idea (heavy condensed, typewriter, hairline serif).
5. **Color:** Run photos through `filter: grayscale(1)`, a duotone or a bleach (`contrast(.8) brightness(1.2)`), and give the type on top a hard pale copy offset ≈0.5% of the width (`text-shadow: 3px 3px 0 #AFC2AF`).
6. **Texture:** Torn-paper `clip-path` edges on collage images; uneven letterpress inking (a grain mask at 6–10%) on display type. Keep the ground white.
7. **Motion:** Use hard-cut transitions between sections (`animation-timing-function: steps(3)`) with a brief offset jitter before elements settle.
8. **Interaction:** On hover, pull a headline apart by letterspacing (`letter-spacing` 0 to 0.4em) or swap it briefly to a dingbat or glitch version, then restore it.
9. **Interaction ("Ferry move"):** Let an editorial "verdict" scramble a teaser into symbols, with a clear "read plainly" toggle that shows clean, accessible text.
10. **Usable in UI:** marketing heroes, editorial features, music/fashion/event sites, 404 and empty states, splash screens, share cards.
11. **Not usable in UI:** forms, navigation, checkout, settings, error messages, data tables, body copy longer than a paragraph. Leaving out headings, covering text or keeping typos there breaks wayfinding and WCAG. Keep real semantic text in the DOM and apply the distortion through CSS or `aria-hidden` decorative layers.
12. **Rule of thumb:** At most one fully illegible element per screen, and it must be decorative or have a legible duplicate.

## What this artist would reject
- Identical card grids in which every story gets the same template, no matter what it says.
- Letting the software decide: default type scales, auto-layout spacing, layouts produced from presets.
- One tasteful sans-serif in three weights as the whole personality of the site.
- Smooth, generic ease-in-out fades on everything.

## Signature prompt seed
