# Wolfgang Weingart — Swiss Punk / New Wave typography

## Essence
Weingart took the Basel School's own tools (Akzidenz-Grotesk, the grid, the rule, black and white) and pushed each one past its tolerance: letterspacing until words became pictures, text blocks stepped down like stairs, films stacked until halftone dots turned into texture. It is rebellion from inside the system, not outside it; he held that you could only break typographic rules after mastering them. The result looks chaotic, but every piece was set, measured and spaced deliberately by hand.

## Key works examined
- **Round Compositions, 1962** — a dropped case of 6pt Akzidenz-Grotesk tied in a ring and printed, faces and feet: accident turned into method. *(not obtained; unverified.)*
- **Typografische Monatsblätter covers, 1972–73** — fifteen covers with stepped text blocks cut through by heavy black rectangles; Tschichold called him the "anti-typographer." *(visually checked: secondary source, neugraphic.com, one cover, TM 5/1973; earlier, stepped blocks and black bars were checked on the 1974 Typographic Process posters.)* Seen on 5/1973 (a quote from Emil Ruder, 1967): a thin rule frame with a hairline cross dividing the page into quarters; text blocks in German, English and French set in a bold grotesk with the letterspacing squeezed until letters touch, stepped down the page from top left to centre right; the French block is force-justified so its word gaps open into big holes; a large numeral 5 top centre and a small spaced masthead line at the foot. No black rectangles on this cover: the disruption comes from spacing (too tight in one block, too loose in the next), quoting the rule it breaks.
- **"How Can One Make Swiss Typography?", 1972** — lecture manuscript treating Swiss style as principles to question, not a style to copy. *(text, not checked visually.)*
- **Nine Collages series, 1974–75** — hand-cut films, type fragments, overlapping halftones. *(visually checked: Cooper Hewitt 18733995 Collage 1, 18733999 Collage 3, 18734003 Collage 5, 18734009 Collage 8, and the order poster 18733993 showing all nine. Every sheet is black on white with grey halftone photos. Solid black shapes (circles, crosses, arrows, a stepped L) take 24–28% of the image square, and type fragments are mostly legible (Greek and German text blocks, "ITALY", "EARTH" set on an arc). No moiré or colour seen.)*
- **TM supplement, December 1976** — seven years of work: spaced-out letters, thick black text boxes, a broken grid over a desert photo, text fragments that come back on later pages. *(not obtained; unverified.)*
- **Kunsthalle Basel Kunstkredit 76/77, 1977** — world-format (128×90 cm) silkscreen film collage, black and white; won a Swiss Poster Award. The B/W series ran 1976–79. *(visually checked: V&A O1155283. A heavy black frame runs down the left (≈14% of the width) and across the top, with only a ≈3% bar on the right, and breaks into 45° parallelogram stairs at the bottom-left. Coarse dot-screen photos overlap inside it, labels reverse out of black bars that run to the left edge, and "BASEL" is printed as a halftone.)*
- **Kunstkredit color posters, 1980–83 (e.g. 1981, offset)** — layered dot screens in process colors held together by an irregular, wide border. *(visually checked: LACMA collection images 207626 (Kunstkredit 1980/81) and 207619 (1982/83); also Schreibkunst 1981, 207227)* Seen: the two colour posters work differently. 1980/81 is two flat inks on paper: an orange-red field (≈60%) inside a white margin, the title KUNST KREDIT 1980/81 in huge rounded bold letters filled with diagonal yellow hatching, thin yellow rules, a sawtooth edge and a yellow triangle in one corner; no dot screens and no magenta or cyan. 1982/83 is the layered one: soft cyan and yellow dot-screen gradients overprint to make greens (blue-grey ≈36%, paper ≈26%, cyan ≈12%, green ≈10%, yellow ≈7–10%), with torn-paper and folded shapes, the title in white and yellow letters partly knocked out, stripes and a few hard geometric tabs. Magenta does not appear in either. Schreibkunst 1981 stays black and white.
- **Typographic Process posters NR.4 and NR.5, 1974 (added)** — *(visually checked: Cooper Hewitt 18673299, 18673301.)* NR.4 builds hybrid letters from stacked blocks on cream (ink ≈30% of the sheet). NR.5 ("Typography as Painting", TEE trip) has stair-stepped black bars, "L u g a n o" descending one letter per step, vertical hairline screens used as tone, and "TRANS EUROP EXPRESS" tracked to ≈2 cap heights per letter.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Space** (TM 5/1973): a quartered frame; four text blocks stepping diagonally across the quarters; spacing is the variable (crushed tracking against force-justified gaps).
- **Space** (Kunstkredit 1980/81): a single coloured field inside a white margin; the title stacked in three huge lines across the upper half; information in small blocks tucked against thin rules; one corner broken by a sawtooth and a triangle.
- **Space** (Kunstkredit 1982/83): a framed central panel with layered images spilling over its edge on all sides; the title partly lost in the layers; information in small blocks along one axis.
- **Colour relations:** either two flat inks (a dominant warm field ≈60% + one lighter accent ink for type and hatching) or two transparent process inks that overprint into a third colour; paper always present as margin and knock-out type.
- **Line and edge:** hatching inside letters, thin rules, sawtooth edges; in 1982/83 soft dot-screen gradients against hard folded shapes.
- **The procedure:** choose two inks; make one the field and the other the line/hatch (flat mode), or let both overprint as screened gradients to produce a third colour (layer mode); keep a paper margin; break one corner with a mechanical edge (sawtooth, stair).

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Wolfgang Weingart.** *Keep the inherited structure in plain sight and overdrive one of its variables at a time (spacing, step, frame weight, layer) until text starts to read as image, while a plainly set copy stays within reach.*
Process:
1. Lay down the orthodox structure first and leave it visible: a frame, a division into quarters or columns in hairlines, one family in two weights. Every later step is a measured departure from this, so it must be legible as the norm.
2. Per block, choose ONE variable and push it past tolerance while the block beside it takes the opposite extreme: spacing opened until each letter stands alone (about 2 cap heights per letter) against spacing crushed until letters touch; or a fixed indent added per line (2–4 characters) so the block's edge becomes a diagonal. Only short or secondary text is treated; the information is also set once, normally.
3. Add solid masses as structure: 12–30% of the surface in the darkest value, as bars and blocks that carry reversed labels and run past their text unevenly toward one edge.
4. Weight the enclosure unequally: heavy on one side (≈14% of the width), thin on the opposite (≈3%), and break one corner with a mechanical edge (steps, a 45° cut, a sawtooth). The dense material sits inside, pushed off-centre.
5. Make tone by screening, not by tinting: greys are coarse dots or fields of parallel hairlines (each grey 15–25% of the area), and 3–5 planes overlap. Labels never sit on a screened field; they sit on the solid masses of step 3 or on bare ground.
6. Colour mode last. Default: none (darkest value, screened greys, ground). Otherwise exactly two inks plus the ground, in one of two ways: flat (one ink is the field, ≈60%, the other is line and hatching inside large letters) or screened (both inks as dot gradients that overprint into a third colour).
Why it works:
- A deviation is only visible against a norm (salience by oddness): with the frame and the normal block present, the crushed or opened block reads as a decision about spacing. Remove the norm and the same block is merely badly set.
- Proximity makes letters into words. Open the spacing far enough and each letter groups with the field instead, so the word turns into a row of marks and slows the eye: the text becomes image without any image being added.
- Fine regular marks fuse into tone, and overlapping screens fuse into a third value, so depth and grey come from the reproduction process itself. An unequal frame gives the dense core a direction, where an even border would make it a picture in a mount.
Colour relations: darkest value 12–30% solid; one or two screened greys at 15–25% each; ground the rest and always present as margin and reversed type. Ten of the twelve checked works are without hue. Each colour mode was seen in one work only: two flat inks (field ≈60% and a lighter line ink), and two screened inks overprinting (ground ≈26%, the mixed tones the majority). No third ink, and none of the checked works uses a red-violet process ink.
Space and line: visible hairline frame and divisions; frame 14% against 3%; one broken corner; step of 2–4 characters per line; bars overshoot on one side only; rules are type-high or hairline, nothing between.
Type requirement: one family, two weights, with letters sturdy enough to survive touching and to stand alone when spaced, and to hold when reversed out of a solid at small sizes.
Motion and interaction: reveals arrive in discrete steps, one line per beat (60–80ms a step, ≤320ms in all), once. Press shifts the layer 2px, like a plate out of register. Hover may open a word's spacing, but the accessible name and a normally set copy stay unchanged; layers that slip on scroll stop under reduced motion; nothing flashes.
Fails when: the text is long or must be scanned fast (prices, errors, forms); the structure being broken is not shown, so the break reads as a bug; every block is overdriven (no norm left); labels have to sit on screened or layered fields.
Signature (do not carry together): stair-stepped text blocks and bars · labels reversed out of heavy black bars · coarse halftone collage.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Booking confirmation for a small hotel, in ivory and forest green (phone, 390 × 844; old-style serif, calm photography, generous leading).
1. *Structure.* A 1px green frame inset 16px and a hairline cross dividing the screen into quarters. Serif in regular and bold. The booking details are set normally in the lower-left quarter: this is the norm.
2. *One variable each.* The hotel name, 14px, is spaced to 28px per letter and spans the top; directly below, the strapline is set bold and tightened until letters touch. The four itinerary lines (arrive, room, dinner, leave) step right by 3 characters per line, so their edge runs diagonally into the lower-right quarter. Each also appears in the normal block.
3. *Masses.* Solid forest green 22% of the screen: one bar under the name running to the right edge and stopping 9 characters short on the left, and one block holding the confirmation code reversed in ivory.
4. *Enclosure.* The frame thickens to 54px (14%) on the left and 12px (3%) on the right; its bottom-left corner is cut into three 45° steps.
5. *Tone.* The room photo is rendered as a coarse green dot screen, 20% of the area, overlapping the cross and the bar by one layer; a field of vertical hairlines stands for the map. No text on either.
6. *Colour.* Default mode: green, its screens and ivory. No second ink.
- *Interaction.* "Add to calendar" is a 48px solid bar; on press it shifts 2px down-right.
- *Motion.* The four itinerary lines land one per 70ms beat, once.
Result: a quiet green-and-ivory serif confirmation with one loud idea. No black, no grotesque, no collage. What survived is the visible norm, opposite spacing extremes side by side, solid masses, and an unequal frame.

## Space & composition
- **The grid is visible so it can be violated.** Keep a strict 12-col Swiss grid, then offset one block by half a column and let a black bar cross two others. The break only reads because the structure is obvious.
- **Staircase blocks.** Text is set as a run of short lines, each indented one fixed step (e.g. 2–4ch) further than the last, so the block's edge becomes a diagonal. Stack 2–3 of these at different step sizes for counter-rhythm.
- **Heavy rectangles as structure.** Solid black bars and boxes are compositional weights, not decoration. They hide, divide and underline. Measured ink share: 12% (NR.5), 24–28% (Collages 1, 8), 30% (NR.4) of the surface.
- **Collage density inside a heavy frame.** Kunstkredit 76/77 packs fragmented images into the centre, held by a black frame that is heavy on one side (≈14% of the width, left) and thin on the other (≈3%, right). It ends in 45° stair bars at one corner. Dense core, weighted edge.
- **Layer, don't place.** Elements overlap by design. Assume a z-axis of 3–5 translucent planes rather than a flat flow.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

The logic is reprographic: black, paper, and the halftone in between. Ten of the twelve checked works (among them Nine Collages, NR.4, NR.5, Kunstkredit 76/77, Schreibkunst 1981) are achromatic, with chroma ≤3; two are in two inks. The 1982/83 Kunstkredit (LACMA 207619) is two screened inks, cyan + yellow dot screens overprinting into greens; the 1980/81 sheet (207626) is two flat inks, orange + yellow. No magenta appears in any checked work.

| Hex | Role |
|---|---|
| `#E2DED0` | Paper ground, slightly warm newsprint (NR.4; collages are cooler `#ECEEED`) |
| `#20201F` | Ink: type, bars, solid blocks (12–30% of the surface) |
| `#85807E` | Halftone grey: dot-screen fields, secondary type (Kunstkredit 76/77: greys 16–23% each) |
| `#00A0D6` | Process cyan, screened overprint layer 1 (seen in Kunstkredit 1982/83; hex not sampled) |
| `#FFE100` | Process yellow, screened overprint layer 2 or flat line ink (seen in Kunstkredit 1980/81 and 1982/83; hex not sampled) |

No magenta: none of the twelve checked works uses it.

Rule: screens stay in black, grey and paper. If colour is used at all (seen in two works only), use at most two inks per view: either two flat inks, or two screened inks always overlapping each other.

## Form, line & typography
One grotesque in a few weights, fully exploited: extreme tracking (`letter-spacing: 0.5em–1.2em`) on a single word so it reads as a shape; tight negative tracking (−0.04em) on the block next to it; mixed sizes within one line; heavy horizontal rules; type reversed out of black bars; words split across lines or across the page. Lines are type-high rules: horizontal, thick, sometimes cut off sharply. Fields of vertical hairlines stand in for grey tone (NR.5), and single words bend along an arc ("EARTH", Collage 3).
Fonts: **Archivo** (grotesque with variable width, so it can do Akzidenz-like stretching and condensing), **Inter Tight** for dense small text, **Archivo Black** for knockout bars.

## Rhythm, motion & time
Weingart compared typographic unity to musical composition, so motion should feel syncopated rather than eased. Blocks enter in steps (`steps(4)` timing, 60–80ms per step) so a staircase builds one tread at a time. Letterspacing animates: on hover, tracking opens from 0 to 0.6em and the word dissolves into a pattern. Layers slip against each other on scroll at different rates (parallax of 0.9/1/1.15), and grain or halftone fields shift 1–2px, like misregistered film. A text fragment seen earlier comes back later in the flow, a callback.

## UI translations
1. **Layout:** Build on a visible 12-col grid, then shift exactly one module per screen by 0.5 column and let it overlap its neighbor.
2. **Layout:** Set section intros as staircase blocks, with each line indented `calc(var(--step) * n)`, where `--step: 3ch`.
3. **Type:** Track one display word to 1em+ and set it at the same size as body text. Readability shouldn't depend on size; the spacing alone makes it a headline.
4. **Type:** Reverse key labels out of solid black bars that run past the text by an uneven amount on one side, e.g. 2ch left and 9ch right.
5. **Color:** Keep the view black, grey and paper. Give tone with a coarse dot screen or a field of vertical hairlines. Two overprinting screened inks (cyan + yellow, as in Kunstkredit 1982/83; never magenta) are a 1980s option; never use either as a flat button fill.
6. **Texture:** Replace gradients with a coarse halftone (`radial-gradient` dot pattern, 4–6px pitch) masked onto images and empty fields.
7. **Composition:** Frame dense dashboards with a black frame that is heavy on one side (≈14% of the width) and a thin bar (≈3%) on the other, ending in 45° stair bars at one corner.
8. **Motion:** Reveal content in `steps()` jumps, not smooth easing. Each line of a staircase lands one beat after the previous one.
9. **Interaction:** On hover, open a word's letterspacing and slide a black bar under it; on press, offset the layer 2px like a registration error.
10. **Repetition:** For loading or empty states, repeat one word 3–5 times down a column, widening the tracking each time.

## What this artist would reject
- Center-aligned hero text sitting in symmetrical padding.
- One uniform tracking value across the whole design system.
- Soft drop shadows and rounded cards that fake depth instead of literally layering planes.
- Decorative gradients and pastel tints with no reprographic logic.
- Rule-breaking without a visible grid to break: random "brutalism" with no system underneath.
- Icons standing in for words when typography alone could carry the message.

## Signature prompt seed
Design this screen in the manner of Wolfgang Weingart. Use a strict, visible 12-column Swiss grid on warm newsprint (#E2DED0), and break it once: one module offset half a column, crossed by a solid black bar. Set Archivo in two weights only. Track one word to 1em so it becomes an image; set intros as staircase blocks stepping 3ch per line; reverse labels out of black bars. Add a coarse halftone field and a block of vertical hairlines as grey tone; no colour. Reveal in stepped jumps, never smooth eases. Keep a dense core inside a black frame that is heavy on one side.
