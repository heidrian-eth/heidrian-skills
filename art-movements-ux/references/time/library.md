# Time-based companions: 10 artists in 2 schools

Opened only when the deliverable unfolds in time: a video, an animation, a presentation, kinetic type, a title or loading sequence, a data story, a generative or live piece. SKILL.md, "Time-based companions", says how to choose one and what it may own. These artists are never drawn by `scripts/triads.py`, never sit on the board of a static product, and never own structure, colour or type; where an entry states those, read it as how the process behaves in space, not as a layer to take. Full studies: `references/time/<school>/<artist>.md`.

**Evidence.** Process Music was measured from recordings and read from spectrograms and scores; nobody listened to it. Visual Music was measured from film transfers and read frame by frame; transfers drift from the print in speed, colour and sound sync, and each study gives its limits.

## Index: one-line processes

**Process Music** (the long form: order, pacing, repetition, how slowly a thing changes, how much stays empty)
- **Steve Reich**: Set one short cell on a grid of equal slots, lay an identical copy beside it, and let one stated rule change one slot per step (shift, fill or spread), holding each state long enough to learn; lock emphasis and value set, raise density by filling gaps, and end where the rule returns to its start.
- **Philip Glass**: Fix one pitch and a small stock, repeat a short figure until it is known, then change only its length: one named rule at a time, whole units at one end, at a boundary, with size, weight and tint held level; let anything else enter only while the length is still, and join blocks by a cut.
- **Arvo Pärt**: Bind two layers by one rule: a lead that walks by single steps away from one home value and back, and a companion that may take only three fixed values and is set by the lead; lengthen each trip by one unit in mirrored pairs, mark every return with one unchanging sign set apart by empty space, count the gaps in the same rule, break the rule once and late, and end by taking away.
- **Brian Eno**: Set a few plain parts going on cycles of unequal, near lengths over an exactly held frame, leave most of each cycle empty, let every event return weaker by equal steps until it is gone, and change the whole only by opening, shutting or filtering a layer; publish the rules, run them, and tune the rules, never a result.
- **John Cage**: Fix the frame before the content, divided by one short series used at two scales; fill its cells by a seeded draw from a closed chart in which empty is an entry with a set share; never edit a result, only the chart; run a second layer on an unrelated interval; give windows, not points; join by cuts.

**Visual Music** (the event: how one thing appears, moves, decays and lands against a sound or a clock)
- **Oskar Fischinger**: Rule the time before the form: give every event a start and a length on one ruler, open its figure from a seed in one to five units, let it decay in place many times longer on one law, overlap the next, keep a thin residue, and spend the residue in one unison per section before a short blank.
- **Norman McLaren**: Set one limit (one mark, one operation) and count the events in time units before drawing; land each mark whole on its event, let it decay front-first and leave its place empty; bind one slow line strictly and mark about half of the rest; move at constant speed, accelerating only into a break; multiply by one slow rule, then return to one.
- **Len Lye**: Cut the run into short phrases at the joins the process itself has; hold one figure and one ground through each phrase and change one of them in a single step; redraw a live thing in place and move a travelling one at constant speed; break often with short empty beats, and still nearly everything while words are read.
- **Mary Ellen Bute**: List the events first; give each kind one plain mark at a fixed seat on a still carrier; a mark either stays (count) or flashes (acknowledge), never both; time to the frame only sparse, sharp events and carry the rest by slow light; raise emphasis by peak or rate; close on one held figure.
- **John Whitney**: Give every member of a set the same rule of motion and a whole-number rate of its own on one shared period, so that order is the brief moment when the rates agree at a simple fraction: drift between, slow and dwell there, enter copies at a fixed step, and start each new thing in the gap the last one leaves.

## Problems: where to look

**One thing must win the first glance**
- Hold one rule everywhere and break it exactly once, 60–87% of the way through, by one value outside the allowed set; follow it with the longest gap in the view (about 1.4 times the next longest) and a small sign, with no change of size or colour. *Pärt*

**Many equal things, no hero**
- Fix the frame first and fill its cells by a seeded draw from a closed chart (at most 8 × 8) in which empty is an entry with a stated share (5–45%); bound the draw by counts (about 5 marks per 12–15 units, no gap over 4) and change the chart, never a cell. *Cage*

**Trust, stability, seriousness**
- Give every free value a companion that may take only three fixed values, picked by one rule (nearest below, nearest above, or alternating) and never shown alone, so whatever the content does one of three known values stands beside it. *Pärt*

**Where am I in a sequence (progress)**
- Draw every state as its own equal module, three to a row, each holding two identical lines set 1 : 7 as close as the rows, one fixed and one displaced one slot per module; the last module returns to the first state and its short row (0.35) is the end. *Reich*
- End every stage on one home column and set one unchanging mark there, parted from the content by an empty band 0.6–1 of its width; stages grow by one unit in equal mirrored pairs (1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3), so the widening distance between identical marks is the progress. *Pärt*
- Let each finished step leave a thin still mark at the place where it happened (a tenth to a thirtieth of its peak), so the count of marks is the progress; at the end of the section all marks swell together once (to 10–18% of the view in 4 units, back in 2), then 120–170ms in which nothing moves. *Fischinger*
- Leave one copy at each completed pose, up to 5, each in its own flat colour and overlapping the last by a third to a half; hold the full set for 1s, then remove oldest first, 0.2–0.4s apart, until only the live one is left, with the count in figures. *Lye*
- One unit per completed event, lit at the next seat along one fixed axis by a 0.6–0.9 s fade and never moved again, so the number of marks is the number of events; a mark either stays or flashes, never both. *Bute*
- Tie the loop to the period of the process and not to each event: an exact loop of 2–4s, copies entered a fixed step apart (a third of the loop), progress read as the simple fraction at which the set resolves and stated in figures; with motion off, show only those resolved states. *Whitney*

**Arriving and crossing between contexts**
- Join blocks by a cut: each block is flat at one of two levels (variation inside under a tenth) and the next starts whole at a boundary, with no ramp or blend; an empty interval of about 1% of the block may mark the join and appears nowhere else. *Glass*
- Hold the figure and replace the ground behind it in one step at the join, in a different hue and with no blend, then leave 0.5–0.7s of ground alone before the next phrase starts; time the change to the cause, 20–70ms early and never late, and mark nothing inside the phrase. *Lye*
- Start the new view small (0.07–0.2 of the view's height) in the gap at the centre of the one that is leaving, overlap the two for about a tenth of the shorter life, and let the old one leave by growing past the edge; no wipe and no cut. *Whitney*

**Empty state, invitation**
- Keep the frame, its divisions and its one scale line, and put nothing in: an empty cell has the same edges and the same size rule as a full one, so a blank reads as a member of the set and not as a fault. *Cage*

**Feedback on hover, press, select**
- Make the response lopsided and early: start it at the input, 60–120ms ahead of the result it marks, full within one to five 40ms units, then let it decay in place 2–80 times longer on one law (equal step or halving); a symmetric ease puts the peak mid-way and the event is felt late. *Fischinger*
- Land the response whole in the frame of the input, with no grow-in; let it lose half in 80ms and 90% in about 330ms, then keep its place empty 80–250ms before anything else arrives there; gather faster events into one mark per slot, at most 3 a second. *McLaren*
- Answer a struck event with one small mark (0.5–1% of the view) at a seat fixed per kind on a carrier that does not move: on within 50 ms, alive 100–200 ms, off hard; it may be early, never late. *Bute*

**Depth and layering without shadows**
- Run a second layer on an interval that is no multiple of the first, one unchanging mark at 2–4% of the first layer's rate, meeting it only at the frame's outer edges and told apart by line pattern; layers that never step together separate without tint. *Cage*

**Identity with very little surface**
- Fix 5–7 plain parts and a seed, run them in unruled lanes on unequal cycles so no two views match while every part is known, repeat the whole block only after 8 or more turns of the longest cycle, and print the rule as a one-line key beside the result. *Eno*

**Live and changing data**
- Change one slot per step and hold each state at least 12 cell-lengths (holds 80% of the time, transitions 20% or a cut); show rising load by filling empty slots (8 → 12 of 12, or an on-share of 21% → 100% at a fixed period), never by size, contrast or speed. *Reich*
- Hold updates and apply them on a fixed tick as whole units added or removed at one end, in one step of 100ms or less with no highlight; size, weight and tint stay level, and a second kind of change (a new layer or series) enters only while the count is still, on a plateau about twice a normal step. *Glass*
- Let each arrival leave a trace that loses one equal value step per refresh and is gone after 10–14; admit the next when the last is two thirds gone so density stays level, hold arrivals to a hidden slot of 2–6s, fade them in over 600–1200ms and out 2–4 times slower, and keep the cut for a change of state. *Eno*
- Give every change a start and a length on one ruler and run two layers on unequal clocks, one at least five times slower (2.5–3.2s against 0.2–0.5s); a figure opens from a hairline at its home, the next overlaps it by 40–300ms, and colour steps one way along 3–5 stops with age, so a still frame shows what is new. *Fischinger*
- Bind one slow series strictly (place from one attribute, size from another, one mark per event) and mark only 40–50% of the rest; admit new units on one slow clock, and trail a moving value with delayed copies (125–210ms apart, at most 11) that fold into it within 0.75s when it stops. *McLaren*
- Show that a thing is live by redrawing it on each real tick of its process, the same in width and colour and different in bend and place, and show that it is going somewhere by a glide at constant speed (0.3–1.2 of the view per second, turnaround in one step); two kinds of motion for two meanings, with the bare ground between them, and the bare ground again whenever the process pauses. *Lye*
- Time to the frame only the sparse, sharp events and let one slow property follow the 2 s level for the rest; raise emphasis by peak size (×1.3–1.5 per step) or by halving the interval, never by smoother motion, and close on one figure held still. *Bute*
- Give every item of a set the same rule of motion and a whole-number rate of its own on one shared period (item k laps k times, all at constant speed); the items then fall into as many groups as the denominator at each half, third, quarter or fifth of the period, so alignment is the event: slow and dwell there 1–3.5s, drift between, and keep order to a tenth to a third of the time. *Whitney*

**Comparing and choosing**
- Stack variants of one figure of 4–8 units on one pitch so identical units share a column and each differs from the last only in length, by one named rule (+1, +k, doubling); additions sit at one end, each variant gets the same room, and its count is written beside it as a sum ("5+3+3"). *Glass*

**Calm over long sessions**
- Lock emphasis and value set for the whole session and layer two clocks about 66 : 1 apart: a fixed unit and a slow swell on one secondary attribute, two per section, that moves the whole by a third of what it moves its own band. *Reich*
- Put recurring items on cycles that are near and unequal (within 2 : 1, none a multiple of another), counted in visits or days and not on a clock, each filling a third of its cycle at most; the frame stays exact, the rests stay empty (about a fifth of the span), totals hold level per screen, and nothing new is marked. *Eno*
- Treat the unchanged view as a rest: when no events arrive nothing moves, and 25–45% of a sparse passage is ground alone; travel at constant speed with one-frame turns, and keep acceleration for the exit of a thing being replaced (each step 1.5–3.3 times the last over about 290ms). *McLaren*

### T.1 PROCESS MUSIC: pulse, gradual process, loops and silence
The movement: music built from a rule stated in advance and left to run, so the change itself is what you attend to. Carry the rule, the hold, the count, the unequal cycle and the planned emptiness; leave the sounds, the repeat marks and the pulsing-visualiser look behind. These studies were measured from recordings and read from scores and spectrograms; nobody listened to them.
*The problem it answered:* How do you make slow change legible, keep long repetition alive, and let a system produce variety without choosing each result? The answers here fix a unit and a frame first, change one thing at a time, hold each state long enough to learn it, give silence a stated share, and publish the rule beside the result.

**Steve Reich.** *Set one short cell on a grid of equal slots, lay an identical copy beside it, and let one stated rule change one slot per step (shift, fill or spread), holding each state long enough to learn; lock emphasis and value set, raise density by filling gaps, and end where the rule returns to its start.*
Process:
1. Fix the cell and its grid. One short pattern on 8 or 12 equal slots, about two thirds of them filled (8 of 12), the filled slots in groups of unequal length, each closed by one empty slot. Every module is the same width whether it is empty or full. Nothing outside the cell is ever added; if the cell changes, every later step is redone.
2. Lock the frame. The value set (5–11 members), the slot size and the emphasis are fixed for the whole view or session: no size, weight or contrast step is added while the rule runs. Whatever differs later can then only be the rule.
3. Duplicate the layer. A second copy, identical in every property, sits so close to the first that the two read as one object (the gap inside the pair about 1/7 of the gap between pairs). One copy never changes: state it once and abbreviate it afterwards to a repeat mark. Only the other copy is written out.
4. Choose ONE rule in ONE mode and print it as one sentence on the view. Shift: the moving copy is displaced one slot per step, so a cell of n slots gives n steps and the state after the last equals the first. Fill: one empty slot is filled per step, from 1 filled up to the cell's full count, in an order that is not left to right. Spread: the period stays fixed while the share of it that is "on" grows from about 20% to 100%. Stepped or continuous is decided here, once.
5. Set the pace by the hold. One step changes one slot and nothing else. Each state is held for at least 12 cell-lengths (11–35 measured), and a finished build about three times as long before anything else moves. Holds take about 80% of the time and transitions 20%, or the transition is a cut. The still form is the default: every state drawn as its own equal module, in order, three to a row.
6. Raise density by filling, never by emphasis. When more is happening, empty slots close (8 → 10–12 of 12 once two copies overlap; an on-share of 21% → 93%) while the strongest mark gains at most about a seventh of what the emptiest part gains. Keep the gaps wherever items must be read one by one; close them where the items should read as one tone.
7. Add at most one second clock and one reserved band. The slow clock runs about 66 times the fast unit, two cycles per section, and moves one secondary attribute about three times as far as the whole moves. One band stays empty for the first 10–17% of the sequence; one new layer enters there alone, while everything else carries on, and that entry is the only structural marker. Sections take time or space in proportion to the length of their cell (12 : 8 : 4 slots → about 49 : 33 : 17%).
8. End from the rule. The sequence stops at the state it started from (or when the fill or spread is complete), with a cut and no fade, and does not start again by itself. In the still form the last row is shorter than the others (about 0.35 of a row) and that is the end mark.
Why it works:
- Grouping by likeness: two layers that match in every property fuse into one object, so the only thing left to read is the offset between them, and the lines that neither copy contains by itself appear in the overlap. Give the copies different colours and they become figure and ground; the offset then reads as two separate things, not as one thing changing.
- Adaptation to the previous state: a hold many cells long makes the state the baseline, so a change of one slot is found by oddness with no added emphasis. Shorten the hold and the steps blur into continuous movement; change two slots at once and the rule can no longer be told from noise; raise the emphasis at the change and the eye reads an alarm, not a step.
- Fusion of fine detail into tone: separate items need the empty slot between them to stay items. As the gaps fill, the range between strongest and weakest collapses (to about a third of what it was) and the row is read as one texture. Density can therefore carry "more" while the frame stays quiet. Raise the level instead and the frame is no longer constant, so nothing is left to measure the rule against.
- Closure: a rule that returns to its start lets the viewer predict the end and confirms that the rule was understood. In a shift the states mirror around the middle step, so the second half is recognised, not learned. A looped or faded ending removes that proof.
- Two clocks far apart (about 66 : 1) are read as different kinds of thing and do not compete; brought near each other they beat against one another and both are lost. The entry into the band kept empty is found by oddness of place, so it needs no emphasis of its own.
Colour relations: one mark value on one ground, with the ground at about 84% of the view and the marks at 6–15%; the two process layers share one colour, with no second hue between them; texture, where there is any, stays in near-neutrals whose chroma is a small fraction of any accent's; the darkest value is the smallest share (about 1.5%) and belongs to labels. The process has no accent of its own: a case colour can only mark the single entry in the reserved band. A change of colour is a cross-fade on the same slots, after the rule has finished.
Space and line: 8 or 12 equal slots per module, about two thirds filled; modules equal in width, three to a row; gap inside a pair to gap between rows 1 : 7; the fixed layer stated once, then a repeat mark; the rule as one line above the field; a shorter last row (0.35) as the end; one band empty for the first 10–17%; section extents proportional to cell length; no outline, no hero, no centre.
Type requirement: text is set by slot, so figures and labels are of equal width and a module keeps its width at any fill. One size and one weight inside the field. Modules are numbered. The rule is one plain sentence with its count ("each state 12 times"). Longer instructions are prose in as many paragraphs as there are phases, separated by a gap of about two lines.
Motion and interaction: the still form carries the whole process, so nothing has to move. Where time is used, steps are bound to the user's own events (a save, a new day, a page), one slot per event, as a cut or as a slide lasting at most a fifth of the shortest hold (200–400ms for an event step). A spread indicator keeps one period of 1.6s or longer and widens its on-share from 20% to 100% as work completes, with no change of speed and almost none of brightness. A slow swell of about 9s may move one secondary attribute by a small amount, two per section. Urgency comes from halving the interval between two alternating states (8, 4, then 3 cells) while the unit stays the same. No mark passes from light to dark more than 3 times a second. Reduced motion: every step is a cut, the swell is off, and the spread indicator becomes a still row with n of 12 slots filled.
Fails when: the content has no repeating cell (unique items, long prose, a single figure); a change must be noticed at once (errors, alarms), since a rule slower than noticing hides it; the layers must be told apart by category, so they cannot be identical; fewer than about 8 slots fit; the visit is shorter than a few holds and the states cannot be laid out side by side; the brief needs a hero or a climax.
Signature (do not carry together): two identical tracks drifting out of step between left and right · a twelve-slot row of hits and rests rotated one place at a time · a fast even pulse under slow swells of about two breaths.

**Philip Glass.** *Fix one pitch and a small stock, repeat a short figure until it is known, then change only its length: one named rule at a time, whole units at one end, at a boundary, with size, weight and tint held level; let anything else enter only while the length is still, and join blocks by a cut.*
Process:
1. Fix one pitch and one stock. A single smallest unit (a cell of one width, a row of one height, or one tick of time) serves the whole view, and every size and every moment of change is a whole multiple of it. No half units, and no gap inside a run. The stock is 3–5 kinds of unit and nothing is added to it later. If the pitch changes, every later step is redone.
2. Write the base figure: one group of 4–8 units from the stock. Show it unchanged at least 5 times (as repeated rows, or held over 5 ticks) before any variant appears, so that it is known and can serve as the measure.
3. Lock everything except count. Unit size, weight, value, tint and spacing are the same in every repeat and every variant of a block; any variation in them stays under about a tenth. No new unit is marked, accented or announced.
4. Choose ONE length rule for a run and name it: add one unit (8–20% of the figure); append a copy of the figure less its last unit; insert the same group of k units each time; or double a tag (+4, +8, +16). Run it for 4–8 stages, to between 2 and 4.5 times the base. New units always join at the same end and old units keep their column. Inside a run the rule is exact; which rule comes next is a free choice.
5. End the run in one of three ways: walk back by the same stages; strip from the old end so that what remains is the newest part; or stop at the longest stage.
6. Give every stage the same room, not the same number of repeats: an equal span of the view, or an equal dwell. A short figure is seen many times and a long one a few times (33 down to 5).
7. Change a second thing only on a plateau. A new layer, a new band or a new kind of unit enters only while the count is still; it starts on a cycle boundary or its half, takes a band of its own that overlaps no other (the lowest band is kept for the ground), and the plateau lasts about twice a normal stage.
8. Join blocks by a cut. A block sits flat at one of two levels (full or quiet) and the next block starts whole at the boundary, with no ramp or blend. A short empty interval, about 1% of the block, may stand at the join and nowhere else.
9. State the count. Beside each stage write its length as a sum and a multiplier ("5+3+3", "×12"), and stack the stages so that identical units share a column: additions show as marks spreading at one end, removals as blank at the other.
Loop mode (in place of steps 4–5): a ring of 3–5 members with unequal whole-unit lengths in a simple ratio (2 : 3 : 4). Reach it by shortening in two or three stages, or stretch one member at a time to about double while at least one member never moves. Once set, the ring stays fixed and step 7 applies.
Why it works:
- Adaptation: five or more identical repeats set what the eye expects, so one added unit is found by oddness with no emphasis at all. Accent the new unit and the accent is read in place of the length; show the variant before the figure is known and there is nothing to measure it against.
- Grouping by likeness: with size, weight and tint level, every repeat belongs to one texture and count is the only difference left. Let level move with length and the change reads as a swell, which hides where each stage begins.
- Alignment as scale reference: units that keep their column let the eye subtract one stage from the next, so growth is read as extent at one end. Reflow the row and every unit has moved, and the difference has to be counted.
- One change at a time can be attributed. A layer that enters while the count is still is read as an arrival; entering during a length change, neither is read. The plateau is long because the eye needs the old figure confirmed under the new layer.
- Equal room per stage keeps the form even: with equal repeats the long stages would take 3 to 25 times the room of the short ones and the end would swallow the beginning.
- A cut keeps level a category with two values. A ramp makes it a third variable and promises a climax that the process never delivers.
Colour relations: tint is a constant, not a variable: one mark colour on one ground inside a block, marks about a tenth of the surface. Layers are told apart by band (position and value range), never by a hue per item, and bands do not overlap; the lowest and darkest band belongs to the ground layer. A block has one of two levels, differing by a clear step, and a shift of tint happens only at a block boundary, where it means "new block". No colour marks what is new.
Space and line: one pitch; base figure 4–8 units; 5 or more repeats before a change; 4–8 stages a run; growth 2–4.5 times; one rule a run; additions at one end; equal room per stage; plateau about 2 times a stage; ring ratios 2 : 3 : 4; hard start and hard end with no fade; an empty interval only at a block join, about 1% of the block; density level inside a block and stepped between blocks.
Type requirement: every unit is the same symbol: one size, one weight, no emphasis style. The face needs figures of equal width and a sum sign that sets cleanly, since counts are text ("5+4+3", "×12"). Grouping is shown by a bracket or a rule over the group, not by weight. Labels sit beside the stage they count and are stepped in on a fixed indent.
Motion and interaction: nothing runs unprompted; the repeats are in the layout. A change lands whole in one step of 100ms or less, with no easing and no highlight, and only at a boundary: live updates are held and applied on a fixed tick (chosen once per product), one unit at one end, the rest keeping their columns. After a change the state holds for one dwell before the next is applied, and for two dwells when a layer has entered. Blocks swap in 100ms or less with no crossfade. Reduced motion: the same, with changes instant; nothing is lost, because nothing depended on movement.
Fails when: fewer than 5 repeats of the figure fit; the units are unequal (prose, media, mixed cards), so count cannot be the variable; one item must win the first glance or a change is urgent, since nothing new may be marked; items differ in kind, not in quantity; a figure grows beyond one line or one view (over about 30 units) and wraps, which breaks the columns.
Signature (do not carry together): a fast unbroken stream of identical marks that runs by itself · numbers and counting syllables set as the display text · ruled staves with beamed groups stepping out to one side.

**Arvo Pärt.** *Bind two layers by one rule: a lead that walks by single steps away from one home value and back, and a companion that may take only three fixed values and is set by the lead; lengthen each trip by one unit in mirrored pairs, mark every return with one unchanging sign set apart by empty space, count the gaps in the same rule, break the rule once and late, and end by taking away.*
Process:
1. Fix the home and the ladder. One home value (a column, a level, a state) and one ordered ladder of equal steps on both sides of it. Every sequence in the view ends on home, and home never moves. If home changes, every later step is redone.
2. Bind two layers. The lead may stand on any rung but moves only to an adjacent one. The companion may take only three fixed values spread across the ladder, and for each lead value ONE rule picks it: the nearest of the three below, the nearest above, or the two in alternation. The rule is chosen once for the view. A companion is never shown without its lead, and each layer keeps one channel (a position, a second line, a bar) from start to end.
3. Reserve a band with a hole in front of it. The marker of step 5 gets a band of its own at one edge, parted from the content by an empty band 0.6–1 times as wide as the content band at its widest. Nothing else is ever put in the hole.
4. Grow by one, in mirrored pairs. A trip is a run of units that ends on home. Trips come in twos of equal length, one reaching home from below and one from above, and each pair is one unit longer than the last (1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3). Run 3–8 sizes. The step is constant, so growth is linear and never speeds up, and a mirror costs exactly what its original cost. Stop at the longest, or walk back down by the same steps.
5. Mark each arrival. One sign, identical every time, appears once whenever the lead is on home, and nowhere else and for no other reason; its spacing therefore widens as the trips grow. A separator between modules obeys the same law: constant size (0.2–0.3 of a module) and constant level while the modules vary.
6. Count the emptiness. The gap after each trip is a number in the rule, in one direction chosen once: it grows with the trip (to about 1.75 times across a fourfold growth of the trip), or it shrinks by one unit each time while the trips lengthen (8 down to 1). Gaps take 10–50% of the run. A layer with nothing to show keeps its slot, drawn empty, and a gap is never filled.
7. Vary one surface only. If the view needs variety, a single layer may change its density from section to section (up to about 12 : 1) while home, the three companion values, the marker and the separator stay exactly as they were.
8. Break the rule once, late. At 60–87% of the run, most often near three quarters, one companion value falls outside its three, or one count is off by a small whole number. The gap that follows is the longest in the view, about 1.4 times the next longest, and a small sign sits beside the exception. There is no second one.
9. End by subtraction. Remove layers one at a time from one side until the thinnest layer or the marker stands alone, leave out the last unit the rule predicts, and keep an empty interval after it of about 3% of the run.
Scaled mode (in place of step 4): show the same line 3–5 times at rates 1 : 2 : 4 (up to 16), each copy one band further along and entering after a delay that doubles each time; rate and band move together, in one direction chosen once, and no copy ever changes its rate.
Rotation mode (in place of step 4): put a loop of n − 1 items in a frame of n slots (15 in 16), or move the start by a stride that does not divide the ring (2 on 7), so that each row begins one position later and the first state returns only after n rows (or 7). Stack the rows, frame the part that has moved and put a small cross on the item that has not.
Why it works:
- Grouping by likeness: whatever the lead does, one of three known values stands beside it, so every state of the view belongs to one family and the lead's movement is the only news. Free the companion and there are two lines to follow; remove it and the lead has nothing to be measured against; give it five values and it stops being a constant.
- Scale reference: every trip ends on the same point, so its length is read as distance from home without counting. Let trips end anywhere and growth reads as drift.
- Adaptation to the previous state: the first trip of a pair teaches a size and the second confirms it from the other side, so the one added unit of the next pair is found with no emphasis. Unpaired growth has to be counted; growth that speeds up promises a peak that the rule never delivers.
- Salience by oddness: a marker that never varies carries one fact, where. Vary its form and it becomes a third layer; use it for anything but arrival and the widening distance between two markers no longer measures the growth.
- Figure and ground: an empty band as wide as the content turns the marker into a different kind of thing by position alone, with no second colour. Close the hole and the marker is one more unit.
- A gap is where the last group is taken in. Tied to the group's size, it gives long groups more room; equalised, the long groups crowd; filled, there is no boundary left to see.
- An exception is odd only against a rule that is already known, which takes most of the run. Centre it and the rule is not yet proven; multiply it and it is a second rule; the longest gap after it gives the eye time to check that it was meant.
- Removing layers from a full state uncovers what was under them all along, so the last thing seen is the constant. An ending that adds reads as a climax, and nothing before it was built to carry one.
Colour relations: the ground lies at one end of the value scale with low chroma and takes everything that is not marked; marks are one colour and stay under 5% of the surface. A second neutral close to the ground's value may hold one panel of about a third. Hue stands for a role (a layer or a function), never for an item, each hue under 5%, and only in working views; a finished view has one mark colour and at most one hue, under 1%. The marker differs by band and position, not by colour, and the exception carries a sign, not a tint.
Space and line: one home; two layers plus a marker; three companion values; adjacent steps only; trips in equal mirrored pairs, +1 unit per pair, 3–8 sizes; marker band parted by a hole 0.6–1 of the content band; separator 0.2–0.3 of a module; gaps 10–50% of the run; one density-varying layer, up to 12 : 1; one exception at 60–87% followed by a gap 1.4 times the next longest; last predicted unit left out, then about 3% empty. Rows are as long as their content and are never stretched to match; an unused slot keeps its rule.
Type requirement: two values only, short and long, shown as two forms of one size (a filled and an open form), not as two sizes; the face needs both forms on one width, so that exchanging them moves no neighbour. One weight. A line keeps its measure while its units keep their natural width, so later lines hold more. Dividers fall after a word or a group, drawn lighter than the text, never at a fixed interval. One instruction per view, set large and alone; title and subtitle share one size and differ by value.
Motion and interaction: nothing runs unprompted and nothing loops; trips, pairs and gaps are in the layout. A change moves the lead one rung in 240ms and the companion follows in half that, 120ms: long then short, 2 : 1, the only rhythm. An arrival sets the marker in one step and holds it, with no pulse and no repeat. Counts are exact and timing is the user's, so no timer advances a sequence; where a message must leave by itself, its dwell follows step 6, about 3 s plus 0.4 s per unit it holds. In scaled mode the copies enter 60, 120, 240 and 480ms apart. At the end layers leave one at a time, 240ms each, the last state is cut in 100ms or less, and the view stays empty. Reduced motion: every change is instant and in the same order; nothing is lost, because pairs, marker, gaps and exception are positions, not movements.
Fails when: fewer than 3 sizes or 6 trips fit, so no pair can be compared with the next; the values have no order or no natural home (unranked categories); the companion needs more than three values (many statuses); the view must be dense, since gaps under a tenth of the run stop reading as counted; several things are urgent at once, since there is one exception and the marker may not be borrowed for emphasis; the task must get faster with practice, because trips and gaps only lengthen; the brief asks for a peak or a hero.
Signature (do not carry together): a slow stepwise line shadowed by the three notes of one chord · a low bell stroke followed by a long ringing silence · stemless dots on cream manuscript paper with empty staves left in.

**Brian Eno.** *Set a few plain parts going on cycles of unequal, near lengths over an exactly held frame, leave most of each cycle empty, let every event return weaker by equal steps until it is gone, and change the whole only by opening, shutting or filtering a layer; publish the rules, run them, and tune the rules, never a result.*
Process:
1. Fix the stock and the window. Choose 5–7 kinds of plain part and let five of them cover about nine tenths of everything shown; each part is one thing (one item, one mark, one line) and all sit in one narrow band of size and value. Nothing is added to the stock later. The view is a window on something that could run on: windows are the same size whatever the length of the content, the arrangement is cut by both edges, and there is no opening or closing figure. If the stock changes, every later step is redone.
2. Divide the stock into two kinds and draw them two ways. The held kind (frame, navigation, text, controls) is exact and identical at every return. The travelling kind recurs and shifts. Give each kind one treatment (tint against line, or thick against thin) and never swap them. The travelling kind is thin marks on 2–5% of the surface, or pale tint on up to a quarter of it.
3. Give every travelling part a lane and a cycle of its own, in 2–7 lanes. A cycle is counted in whatever the product already counts: distance along a scroll, rows, items, visits, days. The longest cycle is at most about twice the shortest, neighbours differ by 2–20%, and no cycle is a whole multiple of another, so that at least one pair of lanes does not line up again inside the span a person sees.
4. Leave most of each cycle empty. A part fills one fifth to one third of its cycle, so the rest is at least twice the part. No rules are drawn between lanes: where parts coincide they merge, and where none falls the ground shows and stays unfilled. Check the count over a long span: nothing present about a fifth of the time, two lanes or fewer about four fifths, every lane together almost never. Totals per screen or per day stay level within about a fifth, while short spans run from empty to crowded.
5. Start everything on a hidden slot. Every start falls on a whole multiple of one small unit, with a drift of up to a tenth of the unit; only some slots are used, and the slot itself is never drawn or marked.
6. Let an event come back weaker until it is gone. A new arrival, a touch or a change returns at one fixed spacing and loses an equal step of strength each time, reaching nothing after 10–14 returns. Events made together share a spacing; the next batch draws another from a range of about 1 : 3. Admit the next event in a lane when the last has lost about two thirds of its strength, so that fading balances arriving and density stays level.
7. Let the frame be an exact copy. Compose one block at least 8 turns of the longest cycle in length, fix it by a seed, and repeat it whole. Inside the block nothing lines up; the block itself returns unchanged, so the same seed always gives the same view.
8. Change state by gate and filter only. Open or shut a whole layer, or move one filter across the same stock so that a different band of it comes forward. No new kind of part ever marks a change. Make one such change at a time, at intervals 10 or more times the finest spacing of events.
9. Keep cuts for changes of state. Inside a state a part arrives slowly and leaves more slowly (the exit 2–4 times the entry) and nothing is cut. A gate from step 8 is square: the layer is there or not, whole, in one step.
10. Publish the rules and tune them by running. Show how the arrangement is made (lanes, cycles, what is held, what travels) as a one-line key or a small diagram of the apparatus beside it. Run the rules over a span long enough for the two closest cycles to meet, look at the whole run, then change a cycle, a filter or a layer. Never correct a single result by hand.
Why it works:
- Adaptation to the parts, not to the whole: a plain part on a fixed cycle is learned in two or three returns, and near, unequal cycles keep the combination from being learned at all, so the view stays familiar and never finished. Equalise the cycles and the lanes lock into one grid that is read once and then ignored. Stretch the ratio to ten and the fast lane becomes a texture under the slow one, no longer a peer. Make one a multiple of another and their meeting recurs as a visible stripe.
- Grouping by likeness: the variety is read as overlap only while each part can be recognised alone. Put complexity inside the parts as well as between them and the result is noise. The other way round also holds: an elaborate part may be used if it never varies and only a few per cent of the surface does.
- Figure and ground: with rest at least twice the part, a coincidence of two lanes is a small event found by oddness and an empty span is a pause. Fill the rests and overlaps cannot be told from the parts, and the level becomes one flat tone.
- Constancy as information: an exact repeat is discounted at a glance, so the eye goes to the few per cent that differs with no emphasis on it. A held kind that is only roughly repeated cannot be subtracted, and the travelling kind is lost in it.
- Value as age: equal steps of fading let strength be read as time since the event, and order as rank of strength. Without fading, an item leaves by a cut nobody sees and new ones pile on a full field; with unequal steps the ranking breaks.
- Salience of onset: an abrupt edge takes the eye. Slow edges inside a state leave attention free, and keeping the cut for a change of state makes the cut itself the message. Mark every slot and it becomes a beat that asks to be followed.
- Common spacing groups events made together without a drawn grid, and a slot that is used only now and then orders them without being seen.
Colour relations: the arrangement is close to one hue. Ground takes 70–80% of the surface; one kind is a mid tint on about a tenth and the other is the darkest value on 2–3%, as thin marks (which kind is tint and which is line is free, but fixed once chosen). No hue per lane: lanes are told apart by spacing and length, then by position. Where two tinted parts meet the overlap is simply the next darker tint and is the darkest tint allowed. Fading runs in equal value steps toward the ground. A brand hue stays a flat field outside the arrangement and carries no state. No colour marks what is new.
Space and line: 5–7 kinds of part, five covering about nine tenths; 2–7 lanes; cycles within 2 : 1 and neighbours 2–20% apart; a part one fifth to one third of its cycle; empty about a fifth of the span and two lanes or fewer about four fifths; long-span totals level within a fifth; slot drift up to a tenth; 10–14 fading steps; next admitted at two thirds faded; copy block 8 or more turns of the longest cycle; no rules between lanes; one line weight with square or quarter-round corners; equal windows cut by both edges; rests left open.
Type requirement: text belongs to the held kind: it never fades, drifts or takes part in a cycle. Labels are small and lower-case, set inside or beside the part they name with no box round them, flush left, in two weights at most and in narrow columns with empty space kept beside them. Each window carries its number at one end and its length at the other, so the face needs figures of equal width. The key to the arrangement is one line of plain words.
Motion and interaction: by default nothing runs on a clock; cycles advance on what the person does or on the calendar (scroll, page, visit, day), and the unequal spacing is already in the layout. A part that arrives while the view is open fades in over 600–1200ms and leaves over 2–4 times that; a trace loses one step per refresh or visit and is removed after 10–14. A gate is one step of 100ms or less with no easing. Live arrivals are held to a slot of 2–6s and enter one batch at a time. An ambient mode is allowed only on an unattended or idle surface: each lane has a period of 30s or more, the periods obey step 3, at most one part is changing at any moment, and it stops on the first input. Reduced motion: fades become single steps, the ambient mode is off, and cycles still advance per visit, so the arrangement loses nothing.
Fails when: something must be found in the same place every time and it has been put in the travelling kind (navigation, controls and totals are always held); one item must win the first glance or a change is urgent, since nothing new is marked and arrivals are slow; fewer than three returns of each cycle fall inside what a person sees, so no part is ever learned; the content cannot give up a fifth of its span to rest (dense tables, forms); values must be compared exactly, since overlap and fading blur them; the state must be reproducible and no seed is kept.
Signature (do not carry together): soft glowing shapes that drift and fade by themselves on a timer · pale bars in unruled lanes sliding out of step, shown as the picture · a card that gives a cryptic instruction drawn at random.

**John Cage.** *Fix the frame before the content, divided by one short series used at two scales; fill its cells by a seeded draw from a closed chart in which empty is an entry with a set share; never edit a result, only the chart; run a second layer on an unrelated interval; give windows, not points; join by cuts.*
Process:
1. Fix the frame before any content. A frame is a whole count of equal units (15 × 15 or 16 × 16), or one fixed window of space or time. It never grows or shrinks to suit what goes in it: content is fitted to the frame, set loose when short and tight when long, so that it occupies 35–88% of its frame, and the last 7–13% of every frame stays empty as its close. If the frame changes, every later step is redone.
2. Divide the frame by one short series of 3–5 whole numbers that add up to the count (4-3-2-3-4 for 16; 5-7-3 for 15), and use the same series twice: to group the units into sections, and to divide each unit into its parts. Two scales, one series, no third.
3. Write the chart before filling anything: a closed table of at most 8 × 8 entries per property (kind, span, level). The stock of kinds is small (1–16) and nothing joins it later. Empty is an entry in the chart, and its share is a setting chosen now and stated: 5–10% for a full view, 20–30% for a sparse one, up to 45%. Spans come from the same table for empty and filled cells alike. A scale that cannot be judged finely is cut to two values.
4. Fill by draw, in cell order, from a seed (a person, a date, a place), so the same seed always gives the same view. Each cell takes kind, span and level from the chart. Bound the draw with counts, not with taste: about 5 marks in every 12–15 units, no gap over 4 units, mean gap near 3.
5. Never edit a result. If a view is unacceptable, change the chart or a bound and draw again. Do not sort, grade or centre the outcome afterwards: levels fall in steps, neighbours may differ by the whole range, and there is no build toward a peak.
6. Add at most one second layer, on an interval of its own that is not a multiple of the first; the two share only the frame's outer edges. Make the second layer one unchanging mark or one continuous kind of line, 2–4% of the first layer's rate, and let it begin before the first layer and end after it. Neither layer is snapped to the other.
7. Give a window where a point is not needed. A start and an end are each a range covering about 60% of the span and overlapping by about 20% (start anywhere in 0–45, end anywhere in 30–75), and whoever receives the view (the reader, the device, the data) settles the exact value. About one item in ten may be pinned exactly.
8. Join by a cut at a frame boundary. A new section is a change of the whole group of kinds at once, edge to edge, with no blend; inside a section the group does not change.
9. State one conversion and keep to it ("1 unit = …"), printed on the view, so every length can be read as a quantity. Each kind of mark carries one property and no other: line pattern names the layer, distance from a datum gives the value, a label at each end of a line fixes it. Where nothing was drawn, nothing is put.
Empty mode (in place of steps 3–5): keep the frame, its divisions and the conversion, and put nothing in. What the receiver brings is the content, and the frame's edges are the only marks.
Why it works:
- Scale reference: when the frame holds still, how full it is becomes a quantity the eye can compare from cell to cell. Let the frame grow with its content and every cell looks equally full; the amount has gone into the layout and cannot be read off it.
- Figure and ground: an empty cell with the same edges and the same span rule as a full one is read as a member of the set. Take away the frame or give blanks their own rule, and the same blank reads as a fault or as something still loading.
- Grouping by texture: an unsorted draw from a small closed stock makes one even texture without a figure, so the eye stops ranking and reads any cell on its own terms. The bound on gaps is what keeps it even: without it chance makes clumps, and a clump is read as a figure nobody intended. Sort or grade the outcome and it becomes a ramp that promises a climax.
- Adaptation: with no regular recurrence there is no expected next state, so no item is odd and none is salient by accident. Put the same draw on a steady beat and every departure from the beat turns into an accent.
- Grouping by common change: two layers that never move or step together separate without tint or depth. Align their intervals and they fuse into one pattern whose coincidences read as meaning.
- One series at two scales lets a part stand for the whole: someone who has read one unit can place themselves in the frame without a map. Hard joins keep that structure legible, since the frame is the only regular thing on the view.
Colour relations: two values and nothing else: a ground of 87–97% and marks of 0.3–3.3% (up to 7–8% where the view is running text). Kinds are told apart by line pattern and position, never by hue. One saturated tint at most, under about 2.5% of the view, and it belongs to the receiver's own marks (annotations, selections), never to the drawn content. Level is one of a few stepped values taken from the chart; colour does not mark what matters, because nothing is ranked.
Space and line: frame 15 × 15 or 16 × 16 units; series of 3–5 terms at two scales; chart at most 8 × 8; empty share 5–45% by setting; content 35–88% of its frame; closing margin 7–13% of the frame; about 5 marks per 12–15 units, no gap over 4, mean near 3; blank runs of 26–52% of a row are normal, and a view may be 60% empty with its block set low (first line near 57% of the height); one line weight per kind and up to four kinds by pattern (solid, dotted, dashed, dash-dot); the measuring grid is ruled and regular, and what lies on it is free.
Type requirement: text is fitted to a fixed frame, so the face must stay legible across a range of setting widths, from loose to tight, at one size. Figures of equal width, with a clean sign for a range, since quantities are written as windows ("0–45 ↔ 30–75") and as one conversion line. Blocks of 2–10 lines, each keyed by a letter or number larger than the text, in up to four columns. One weight; no emphasis style, since nothing is ranked.
Motion and interaction: nothing has to move, because the draw is in the layout. A seed holds for a stated period (a session, a day), so the view never rearranges under the pointer; at the period's end the new draw lands as one cut of 100ms or less, at a frame boundary. An update without urgency may land anywhere inside its window and need not be on a beat. If the second layer is shown in time, it is one identical mark lasting 1–1.5s, at gaps of 1–4 beats of 3s, never regular and never faster. Controls, navigation and anything a user must find again are outside the draw and keep fixed places. Reduced motion: the second layer is drawn as still marks at its positions and every change is instant; nothing is lost, because no step depended on movement.
Fails when: one item must win the first glance, or the items have a rank or an order that matters (search results, chronology, steps); there are fewer than about 12 cells, so a draw reads as a mistake and an empty share cannot be seen; content cannot be fitted to a fixed frame (legal text, forms, long tables); a blank would be taken for a failure and cannot be explained on the view; an update is urgent, since a window delays it; the same thing has to be in the same place for every person.
Signature (do not carry together): nearly blank cream pages with a few ruled lines and traced curves · transparent sheets of points and loops dropped over a tilted grid · a titled, timed stretch of nothing presented as the work.

### T.2 VISUAL MUSIC: abstract film composed with sound
The movement: abstract film planned like a score, where a figure's birth, motion and decay are composed against sound. Carry the event list, the ruler, the decay law, the residue, the shared period and the blank; leave the circles, scratches, dot grids and music-visualiser look behind.
*The problem it answered:* How do you make motion mean something without a story or a picture? The answers here list the events before drawing, land them whole, let them decay on one law, move at constant speed unless something breaks, and spend stillness and unison where they count.

**Oskar Fischinger.** *Rule the time before the form: give every event a start and a length on one ruler, open its figure from a seed in one to five units, let it decay in place many times longer on one law, overlap the next, keep a thin residue, and spend the residue in one unison per section before a short blank.*
Process:
1. Rule the time first. Fix one unit (about 40ms) and write every event the view can show as a span on one ruler: a start and a length, both in whole units. Nest three rates: a pulse of 4–10 units (170–420ms), a phrase of 4–7 pulses (1.0–3.2s), a section of 8–25s. An event whose end cannot be marked gets no motion. If the unit changes, every later step is redone.
2. Give each span one envelope, and make it lopsided: full strength within 1–5 units of its start (one unit for the strongest event), then a decay 2–80 times longer than the attack. The reversed envelope (3–4 units up, 1–2 down) is kept for step 7 and for nothing else.
3. Give each figure one home position and two small ends. It opens from a seed (a dot or a hairline, a tenth of its peak or less) at the place where it will live, and closes to a hairline or a dot at the same place. Nothing fades in or out as a whole. One state turns into another through that minimum (form, line, form), so the line is the state every form shares.
4. Bind size and colour to age by one law per phrase: an equal step each unit, a constant ratio each unit (halving or doubling), or a constant speed of recession (the reciprocal of the size grows by equal steps). The law holds from the first unit to the last and resets in one unit. Colour walks one way along a path of 3–5 stops as the figure ages, starting at the path's lightest or most saturated end. A phrase that recurs keeps its length to the unit; only what it carries changes.
5. Overlap the spans and keep the residue. The next figure opens 40–300ms before the last has closed, so the field does not empty inside a phrase. A finished figure stays at its home as a thin still mark, a tenth to a thirtieth of its peak, drifting less than 2% of the view per second. The count of marks (from 3 up to 20–45 in a section) is the record of what has happened.
6. Run two clocks. One layer cycles at least 5 times slower than the other (2.5–3.2s against 0.2–0.5s) and the two never share a period. The ground is a third voice: it may change whole in one unit to mark a section and holds the new state for at least 750ms.
7. Spend the residue once per section. After the quietest stretch, all the marks swell together (to 10–18% of the view in 4 units, back in 2), then nothing moves for 120–170ms, then the next section starts from about three seeds. The largest unison comes in the last quarter. Up to that point about three families of form take turns and do not mix.
8. Lead the accent. Start an attack 60–120ms before the moment it marks, so the peak and the moment coincide. In an interface the attack starts at the input, not at the reply.
9. Write the still form of every event before animating it: where the figure sits, which stop of its colour path states its age, and what mark it leaves. If an event has no still form it has no span, and it is cut.
Why it works:
- An abrupt onset is found by the eye without search (salience by transient), and a slow decay leaves time to see what arrived and where it went. Equalise the two (ease in, ease out) and the peak falls mid-way, so the event has no moment and is felt late. Reverse them and it reads as a departure.
- A figure that opens and closes at one place is read as one object with a life (object persistence), so a change of state is seen as the same thing changing. A whole-view fade has no location until it is half there, and it wipes the record. Remove the residue and the user has to remember the count.
- Parts that move together are grouped as one (common fate). After a stretch in which every mark kept its own time, a single unison reads as one event made of all of them. Multiply the unison and the field becomes one object that blinks; the parts can no longer be told apart.
- Two layers at clearly different rates separate without shadow or overlap, and the slow one gives the fast one a reference. With equal periods they fuse into one pattern.
- A change that begins at its cause, within about a tenth of a second, is bound to it. One that begins after is read as a separate, later event: lag.
- A law and a length that repeat exactly set the rate the eye expects (adaptation), so any difference in a repeat is read as content. Durations tuned one by one have to be read again each time.
- Colour bound to age puts elapsed time into a single still frame, which is what lets the whole logic survive with motion off.
Colour relations: the ground is one dark value or one flat hue on 67–85% of the view; figures average 1–4% and peak at 10–18%; a figure stands 25–80 L from the ground, except where meeting the ground is the event; one opposed pair of hues with a shared middle that both can travel to; one hue family per section; the attack takes the path's lightest or most saturated stop and age moves it one way (darker, cooler or warmer, chosen once); where two figures overlap the overlap is a planned third colour; the residue takes the path's quietest stop and about 1–2.5% of the view in sum.
Space and line: figures are small in a large ground; the centre is kept for the stillest or the strongest event; a lead sits off-centre, near 0.3 of the width, and answers mirror about the vertical axis; position carries order (each successor one step along a diagonal, or rising as it lives); depth comes from size alone (1 down to 0.3); figures may cross the edge of the field; about three families of form per section, with the hairline as their common minimum; a leading edge is crisp, and a trailing edge may be soft to show direction; anything near the size of the view lasts 10 units or less.
Type requirement: text is never a figure. It arrives whole in one unit, holds its size and its place, and shows age by a step of weight or tone, so the family needs at least three such steps (new, settled, residue). Figures are tabular, so a changing number does not move its neighbours from their home positions. Every span is also stated in words or digits (a count, a time, "3 of 20"), because the ruler has to be readable without the motion.
Motion and interaction: unit about 40ms; attack 40–200ms with no easing (a hard step, or an equal step per unit); decay 80ms–4s on the phrase's law (linear, halving per unit, or reciprocal), never ease-in-out; pulse 170–420ms; phrase 1.0–3.2s, repeated to the unit; overlap 40–300ms; unison 160ms up and 80ms down, once per section; blank 120–170ms; a whole-ground change holds 750ms or more. Feedback starts on press and peaks on the reply. Events that arrive faster than the pulse are gathered into one figure per pulse. No part of the view passes from light to dark more than 3 times a second, and the ground never alternates. Nothing loops while no event is in progress. Reduced motion: every figure appears in one step at its residue size at its home, age is read from the colour stop and the written count, and the unison becomes one change of tone on the marks plus a line of text; order, overlap of spans, residue and the single summing moment all remain.
Fails when: an event's end cannot be known (an open-ended wait has no span, so only the slow clock and a plain statement of time are honest); the view has no events (long reading, reference tables), where nothing should move; events outrun the pulse for a whole section; fewer than about three events fall in a section, so there is no residue to spend and the unison is empty show; a message must stay until it is acknowledged (errors, destructive confirmations), since decay would remove it; the record must not be visible to others on a shared screen.
Signature (do not carry together): pale hand-drawn crescents and hairlines swelling on black · saturated discs and concentric rings pulsing on a flat blue ground · every figure struck to the notes of an orchestral score.

**Norman McLaren.** *Set one limit (one mark, one operation) and count the events in time units before drawing; land each mark whole on its event, let it decay front-first and leave its place empty; bind one slow line strictly and mark about half of the rest; move at constant speed, accelerating only into a break; multiply by one slow rule, then return to one.*
Process:
1. Set the limit. One mark and one operation for the whole view: appear and decay, travel along one axis, or copy with a delay. A second shape is allowed only for a second voice. Ground, weight and the section's colour are locked from here on, and all expression goes into what differs between one moment and the next. If the limit changes, every later step is redone.
2. Count the script before drawing anything. List the events with their times and lengths in units of about 40 ms. Choose two or three tempo levels at least 4 times apart (unit 40 ms : slot about 160 ms : cycle about 1.3 s : generation about 15 s : the whole sequence) and use nothing between them; every duration in the view is a whole multiple of the level below it.
3. Write duration as extent. A sustained state gets one line that runs 90% or more of the view's length, 1–3.5% of the short side thick, and stands exactly as long as the state does. An instant gets one small mark, 0.5–4.5% of the view (typically about 1.4%). The rarest, heaviest event gets a mark about 10 times the usual one, once or twice in a sequence.
4. Split the events into one strict line and a loose remainder. The strict line is slow (about one event a second while it is being learnt) and bound in everything: one mark per event with no exceptions, its position stepping along one axis with one attribute of the event and its size with another. Of all the other events only 40–50% get a mark, on time; the rest pass unmarked. Never use one mapping for everything.
5. Arrive whole, decay front-first, then rest. The mark is at full size and contrast on the event's own unit, with no grow-in. It loses about half in the first 1–2 units (80 ms), 90% by about 330 ms and its last trace by 0.8–0.9 s; the briefest kind is on for 1 unit of a 4-unit slot. Its place then stays empty for 2–6 units (80–250 ms) before anything else arrives there. In a sparse passage 25–45% of the time shows the ground alone, and a pause in the events is a pause on screen.
6. Use three motions and no others. Travel at constant speed along one axis (0.10–0.15 of the view per second when it idles; a crossing sweep is on screen 170–580 ms), turning within one unit at a fixed line about 0.15 in from each edge, with no slowing. A pose held 4–6 units and changed in one. Acceleration only for a thing about to end: from standing still, each step 1.5–3.3 times the last over 6–7 units, finishing on the event that replaces it.
7. Grow density by one rule, never by hand. Either a slow clock admits one new unit per fixed interval (about a twentieth of the sequence), or a moving thing leaves delayed copies of itself (3–5 units apart, at most 11, about 2 s of its past) that exist only while it moves and fold into one within about 0.75 s when it stops. Counts run from 1 to 10–35, coverage from under 2% to about 30%.
8. Compose the whole on the count. Begin with one unit; put the fullest passage 80–90% of the way through; clear the view completely for a beat before the largest picture; end on one unit, still. Colour holds inside a section and changes only at a section boundary, where the ground may retint over 1.5–13 s as a new unit arrives.
Why it works:
- An abrupt onset has an instant and takes the eye; a ramp has none, so an eased arrival cannot be on its event. A decay that spends half its strength at once clears the place, so the next arrival is read as a new event; fade it evenly and neighbours smear into a texture.
- Figure and ground in time: the empty interval is what separates two events and resets adaptation. Remove the gaps and the hits become a steady flicker that the eye stops reading. A view that rests when the events rest is trusted to mean something when it moves.
- One strict line teaches the code, and the loose remainder keeps it from turning into a meter. Bind everything and the viewer stops looking, because each mark is predicted; bind nothing and marks meet events no more often than chance. Measured: 88% of strict births match an event against 41% by chance, and loose passages sit near half.
- Constant speed makes position a reading of elapsed time and makes no false promise of arrival. Acceleration is then odd, so it is salient, and when it is kept for endings the viewer learns that something speeding up is about to be replaced.
- Units born from one rule are grouped by likeness and common fate, so thirty read as one growing thing, and the count is a clock. Copies that exist only during movement make their spread a reading of speed and their folding a reading of rest. After the fullest passage one unit on an empty ground is the strongest contrast left, so the close needs no effect.
Colour relations: one flat ground takes 80–98% of the view and sits at an end of the value or chroma scale; marks cover 0.2–7% in sparse passages and up to about 30% at the peak; 2–3 hues per section, chosen for the section and held, never per item; the mark is opposite the ground in value or in hue; the widest hue contrast and the highest chroma are kept for the fullest passage, the quiet ones stay in one hue family; ground changes are few (10–17 in five minutes) and gradual; one mode has no hue at all. A swap of ground and mark is allowed for a whole cycle group (4 cycles), entered through 1–3 empty units.
Space and line: no depth, no horizon, no container; marks in the middle third or anywhere in turn; lines and sweeps enter and leave at the edge and run the full extent; equal columns of about 9% of the width, 1 to 11 of them, each filled from one side in proportion to strength; mirror symmetry about the centre axis only while two voices speak together; 1–3 line weights between 1% and 3.5% of the short side, the thicker allowed to move faster; the small irregularities of the tool are kept.
Type requirement: words are the layer that stays. Each event that must be read later has one line of text that appears whole in one step and does not decay; the mark is the event, the text is the state. Labels stand on either side of a mark, never on it. One sturdy weight that holds on a full-strength ground at one-step appearance; figures tabular, changing by whole steps on the slot or the cycle, never rolling.
Motion and interaction: arrival in one frame on the input or the event; decay to half in 80 ms, to a tenth in about 330 ms, gone by 800–900 ms, on a curve that is steep first and thin after; 80–250 ms of empty place after it; at most 3 arrivals a second in the view, faster events being gathered into the next slot as one mark; poses held 170–250 ms and switched in one frame; a reacting neighbour recoils in about 230 ms and recovers in about 580 ms; travel linear, turns in one frame; the only accelerating curve is the exit of a thing being replaced, 250–300 ms, ending on the replacement's first frame; an outline may be redrawn into another over 250–500 ms without a cut; copies trail 125–210 ms apart and fold in 750 ms. No ease-out, no ease-in-out, no overshoot. Marks that alternate faster than 3 a second, or that cover more than a few percent of the view in one frame, are not carried. Reduced motion: a mark appears whole and is removed whole at the end of its life, or stays until the next of its kind replaces it; travel becomes placement at the end position; the accelerating exit becomes a one-step swap; copies are not drawn, and one still marker shows the previous position while the value is changing; the slow clock still admits one unit per interval, in whole steps. The strict line's positions and sizes, the empty intervals, the count and the return to one are unchanged.
Fails when: events that must each be acknowledged come faster than 3 a second (only the loose half and the text count are left); a state has to stay visible and be read at leisure with no room for a text line (a decaying mark carries an event, never a state); an alert must not go away until it is answered; nothing discrete happens (long reading, a form being filled), where nothing should move; the content needs several unlike marks at once; the view has to look full at every moment (a hero, a shop window), since a quarter to a half of the time is ground; exact magnitudes must be read from the mark alone.
Signature (do not carry together): hand-scratched figures that flash for single frames on black · ruled lines splitting and regrouping on one flat saturated field · white ghost-multiples trailing a moving body.

**Len Lye.** *Cut the run into short phrases at the joins the process itself has; hold one figure and one ground through each phrase and change one of them in a single step; redraw a live thing in place and move a travelling one at constant speed; break often with short empty beats, and still nearly everything while words are read.*
Process:
1. Find the phrases before any form. List the joins the process has of its own accord: a source or stage changes, it pauses, it ends. A phrase runs 1–8s (a figure class holds 2–8s, a single statement 1.2–2.4s). Everything below is timed to these joins, and the events inside a phrase are not marked one by one. If the joins move, every later step is redone.
2. Lock one figure class and one ground per phrase. The figure is one kind of mark in one width and one colour: a band 4–15% of the view's width, or 1–7 hairlines of 1–3% each. The ground is one flat field. The figure covers 1–11% of the view on the resting ground and 10–30% on a coloured one, sits off-centre and does not reach all four edges.
3. At a join change one of the two, in a single step. Either the ground is replaced under a figure that stays (no blend, and the new ground differs in hue from the last), or the ground holds and the figure jumps to a second place or changes class. A ground lasts 0.5–1.1s on a steady clock in the works and the intervals shorten to a half or less just before a passage ends; in an interface the swap happens at a join only, and no more than once a second.
4. Give the figure kinds of motion, not degrees of speed. Going somewhere: a glide at constant speed (a slow class at 0.3–0.4 of the view per second, a fast class at 0.7–1.2), with no ease in or out and the turnaround in one step. Working: redrawn in place, every drawing different in bend and position and the same in width and colour (in the works 20–25 drawings a second, 0.15–0.33 of the width apart). Resting: straight and still, about 0.5s. A figure is in one state at a time.
5. Start whole and small, finish thin. A figure appears in one step in its least form (1–3 strokes), swells to full in 0.1–0.4s, and leaves by thinning over 0.2–0.4s or in one step. Nothing cross-fades into its successor and nothing assembles itself slowly.
6. Breathe. Show the ground alone between two states and at every pause of the process: 0.1–0.2s inside a phrase, 0.5–0.7s at a join, never longer than about 1s, and 7–25% of the running time in all. A pause in the process is the empty ground, never a frozen figure.
7. Meet the process at its joins and nowhere else. When the source or stage changes, the picture changes with the cause: up to 20–70ms ahead of it, never after. A rest shows the empty ground. Between joins the amount of marking follows the level of activity loosely, 0.3–0.4s behind it. Give each source one direction of line and keep it for the whole run.
8. Lay a path out as counted copies when an action has to be seen after it has passed. Leave one copy at each pose, up to 5, each in its own flat colour and overlapping the last by a third to a half. Build over about 1.2s, hold the full set for 1s, then remove oldest first, 0.2–0.4s apart, until only the live figure is left.
9. Quiet the view for words. When something must be read, bring the amount of change down to between a tenth and four tenths of the running level, on a plain ground. Show one to three words, or one figure and its unit, at a time. It dwells nearly still (creeping at 0.1 of the width per second), then leaves at 0.6–1.5 widths per second: the only accelerated move in the piece. The next may overlap it for about a quarter of its life, in a different colour.
10. For an action that gathers before it commits, use load, snap, ring: a slow visible load (the figure widens to about 2.4 times), a release in a tenth to a sixth of the load's time, a recovery a little longer than the release, then two to four swings, each smaller than the last, about 0.6s apart.
11. Compose the density of the whole run. Either rise to a plateau, rest where the words come and close on a short coda at about half the plateau; or open dense, run a long sparse middle at about a quarter of the opening and return twice, briefly, near the end. The busiest stretch of the process need not be the busiest stretch of the picture.
12. Write the still form, one frame per phrase. The ground states the phrase, with its name in words. The figure's shape states its condition: straight for resting or travelling, bent for working, absent for a pause. The copies state the count, with the figure beside them. If these frames do not tell the run, the joins were chosen wrongly (step 1).
Why it works:
- A figure that keeps its width and colour while the ground is replaced stays one object (grouping by likeness), so the change is read as a change in its situation and not as a new scene. Because the eye has adapted to the held ground, a one-step swap is salient with nothing added. Blend the two grounds and a third, mixed state appears that means nothing and smears the moment; change figure and ground together and the thread is cut.
- A drawing repeated exactly is predicted after one cycle and then ignored. Redrawing with small differences in bend and place keeps the figure odd enough to be watched, and holding width and colour keeps it the same thing. Vary the width or the colour too and it reads as several objects; loop it and it reads as a machine idling.
- Travel and agitation in place differ in kind, so the state is read without comparing speeds. The empty beat between them clears adaptation, and the second state is seen fresh.
- Constant speed reads as a law: the position can be predicted, so attention goes to what the thing is and where it is heading. An ease adds a small start and a small stop to every move. With every glide constant, the one acceleration (a word leaving, a release) is salient by oddness.
- A gap under about a fifth of a second is bridged by persistence, so it divides the stream into phrases without losing the viewer's place. A gap over a second reads as finished or broken.
- A claim of coincidence is made only where it can be kept. A change of source is the one event that both streams state clearly. When events come four to nine a second, a mark on each is indistinguishable from chance and too fast to read. Leading by a few hundredths of a second makes the picture read as part of the cause; trailing makes it read as an echo.
- Copies in separate colours stay separate figures where they overlap, so a movement in time is laid out in space and can be counted. Removing them oldest first restates the order and ends on the one that is live.
- Reading needs a steady fixation and motion elsewhere pulls it away. Since the view has been busy, the calm is itself the signal that words have arrived.
- A slow load builds expectation, the short release is salient against it, and the shrinking swings show the energy leaving, so the end is seen as settled and not as stopped.
Colour relations: one flat ground at a time. On a resting ground at one end of the value scale the figure takes 1–11% and the ground 85–99%; on a coloured ground the figure takes 10–30%. The figure is the extreme value in the view, opposite the resting ground, and has almost no chroma; the grounds carry the chroma, and successive grounds differ in hue and never blend. With a resting ground, one strong hue may hold 20–50% of a phrase and a second stays under 13%. Where shapes carry the colour, a neutral holds the larger share and colour stays under about a third. Counted copies take one flat colour each. The ground under words is pale and plain, about 80% of the view, with the words in two hues at 1–3%. No mid-value neutral sits between the extremes. Inverse mode: a light ground of 40–80% with the marks dark.
Space and line: one field edge to edge, marks running along one axis, the field divided into 1–14 parallel bands; a figure 10–60% of the width, off-centre, not touching all four edges; depth from overlap only; a pattern or texture belongs to the object it fills and stops at that object's edge, never at the frame's; a band 4–15% of the width, a hairline 1–3%, 7–25 lines when lines run parallel; from one drawing to the next a figure differs in bend and place, never in width.
Type requirement: words are objects, not lines of text: one to three words, or one figure and one unit, alone on a plain field. They need a sturdy face with open counters that holds at display size on a strong flat ground (letter height an eighth to a quarter of the field's height), in two colours so that successive words can cross. Running text takes no part: it sits outside the field, still.
Motion and interaction: glide linear at 0.3–0.4 or 0.7–1.2 of the view per second, reversal in one step; swell 0.1–0.4s; thinning 0.2–0.4s; empty beat 0.1–0.2s, 0.5–0.7s at a join, 1s at most; ground swap in one step with no fade; copies in over 1.2s, held 1s, out 0.2–0.4s apart; word dwell, then exit at 0.6–1.5 widths per second; load to release 6–10 : 1. In an interface a redraw is fired by a real tick of the process (a chunk, a sample, a line), at most 8 a second, on a figure under 5% of the view that never blinks off, and only while the process runs; a ground swap comes at a join only, at most once a second, on a bounded region or within about 10 L of the last ground, so it is a change of hue and not a flash; empty beats come no more than twice a second; a word stays until it has been read or acted on. Idle, nothing moves. The works' 0.2s colour clock and frame-rate redraw are not carried. Reduced motion: one still frame per phrase, changed in one step at the join; the working figure is drawn bent and still, the travelling one at its destination; the pause is the empty ground with its label; copies appear whole with their count and leave together; load and release are two still states. Phrase, state, pause and count all remain.
Fails when: the process has no joins (a steady stream, a monitor that never changes stage); a value must be read exactly at every instant, since a redrawn figure is noise against a scale; the user must read or press the figure while it moves; every event needs its own confirmation (typing, each tap), which phrase timing does not give; several independent processes share one view, so several figures would work at once; long reading; a view with no second ground distinct from the first.
Signature (do not carry together): white scratched lines trembling on black · full-chroma dye grounds swapped under brushed or combed white ribbons, with stencil lettering · a dance-band or drum record running under the picture.

**Mary Ellen Bute.** *List the events first; give each kind one plain mark at a fixed seat on a still carrier; a mark either stays (count) or flashes (acknowledge), never both; time to the frame only sparse, sharp events and carry the rest by slow light; raise emphasis by peak or rate; close on one held figure.*
Process:
1. Write the event list before any drawing. Name every kind of event the view will show, its expected rate, and whether it is struck (an instant: a press, a receipt, a refusal) or sustained (something that builds: steps done, items in). Keep 1–3 kinds per view. The list fixes every count that follows: n events of a kind get n marks, no more. If the list changes, every later step is redone.
2. Set the carrier. One field or one large plain form takes 30–99% of the view and does not move, reflow or scroll while events arrive; one ground value per section. All marks together stay small against it: 0.5–6% of the view for a single voice, up to about 20% when a build is complete.
3. Give each kind one plain mark and a seat rule. The mark's form never changes; only its count, size, position and light may. Position follows a rule set here: 2–4 fixed seats that alternate and carry the event's attribute (which source, which outcome), or equal steps along one fixed axis from an edge (each step about 0.05 of the view's height).
4. Decide for each kind: it stays or it flashes, never both. A sustained kind accumulates: each event adds one unit at the next seat, lit by a fade of 0.6–0.9 s, and the unit never moves again. A struck kind flashes: on in one frame, alive 100–200 ms, off in one frame, with the seat empty for at least 150 ms before it can fire again (in an interface, at least 400 ms between two flashes at one seat).
5. Spend exact timing; do not spread it. Only kinds that are sparse and sharp (gaps of 0.25 s or more) are timed to the event: the mark lands within 50 ms, and where there is an offset the mark is early (30–300 ms), never late. Every other stream gets no per-event mark: one slow property of the carrier (its light or its lit share) follows the level averaged over about 2 s.
6. Use three kinds of change and no others: a hard on or off in one frame; travel or growth at constant speed from an edge (0.3–1.0 of the view's dimension per second), with a reset in one frame; and a change of light on a thing that stays put (0.6–0.9 s to arrive, about 1.2 s to fill a set of 12). Scale grows or decays by a constant ratio (4–6% per 0.1 s up; a short hold, then about a fifth lost per 40 ms down). If a second clock is needed, put it on the carrier at about 4 times the mark's interval, linear both ways and at zero on each fourth beat; never a third.
7. Raise emphasis by peak or by rate, at an unchanged beat: each peak about 1.3–1.5 times the last in area, or the interval halved (1, 1/2, 1/4) for the close. To broaden instead, stretch the interval to about double across 8 steps. Never add smoothness or speed to the motion itself.
8. Compose density over the whole sequence. Inside a section it grows only by adding copies of the same mark, in a near straight line. At a section break it drops at once to one figure on the ground (under 2%). The fullest passage sits 60–90% of the way through and is followed by a short close on one figure, complete and held still 0.5–1.0 s. When nothing is happening, the last complete figure stays, still; the view is never blank and never idling.
Why it works:
- One event, one mark at a known seat makes the picture countable: the number of marks is the number of events, read without a numeral, and the seat says which kind before the form is resolved. A mark that both stays and flashes cannot be read as either a state or an event. Multiply the kinds and the seats stop being learnable within two or three repeats.
- Salience by oddness in time: on a carrier that does not move, a mark of half a percent alive for a tenth of a second is seen. Move the carrier and every mark has to grow to compete; equalise carrier and mark and the view becomes texture.
- A hard onset has an instant; an eased one has none, so it cannot be on time. Constant speed makes position a reading of elapsed time (halfway along is halfway through), which an ease hides. A mark that arrives with or just before its event is taken as the event itself; one that arrives after is read as lag.
- Adaptation: marks on every event become a steady flicker and coincide with the events no more often than chance. Exact hits read as caused only against passages that do not hit, and a peak reads as louder only against the previous peak on the same beat.
- After the fullest passage one small figure on an empty ground is the strongest contrast available, so the close needs no added effect. A complete figure held still reads as "done"; a blank reads as "broken".
Colour relations: one ground at an end of the value scale, 70–99% of the view in sparse sections and changed only by a cut at a section break; at most 2–3 inks per section; the highest chroma is the smallest thing (the struck mark, 0.5–1%) and sits on the ground furthest from it in value; a carrier form may take about 30% in a strong hue if it never moves; third colours come only from the overlap of two inks already present; swapping ground and figure is one accent, used once in a sequence. Seat and form carry the meaning, colour only repeats it.
Space and line: three placements only: dead centre, growing from one edge, or stepping along one diagonal; a single figure 0.25–0.5 of the view's width, copies 0.05–0.15; accumulated units of equal rise with a constant sideways offset; 10–25 copies when a figure is multiplied, then back to one; one line weight per view; mirror symmetry for the full ensemble only, asymmetry for a single voice; depth by overlap and stepped size.
Type requirement: words are events of the staying kind. A label appears at its seat with its event, on in one step, and remains; text never flashes and never travels. Before anything moves, a legend introduces each mark once with its meaning. One weight; counts in tabular figures that change by whole steps, never rolling.
Motion and interaction: struck feedback on within 50 ms of the input, alive 100–200 ms, at most one flash per seat per 400 ms and never above 3 per second in the view, on marks under 6% of the view; staying units fade in over 600–900 ms, linear; entrances at constant speed (a full-height travel in 1–3 s); a hold of 500–1000 ms on every completed figure; linear, step or constant-ratio only, no ease-in-out, no overshoot; a section break is a cut. The slow clock runs only while work runs and stops with it. Where a result is not yet confirmed, the flash may lead (it answers the input) and the staying unit waits for the confirmation. Reduced motion: a struck mark appears at its seat in one step and stays until the next event of its kind replaces it; staying units appear whole; travel becomes placement at the end position; the slow clock stops at its mean; peaks keep their sizes. Count, seat, order and the held close are unchanged.
Fails when: events come faster than about 3 a second or cannot be counted (a log tail, tick-rate prices), where only the slow level is left; nothing discrete happens (long reading, a form being filled), where nothing should move; more than 3 kinds must be shown at once; the magnitude of each event must be read exactly (money, health), which a mark cannot carry without a figure beside it; the act is irreversible and a leading mark would claim a result not yet true; the view is too small to keep a still carrier around the marks.
Signature (do not carry together): lit rings, stairs and pyramids on a black void · a red outline triangle behind a black grid on drifting blue · oscilloscope ribbons timed to a well-known piece of music.

**John Whitney.** *Give every member of a set the same rule of motion and a whole-number rate of its own on one shared period, so that order is the brief moment when the rates agree at a simple fraction: drift between, slow and dwell there, enter copies at a fixed step, and start each new thing in the gap the last one leaves.*
Process:
1. Fix the set and its one rule. Take 16–360 identical members (below about 12 nothing can resolve) and give them one start, one path and one direction. A member has a number, its rank in the set, and nothing else of its own: no hand placement, no private easing, no drift outside the rule. If the rule changes, every later step is redone.
2. Number the rates on one period. Fix one long period (24–40s when it plays by itself) and let member k complete k laps of the path in it, or, as a chain, follow the leader k fixed steps behind (0.25s each, about 0.08 of the view apart). Rates are whole numbers only. The whole state is then a function of one number, the phase, which a clock, a real process or the user's hand can drive forward or back.
3. Choose the fractions before drawing anything. At a half, a third, a quarter or a fifth of the period the members whose rates share that divisor coincide, and the set shows as many groups as the denominator (three at each third, five at each fifth; rates of 3 : 4 : 5 resolve in turn inside one period). Decide which fractions the view will use and what each one means. A fraction with no meaning is passed without a stop.

Left alone an order lasts 60–130ms, too short to read, so slow the phase toward each chosen fraction and dwell there for 1–3.5s, nearly still for 1s of it; an order may take up to 2.4s to firm. The first order is given away at the start: the aligned set, small and still, for at least 0.5s before it opens.
5. Fix the speed laws, one per kind of motion. Along a path: constant speed (0.2–0.3 of the view per second for the fastest member). Where a motion reverses: sine, fastest in the middle and nought at each end. Settling to rest: ease out over about 0.6s, each step half to two thirds of the last. A figure that leaves by growing: each doubling quicker than the last. Nothing eases in and out along a path.
6. Loop exactly and vary by phase. A short loop (2–4s) repeats to the frame. Change comes from a second cycle of another length laid over it (one colour cycle to three shape loops), never from editing a repeat. One property is in transition at a time: colour either slides over a whole loop or a 1–2s fade, or it snaps while the shape moves on, and a snap recurs no more than once a second.

8. Succeed without a cut. The next figure starts small (0.07–0.2 of the view's height) in the gap at the centre of the one that is leaving; the two overlap for about a tenth of the shorter life; the old one leaves by growing past the edge or closing to a point. The edge is a seam: what leaves one side enters the opposite side.

Let single moments coincide only at the opening and at section marks, within 30–170ms of the cue. Between them the amount of motion follows the amount of activity over seconds, not each event. Blanks between sections shorten (to about a half, then level) while the outer process runs on.

Why it works:
- Members that share a position or a direction are grouped as one (proximity, common fate). With whole-number rates the groups form and dissolve by themselves, so an event appears that nobody drew and the eye reads it as the set agreeing. Equalise the rates and the set is one rigid object with no event. Take the rates off whole numbers and it never returns: noise.
- The long drift sets what the eye expects (adaptation), so order is salient by oddness. Keep order on screen all the time and it is a static ornament; multiply mirrors and it is a kaleidoscope. Remove the dwell and order passes under the threshold of reading.
- Constant speed is read as a law, so attention goes to the relations between members and not to each one's start and stop. A sine at a reversal is what steady turning looks like from the side, so the motion reads as continuing, not as braking.

A snap happens at most once a second and a whole-ground snap marks a section only; no strobing, no full-field flashes. Reduced motion: show the set only at its chosen fractions, stepping from one order to the next in a single change with the fraction in figures; voices become still copies set side by side at their step; drift is not shown. Order, fractions, voices and succession all remain.
Fails when: the set has fewer than about 12 members, or they are unequal, or their rank means nothing, so the rates are arbitrary; no period exists (an open-ended wait has no fractions to resolve at); the user must read or press the members while they move; the state must be legible at every instant (monitoring, tables under comparison), since drift says nothing; each event needs its own confirmation, which a shared grid does not give; long reading, where nothing should move.
Signature (do not carry together): fine points or hairlines blooming into rosettes and looped curves at the centre of a black field · one saturated hue per layer turning through the spectrum · a droning or plucked score running under the pattern.
