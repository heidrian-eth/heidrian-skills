# The Nasrid workshops at the Alhambra — Islamic Geometry

Makers: anonymous tile cutters (alicatado), plaster carvers and moulders (yeseria), carpenters (lazo, ataujerado) and court poets (Ibn al-Yayyab, Ibn al-Khatib, Ibn Zamrak) working for the Nasrid sultans, mainly Yusuf I (1333-54) and Muhammad V (1354-9, 1362-91). "Workshop" here means all of them together.

## Essence
A wall is built in stacked registers, and each register is a different material doing one job: hard glazed tile where bodies and damp touch it, carved plaster where only the eye goes, dark wood overhead. One lattice runs under each field and never shows; colour, relief and script are then laid over it as separate, independent layers, so the same geometry can be made to say different things. Lines of writing are the joints of the system: they frame, separate and turn the corners between fields, and the last thing overhead dissolves into thousands of small prisms around one point of light.

## Key works examined
- **Comares Hall (Hall of the Ambassadors), tiled niche dado of "Nasrid bones", second third of the 14th century (Yusuf I); glazed ceramic mosaic; Palace of Comares.** One tile shape, four colours, a colour rule that halves at each step. (visually checked: Wikimedia Commons, J. L. Filpo Cabana; field rectified and colour classes counted by pixel; palette; tile lattice)
- **Comares Hall, wooden ceiling, second third of the 14th century; about 8,000 pieces (8,017 in common citation, about 125 m2) over a room 11.3 m square and 18.2 m high.** A flat drawing folded over slopes, with the star centres placed on the folds. (visually checked: Commons, two photographs; nested squares, wheel counts, palette)
- **Comares Hall, carved stucco wall band; plaster.** Interlace built from double grooved straps; vegetal fill in every cell; script above and below. (visually checked: Commons, Salon de Embajadores 005; structure, L range)
- **Facade of the Palace of Comares, Cuarto Dorado court, dated 1370 (Muhammad V); tile, stucco, muqarnas cornice, cedar eaves.** The complete vertical order of three materials on one wall. (visually checked: Flickr photographs, plus Goury and Jones's 1842 orthographic engraving on Commons, from which all ratios were measured)

- **Court of the Lions, pavilion arcade, Muhammad V; court 26.5 x 15.7 m in one source (others differ).** Text band, lozenge lattice and arch measured as one counting system. (visually checked: Flickr photographs; autocorrelation of band and lattice periods)

(visually checked: Commons photograph straight up, and Goury and Jones plan and section; radii, octagon ratio, slope, palette)

- **Hall of the Abencerrajes, muqarnas dome, Muhammad V; plaster.** Square to eight-pointed star to point. (visually checked: Commons and Flickr; star tip/valley radii, window count, palette)

## Detailed observations

**Comares Hall, "bones" dado.** One tile outline, a fat "I" with four bevelled corners, is used twice: upright and turned 90 degrees. The lying tiles are all white; the standing ones carry the colour. Standing columns alternate: every other column is black from top to bottom, and the columns between alternate teal and amber down the column, each starting on the opposite colour from its neighbour. Pixel count of the rectified field: white 52%, black and dark 25%, amber 11%, teal about 10% (plus 2% blue-teal in shadow). That is 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8 within photographic error. Grünbaum states the same halving rule for a similar Alhambra tiling and thinks the makers may well have intended it. No tile is cut except at the frame. The frame is one course of plain green bricks, a white band with black stepped merlons, then a second green course; the return at the right changes to a tiny diagonal check, about a third of the linear scale, using the same four colours. The colours are not uniform inside a class: amber runs from ochre to near orange, teal from turquoise to bottle green, black has blue-grey pieces. Regular in placement, irregular in firing. There is no outline colour: white tile touches black tile with a hairline joint.

**Comares Hall, ceiling.** Seen straight up: a dark wooden frame (about 10% of the width on each side) with a small muqarnas cornice, then three sloping skirts and one flat centre, projecting as nested squares of 1 : 0.75 : 0.47 : 0.16. In the centre is one small octagonal cupola about 0.08 of the ceiling's width, the only place with concentrated red-brown painting. Sixteen-ray wheels are the nodes. They sit on the fold lines, half on one plane and half on the next: 4 per side on the outer edge, 4 on the second, 2 on the third, and 1 whole wheel per side on the innermost slope, so the count goes 4, 4, 2, 1 toward the centre (about 44 wheels in all, counting the halves). Between them are eight-petal rosettes and small eight-point stars. The hips of the pyramid are drawn as plain ladder-like bands, the only straight lines that run uninterrupted. The joinery is brown throughout (L 18-49, C 18-38 in warm light) with inlaid pale cells (L 66-76) at about 5-8% of the area; these points of light are evenly scattered, so the dark field looks like a sky before any symbol is read into it. Nuere says the Comares ceilings all rest on a one-to-one relation between an 8-ray and a 16-ray wheel (the throne alcove's uses 9 and 12), and that the geometry made the folded surface easy to control. He adds that only colour could divide it into the seven zones of the heavens reading. I could not see seven nested zones: the pigments are gone and what is left is one brown texture. (A popular source calls the stars twelve-sided; the visible wheels have sixteen rays.)

**Comares Hall, stucco band.** A band of 12-ray stars (two in view) between two bands of script. The straps are not flat lines: each is a triple moulding, two plain ridges with a cord-like hatching between them, and it passes over and under at every crossing. Cells hold miniature palmettes on a dark sunk ground, with a row of pearls round the larger cells. Three depths: strap, leaf, ground. L runs 24-52-74 (5th, 50th, 95th percentile), colour almost none (C 13). The upper script is cursive with stems rising about twice the height of the letter bodies, the lower is Kufic whose stems are plaited into knots. The pearled outline, the cord and the leaf are successive sizes of the same gesture, each about a third of the one before.

**Facade of Comares.** Orthographic ratios from Jones's plate (wall width W = 980 px): pavement to the top of the carved inscription 975 = 0.99 W, so the stuccoed wall is a square. The lower register, doors and their surrounds up to the top of the medallion frieze, is 480 = 0.49 W. Two identical doors (frame 202 x 318, 0.21 W by 0.32 W) flank a blank panel of nearly the same size (205 x 305): the opening and the closed panel are equals, and the wall gives no hint which door is real. Bay centres lie at 0.24, 0.50, 0.76 W, about a quarter module. The window frames are squares (228 x 230). The plate shows three single windows; the court today has double, single, double. Vertical order: dado 0.17 W, tile collars round the doors up to 0.39 W, stucco, muqarnas cornice (0.11 W), one line of writing (0.03 W), carved wood corbels, deep eaves. The wall's value collapses with distance: dado L 3-58 (55 steps), stucco L 35-50 (15 steps), eaves L 3-29 with a median of 11. The stucco, covered edge to edge in pattern, reads as one ochre tone (C 30) between a black-white-green base and a black roof edge. The dado uses one lozenge grid in three panels; in each, colour alone draws concentric rectangles (teal, amber, white) that do not exist in the geometry. Absent: any figure, any axis line, any centred doorway.

**Court of the Myrtles.** Seven arches, 3 + 1 + 3, the middle taller and about a third wider. Long side walls are plain lime plaster (L 75) with ornament only as patches round openings. Pool about 34 x 7.1 m, flat and still, doubles the arcade; myrtle hedges cut as two solid boxes run beside it. Ornamented surfaces are perhaps a fifth of what is seen; plain wall, sky, water and hedge the rest. Above eye level the arcade's spandrels are pierced lozenge, below a low tile dado.

**Court of the Lions, pavilion.** Three arches, widths about 1 : 2.2 : 1. Above them is a field of lozenge lattice, and above that one band of the dynastic motto. The two repeat on the same width by different counts: motto period 117 px, lozenge period 70 px, so 6 mottoes = 10 lozenges (702 and 700 px). The lattice has three rows and is cut by the arches where a lozenge crown stands on each arch tip, so the arch grows out of the pattern rather than interrupting it. The arch edges are tiers of small niches; tiny engaged colonnettes rise on the piers, so the piers are drawn as if they carry the plaster. Each impost block, the cube between capital and pier, carries its own two-line inscription. The lozenge is perforated in places, and light passes through. Two blocks of the same pattern in the same frame look different at a distance because light reaches only the front one: the near field has L 12-87, the far one is a mid-brown. Script bands are nested: thick one (about 46 px), thin frame (about 8 px), thick one.

**Lozenge dado and poem band.** From top to bottom: plain plaster; a row of stepped trapezoid merlons carved in plaster, 11 in view; a thin fillet of motto, then the poem band about three times as tall; another fillet of motto; green tile course; black stepped merlons on white; green course; lozenges. Poem band: one cartouche with scalloped ends, letters about twice the size of the motto's. The vertical stems are evenly grouped into pairs and triplets like a rhythm over a filler of scrolling leaves; the letters are light (L 89), the scroll a middle tone, the ground darker. In the tile, every lozenge is the same size but the colour groups them in small diamonds: 2 x 2 clusters of amber, then black, then green, changing every two or three cells. Small eight-point stars, the only change of shape, sit at every crossing, in five rotating colours. White 36%, black 34%, amber 15%, green 12%, blue 2%. Merlons are black-grey with at least six shades from wear and firing.

**Hall of the Two Sisters, dome.** Plan from the plate: a regular octagon (side to width 0.418, true value 0.414) whose inside is filled by square, triangle and lozenge prisms. Rings at measured radii: a centre medallion (about 0.10 of the apothem), 8 small pockets at 0.52, 16 eight-pointed wells at 0.86. In section it is a cone rather than a hemisphere: muqarnas rise 585 px on a width of about 790 (0.74), side slope about 58 degrees. Tiers step in by the same small amount at every level, so the outline is a staircase of some 16 to 18 steps. The whole rests on little colonnettes between 16 windows, two to a side. In the photograph the sixteen wells line up one over each window, and 8 further small cusps ring the centre. Palette: L 38-71, chroma 15-24, a narrow warm-sand range; remains of green, blue and brown-red in the cell backs at about 4% and only close to the centre. The density rises inward: cells become smaller and darker toward the top. The lower wall steps in three moves: square, then octagon through corner squinches that are themselves muqarnas, then the drum of windows. Light comes from a ring of windows at the base of the cone, so the top is the dimmest place and the eye has to climb. Patronato's page calls the plaster a tribute to Pythagoras' theorem; that is a guide-book claim.

**Hall of the Two Sisters, upper wall and dado.** In Jones's elevation of one bay, 846 px wide, the bands above repeat 6, 4 and 2 times: motto 6 (period 141), shields in scalloped rosettes 4 (212), long Kufic cartouches with round cursive stops 2 (424). So three rhythms close together on one width. The stepped muqarnas arch over the window climbs about 45 degrees in roughly eight steps. The dado is different from most: coloured ribbons over white, under, over, each keeping its colour as far as it can be followed, and crossing mostly ribbons of other colours; white 38% (the ground between), blue 13%, amber 17%, green 8%, dark 23%. Here the straps are the figure; in the Mexuar the straps are white and the cells are the figure. Same method, figure and ground swapped.

**Hall of the Abencerrajes.** The drum is an eight-pointed star rather than an octagon. Mean distance from centre to tip 486 px, to inner corner 368 px (after averaging a tilted photograph): ratio 0.76. Two squares turned 45 degrees give cos 45 / cos 22.5 = 0.765. So the drum is literally the two-square star that the tiles use at small size. Sixteen windows, two in each point, one on each flank. Colour is now grey-cream (L 46-84, C 7) with blue in the pockets of the squinches. The star points carry the same muqarnas as the cone, the squinches continue the fall, and the transition from square to star is done by making the corner niches deeper.

**Mirador de Lindaraja, frontal.** Photograph 1659 units wide. Dado to 1370 of 1880 down from the crown to the floor, so it covers the lowest 27%. Two arched openings, each 400 wide, with one marble column, under one sill at 1715. Above: one horizontal inscription band 60 high, which turns down both sides as bands 65 wide, with a square knot of pattern at each corner so the writing never bends. The lunette holds one big Kufic base line whose stems become a small architecture (arches, roundels, an eight-pointed star with cursive writing) above it, on a dark ground of small scrolls. Above that the muqarnas ring, a smooth outer arch, a scalloped edge, a field of palmettes. Outermost: two vertical bands of large cursive, 95 wide. Band widths run 95 : 65 : 60. Dark-to-light layering: view (L 28-72, green, the only saturated area at C 28), frame (L 17-50), the palest and deepest cut plaster (L 17-78 on the jamb) at the lip. The view is the lit thing; everything else is a middle grey-brown (C 5-10). Low: a strip of eight-point-star tiles under the sill keeps the pattern running where the eye is at floor level. The Rijksmuseum's 19th-century photograph shows the same stacking, with bare plaster where the dado beside the arch is lost; it predates modern restoration but is not proof of the 14th-century state.

**Mirador de Lindaraja, jamb.** The three registers in one picture. Tile to height h, then stucco of about the same height (dado about 1035, plaster about 1025 in the same units). The boundary is always the same five-part strip: plaster edge, green fillet, black stepped merlons on white (11 across, period about 69), green fillet, then a black-on-white inscription in tile with knot and cartouche ends, then the field. In the plaster above, one blind window with its own columns and lobed arch, framed by a band of writing on all four sides, which is cut at the corners by squares of pattern. The dado field is a lattice of tiny eight-point stars and petals, on white straps; the geometry is perfectly uniform. Colour makes the composition: at least four kinds of rosette (black star with blue petals, green star with black petals, blue star with black and violet petals, black star with a blue ring of five-point stars) are placed in mirror symmetry about the vertical axis, with amber as the constant filler. Remove colour and only one pattern is left. Adjacent strip of large many-petalled stars at about three to four times that scale (by eye), then a narrower strip of four-pointed stars and crosses at a middle scale: three scales of tile on three faces of one corner.

**Mexuar wheel.** Sixteen black petals at 22.5 degrees round a star; petals about 365 x 120 px (3 : 1), straps about 45-50 px (so one strap is 0.4 of a petal's width), petals starting 1.8 star radii from the centre and ending 4.3. Amber stars fill the notches between petal tips, small triangles of amber the notches inside, then blue and green cells, then eight-point blue stars at the corners: 16 and 8 again. White strapwork 53%, black 27%, amber 10%, blue 8%, green 1-2% at this crop. Each strap is several pieces laid end to end, and the joint falls where one strap passes under another, so the overlap is not painted but cut. Colour is graded by the hand: black has a violet cast, greens run from L 20 to 45. The centre carries a painted emblem of Charles V's columns in a 16-point star of the same geometry; the Mudejar/Morisco workshop simply used a slot the pattern already offered.

**Wheel panel, Museo de la Alhambra.** Same grammar, partly rebuilt. Sixteen-ray wheels are cut at the corners so only a quarter shows, and the panel's centre is an eight-point star: the crop is at the symmetry lines of the pattern, which lets four panels join. Where pieces are gone the plaster bed is left white and the scribed outlines show as faint thin lines. White 55%, black 34%, amber 9%, teal and blue 3%. Black "kidney" strips link the wheel petals into bigger loops, bent bars among small straight-edged cells.

**Partal.** Arcade to the pool (three arches visible in the photograph, two small and one larger; the full count was not seen), a square tower at the side. The long brick wall has no ornament; the only pattern is the pierced lozenge above the arches and the line of three slim windows in the tower. The pool at their feet doubles the arcade.

**Goury and Jones plates.** Their chromolithographs colour the plaster ground red or blue and leave the raised leaf gold or pale; neighbouring panels of the same drawing swap red for blue. Colour is per-ground, per-panel, so two identical reliefs read as two. Bush notes that a change of colour on two identical tile dados in the Court of the Myrtles makes the eye see two compositions. Jones's own rule, red in the depths, blue in the shade, gold on the lit face, is supported by later conservation reports cited by Bush. Colours in the plates are conjecture made from surviving fragments.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Three materials, one per height** (Comares facade, Lindaraja jamb, Lions, dado of the lozenge photo). Tile at the bottom (facade dado 0.17 W; 27% of the visible wall at Lindaraja; half of the jamb's height), plaster in the middle, wood on top. Nuere gives the reason: damp and rubbing at the base would ruin plaster, so the base is glazed. Each material keeps a different contrast range (55, 15 and 26 L steps on the facade) so the wall has a strong base, a quiet middle and a dark top.
- **One lattice, many readings** (bones dado, lozenge dado, facade dado, Lindaraja jamb). The geometry is uniform; the eye's larger figures (concentric rectangles, diamonds of 2 x 2, mirrored rosettes) exist only as colour assignments on it.
- **Colour by halves** (bones dado, plus the second tiling Grünbaum illustrates). White 1/2, black 1/4, the remaining 1/4 shared equally by the hues. Measured 52 / 25 / 11 / 10. Other dados keep the order but loosen the shares: white 36-55%, black 25-34%, warm 9-17%, green and blue 3-21% (21% only in the Two Sisters laces).
- **Figure and ground are interchangeable** (Mexuar: white straps, coloured cells. Two Sisters: coloured straps, white ground. Bones: one shape, turned). The straps and the cells are two ways of reading the same cuts.
- **Text as structural line, not as label** (facade, Lindaraja, Lions, stucco band). Bands sit on joins and frames, always straight, always 3-9% of the wall dimension, and corners are closed with a square of pattern so letters never bend. Text runs around openings as the frame; the biggest and most legible band is outermost or highest (95 against 65 and 60). Castilla: the most frequent inscription is the dynastic motto, followed by short invocations, then votive words; Qur'an and poetry together were under 10% in the Comares palace corpus (3,116 inscriptions recorded there). Poems are the rare, long, legible line; the motto is the texture.
- **Two scripts with two jobs.** Cursive (naskhi) with tall stems for reading; Kufic with plaited stems that turn into arches, stars and knots for pattern (Comares stucco, Lindaraja lunette). In Kufic, a word's stem grows up and becomes part of the geometry over it.
- **Counts that agree** (Lions, Two Sisters elevation). Bands with different repeats close on the same width: 6 and 10; 6, 4 and 2. Each rhythm is whole at the edges. No band is cropped mid-repeat in the fields I measured, as Jones observed: patterns "fit the places they occupy".
- **Symmetric regular star pairs** (Comares ceiling, Mexuar, museum panel: 16 with 8; Abencerrajes drum: 8 as two squares; throne alcove ceiling: 9 with 12 in Nuere). A large wheel needs its partner, and one partner fixes the other.
- **Fold where the drawing allows it** (Comares ceiling, corner strips in the tile). Star centres are put on the fold lines, half on each plane, so a break in the surface is a place of emphasis instead of a fault; counts shrink 4, 4, 2, 1 toward the centre.
- **Descent of scale toward the centre or the eye** (Lindaraja jamb: three tile scales; dome rings at 0.10 / 0.52 / 0.86 of the apothem; stucco: strap, leaf, pearls). Every field has its own smaller field inside it, in steps of roughly a third to a fifth.
- **Change of shape is achieved by many small equal steps** (Two Sisters: 16-18 tiers at 58 degrees; Two Sisters upper arch: about 8 steps at 45; Abencerrajes: square to star). No single bracket; every step is the same small size and the sum is the curve.
- **Relief carries value, colour carries place** (stucco L 24-74 with C 13; tile C up to 38). The plaster is nearly monochrome and gets its range from light; hue is concentrated in the tile, in the cell backs of the muqarnas, and in the view through the window.
- **Plain rest as the third partner** (Myrtles walls, pool, hedges; Partal brick). Ornament is dense only within its frame and next to blank surface.
- **Open centre** (domes, ceiling, pool). The geometrical centre is marked by the least matter: a small medallion, an octagonal hole of dark wood, a still surface.
- **Regular in rule, irregular in hand.** Every tile class contains several fired shades; there is no mechanical repetition in any measured panel. Grünbaum notes that in many dados the colours have no order and may break symmetry on purpose.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence. The order of the steps is read back from the finished surfaces and is a reconstruction, as is the working lattice (inferred from uniform geometry and from scribed lines on the museum panel); the √2 rectangle system (Fernández-Puertas) and the symmetry-group counts are scholars' claims the measurements did not settle, and no step depends on them.*

**The Nasrid workshops at the Alhambra.** *Stack the view in three registers by who touches them, each with its own contrast range; run one hidden uniform lattice under each dense field, fitted so nothing is cut, and make every group by colour assignment in halving shares; put the words on the joints as straight bands; close every count on the shared width; keep the centre and most of the view plain.*
Process:
1. Stack the view in three registers by use, before any content: a base where the hand works (the lowest 17–27% of a far view, up to half of a close one), a middle that is only looked at, a thin cap (about a tenth). Give each its own contrast range: base the widest (about 55 L steps), middle the narrowest (about 15 at rest), cap pinned to one value extreme (about 26 steps, its median near the end of the scale). From far the view must read as three tones and nothing else.
2. Reserve the rest before the fill. Dense fields take about a fifth of the view (by eye in the sources), each inside its own frame and beside plain surface. The geometric centre gets the least matter: a mark of 0.08–0.10 of the width, an opening, or nothing.
3. Under each dense field lay one working lattice that will not ship as lines: one cell outline (a second only as the first turned 90°), one pitch, hairline joints, no gutter, no outline colour. Fit it to the field, not the field to it: the field's edges fall on the lattice's own symmetry lines (a whole unit centred, quarters at the corners), so no unit is cut inside the field and two fields can join edge to edge.
4. Close the counts. Every band that shares a width completes whole repeats on it, at periods in simple ratios and never one uniform beat (measured: 6 against 10; 6, 4 and 2). Where an opening interrupts a field, cut on a unit's axis so that a whole unit stands on the opening's crown.
5. Make every group by assignment on the lattice, never by changing a cell's size or shape. Rank the states by frequency and share them by halves: a calm neutral ground 1/2, the opposite neutral 1/4, the last 1/4 split equally between two hues (measured 52 / 25 / 11 / 10; loosened elsewhere to 36–55, 25–34 and 3–21 per hue). Assign by a rule of position (every other column, clusters of 2 × 2, nested rectangles, mirror pairs about the vertical axis). A different assignment on the same lattice is a different view. Colour fills and never outlines; lines and cells may swap roles (neutral lines with coloured cells, or coloured lines on a neutral ground), one mode per field.
6. Put the words on the joints. Every boundary between registers or fields is a straight band carrying text, 3–9% of the field's dimension, flanked by fillets about a third of its height; a field is closed by at least two bands of different width before the next begins. Text frames an opening on its sides and top; corners are closed with a square block of pattern, so a line never bends or turns. The outermost or highest band is the largest (95 : 65 : 60). Two kinds of string, two jobs: a short recurring one read at a glance and repeated as the fillet, and a long rare one in the tall band, read once by moving along it.
7. Nest three scales, each a third to a fifth of the one before (unit, its inner figure, its grain), and tie them to distance: far gives the three tones, middle distance the bands and words, near the grain. Inside a round field place rings at about 0.10, 0.52 and 0.86 of the radius, their counts doubling outward (1, 8, 16).
8. Where the surface breaks plane (a fold, a split, a breakpoint), put unit centres on the break, half on each side, and let the count per side fall toward the centre (4, 4, 2, 1). Change one outline into another by many equal small steps (8 at 45°, 16–18 at about 58°), never by one bracket or one curve.
9. Let depth carry value and place carry hue. The middle register is nearly one hue (chroma about a third of the base's strongest) in three depths (line, fill, sunk ground; about 24, 52 and 74 L when lit). Strong chroma lives in the base and in the one thing seen through an opening (about 4% of its frame, the only saturated area there).
10. Keep the rule exact and the instance loose: inside one colour class allow several nearby shades (six were counted in one), placed without order; never let a shade cross into another class.
Why it works:
- Grouping by likeness: when every cell has one size and one pitch the field fuses into a texture, so a colour assignment is the only thing that varies and the groups read without a box. Vary cell size as well and two signals compete; outline the groups and the lattice becomes a table.
- Figure and ground by share: a ground at half, its opposite at a quarter and two hues at an eighth each give four ranks the eye can separate, and the hues, being equal, do not compete with each other. Equalise all four and no class is the ground; enlarge a hue and it becomes the ground, with the neutrals read as marks on it.
- Fusion of fine detail into tone: a middle register covered edge to edge in low-contrast detail collapses to a single tone at distance, which is what lets the wide-range base and the cap read as three tones. Each distance then adds a layer. Remove the plain surface beside it and the fused tone has nothing to be measured against.
- A boundary has to exist anyway. Giving it the words spends no area on captions and keeps every line straight and in one direction; text floating inside a field is a second figure fighting the pattern.
- A unit cut mid-repeat reads as an accident, or as a field that continues behind the frame; whole repeats read as made for the place. Two periods that close together make a larger beat without any larger object.
- Salience by absence: lines and counts converge on the centre, so it is found without mass. Fill it and the field becomes a backdrop to a hero.
- Equal small steps: no single step is salient, so the sum reads as one continuous change, and each step can still hold a unit.
Colour relations: four classes in a dense field, ranked by share: a calm neutral ground (1/2, the largest and lowest in chroma), the opposite neutral (1/4), two hues of equal value and equal share (1/8 each), a third hue only as a trace (about 2%). Base register: full value range and the strongest chroma. Middle register: one hue, chroma about a third of the base's, value from three depths. Cap: one value extreme, low chroma. Colour never outlines and never grades; it fills and it groups. The only saturated thing outside the base is what an opening shows.
Space and line: three registers (17–50% / middle / about 10%); dense fields about a fifth of the view; text bands 3–9% of the field with fillets a third of their height; band widths stepping outward 95 : 65 : 60; bays at quarters of a square whole; a closed panel equal in size to the opening beside it; lines of one width, straight, mitred, passing over and under (line about 0.4 of the cell's width); three scales at 1/3–1/5 steps; shape changes in 8–18 equal steps; centre mark 0.08–0.10.
Type requirement: text lives in bands, so the band sets the size: letters fill the band with stems of one height, evenly grouped, about twice the height of the letter bodies, giving a regular vertical beat; set lighter than a darker band ground, or the reverse, never on the lattice. Two sizes only, the long line about twice the recurring string, their bands about 3 : 1. Rounded forms for the line that is read; a straight monoline cut for the short recurring string. Every word stays legible and in reading order; no line on a curve, round a corner or floating in a field; no sacred or devotional text as texture.
Motion and interaction: regrouping is recolouring in place: cells cross-fade to their new class (200–300ms) and nothing moves. Approaching (zoom, focus, a larger breakpoint) adds one scale at a time; stepping back drops the grain first and the hues last. The middle register opens its value range on focus (about 15 to about 50 L steps) and collapses at rest. One outline becomes another in 8 equal steps, linear, no overshoot. A long line is traversed by the user's own scroll along its band, never by a marquee. Reduced motion: final states at once.
Fails when: one object must own the centre; items of unrelated sizes cannot share one lattice; the width is fluid and the counts cannot be snapped to whole repeats; the main content is long running text (bands hold lines, not paragraphs); more than two or three states need a hue, or a state is told by hue alone with no label or second cue; the view is too short for three registers to keep their contrast ranges apart.
Signature (do not carry together): cut-tile strapwork of star wheels in white, black, amber and green · carved plaster bands of script round every opening, joined to the tile by a strip of stepped merlons · honeycomb prism vaults stepping a square room to a point (sacred or devotional text is never decoration).

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

## Space & composition
- **Registers by material.** Tile at the base, plaster in the middle, wood at the top; the joint between the first two is a five-part fillet (green, black stepped merlons on white, green, inscription) that appears in every palace seen.
- **Square and quarter modules.** The Comares wall is a square (0.99); its bays fall at quarters (0.24, 0.50, 0.76); its windows are squares. The door frame is 1.57 high to wide, the leaf 1.66, the blank panel 1.49. Total wall with eaves 1.16. Fernández-Puertas proposes a system in which each rectangle's side is the previous diagonal (square side 1, then √2, √3, 2), worked with two set squares (one with hypotenuse √2, one √4) and a compass; Bush and the Bridges paper repeat it, and both the Court of the Lions (a 30-60-90 triangle by its diagonal, 26.5 x 15.7 m, 30.6 degrees measured) and facade elements are claimed as examples. My measurements neither confirm nor exclude √2 for the facade: 1.49 and 1.57 are near but not equal to √2 (1.41) or 1.5. The irrational numbers were worked with approximations such as 7/5 and 17/10, according to the Bridges paper.
- **Symmetry groups: disputed counts.** Müller 1944 is reported as 11 groups (Bodner) and as 12 (Grünbaum, who adds one more himself). Grünbaum, Grünbaum and Shephard 1986: 13 at most, with p2, pg, pgg and p3m1 missing. Pérez-Gómez 1987 and Montesinos claim all four exist, so 17. Grünbaum 2006 disputes this: results depend on whether colour, interlace and reflections count, some photographs are too small or too damaged, and two mosaics recoloured could supply all 17. Bodner 2013 finds 14 with her own criteria, missing p2, pmg and p3m1 in public rooms. Bush and others still cite 17. Grünbaum: "Groups of symmetry had no relevance to artists and artisans" who made these walls. Treat the number as unresolved.
- **Centre empty, edge rich.** The lines converge on domes and ceiling; the floor and the court are open.
- **Thresholds doubled.** Openings are seen through openings (Lions: three layers of arches); each nested arch has its own frame, and the view is the last element.
- **Frame, field, frame.** Fields are always bounded by at least two fillets of different width before the next field starts.

## Form, line & typography
- **Line.** Straight straps with mitred turns, equal width, over and under; curves only in leaves and in the profile of the arch. Vegetal fill is a spiral stem with half-palmettes; at the Lions, leaves are cut to fill every lozenge.
- **Shapes.** Square turned 45 degrees (eight-point star), 16-ray wheel with long petals (3 : 1), lozenge, octagon. Very few shapes; scale and colour make the variety.
- **Two lettering systems.** Cursive: rounded lower line, stems all the same height and evenly grouped, so the band has a regular vertical beat; letters about twice the body height where they stand alone, and light on dark. Kufic: straight, plaited, with stems that grow into arches and knots; often illegible to a modern reader and meant as pattern too. Round cartouches and square corner knots are the punctuation.
- **Readable hierarchy.** Widths of bands step (95 : 65 : 60), with poem bands about three times as tall as the motto fillets above and below.
- **Edges.** Stepped merlons as a signature edge, in tile (black on white) and in plaster, each stepped 4-5 times.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- **Viewing distance (inferred from far and near photographs, distances approximate).** From about 10 m the wall is three tones (dado, plaster, eaves); from 2 m the relief and the writing appear; from 0.5 m the pearls, cords and firing shades. Each distance adds a layer, none is wasted.
- **Counting rhythm.** 6 mottoes to 10 lozenges; 6 : 4 : 2 in the Two Sisters bay; 4, 4, 2, 1 wheels in the ceiling; 8 and 16 in the dome rings. The numbers are related by simple ratios, never a single uniform beat.
- **Ascent.** The eye goes up the registers and then in: from a line of text to a ring of windows to the point. Light arrives from the ring at the base of the cone.
- **Daily change.** Plaster range collapses or opens with raking light (24-74 close up; 15 steps on the facade in shade). Muqarnas shift from white to dark by the angle.
- **Water.** Still pools double the arcade (Partal, Myrtles), moving water belongs to the low fountain.
- **Reading time.** A long poem runs along the walls of a room (one is about 23 m long by a press account) and can only be read by walking round it; the motto is read in an instant and repeated all over.

## UI translations
1. **Three registers by touch.** Put what the hand works in the lowest 17–27% of the view (up to half on a close view) with the widest contrast range, what is only read in a low-contrast middle, and one thin dark cap on top; the view reads as three tones from across the room.
2. **One lattice, groups by colour.** Keep every cell of a dense field the same size and pitch and show groups, clusters and nested regions by colour assignment alone; regrouping recolours in place.
3. **Shares by halves.** Map the commonest state to a calm neutral at about half the cells, the next to the opposite neutral at a quarter, and the two rarest to two hues at an eighth each.
4. **Text on the joint.** Replace divider rules and floating captions with a straight text band 3–9% of the field's height, between two thinner fillets a third as tall that repeat a short string.
5. **Square the corners.** Where a text band meets another at a corner, close the corner with a square block of pattern or a glyph; never bend, rotate or curve the line.
6. **Counts that close.** Give stacked bands different periods that all finish on the same width (6 and 10; 6, 4 and 2) and snap the width to whole repeats at each breakpoint.
7. **Crop on the symmetry line.** End a patterned field on the axis of its unit, with quarters at the corners, so adjacent panels join and nothing is cut inside the field.
8. **Three scales, three distances.** Give each field a unit, an inner figure and a grain at 1/3–1/5 steps; drop the grain first when the view shrinks.
9. **Empty centre, plain partner.** Leave the centre of a converging layout as an opening or a mark under a tenth of the width, and keep dense fields to about a fifth of the view beside plain surface.
10. **Step, don't bracket.** Take a square container to a round or pointed focus in 8–18 equal small steps, in layout and in motion; put unit centres on any fold or split, half on each side.
11. **Equal closed panel.** Beside an opening, a closed panel takes the same size and frame, so the row reads as one rhythm.
12. **Hue through the opening.** In a near-monochrome frame, the one saturated area is the live content seen through it, about 4% of the frame.

## What this artist would reject
- Centred hero objects and any figurative focal point: the centre is always the least dense thing, or an opening.
- A pattern cropped mid-repeat to fit a box, or two bands whose counts do not close on the same width.
- Text as a caption floating in a field, and letters that bend round a corner or sit in a curve.
- Decorating without a frame, and framing without a change of material.
- Colour as outline or gradient; a surface whose hue carries no grouping.
- Heavy relief or bright glaze at shoulder height where hands reach; delicate plaster near the floor.
- Filling the court: an ornate wall with no plain wall or water beside it.
- One motif at one scale.

## Signature prompt seed
Stack the view in three registers by use: a wide-contrast base where the hand works, a quiet low-contrast middle that is only read, a thin cap at one value extreme. Under each dense field keep one uniform lattice that never shows as lines, fitted so its edges fall on whole units, and make every group by colour assignment alone: a neutral ground at half, its opposite at a quarter, two hues at an eighth each. Put the words on the joints as straight bands between thinner fillets, with square blocks at the corners. Give stacked bands different periods that all close on the same width. Nest three scales a third to a fifth apart. Leave the centre nearly empty and four fifths of the view plain. Change shape by many equal small steps.
