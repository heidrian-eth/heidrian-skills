# Juan Gris — Cubism

## Essence
Gris lays out a flat architecture first: a handful of plane directions, shared by unrelated objects. Then he lets the planes turn into things. Every plane that passes over an object restates it in another mode (solid, outline, negative, pattern), so one subject is told several times without being drawn twice. The line between two planes does the work that perspective used to do.

## Detailed observations
**Portrait of Picasso.** The seated figure, a thumbed palette low right, and a background of slanted planes all share one treatment: short horizontal dabs, visible as a fine texture over the whole surface. Strong straight edges lean to horizontal (10% within 3.5°) and to one slope at 159° (10%), with a 45°/135° pair at 5–6% each, against 4% per direction for chance. The large coat flap, the background diagonals and the lapel run at about 45°. Buttons are the one repeated unit: centres at (276,772), (394,913), (512,1055), (583,1232) px, gaps of 184, 184 and 191 px (14% of the width), the first three on one 50° line. Each button is drawn differently: disc with diamond, disc with triangle, half disc on a square, so one part is seen at four attitudes. Shading always ends at an edge: along a profile over the arm the value drops from L 56 to 34 across about 110 px (8.5% of width) and then jumps to L 86 in one step. Value range is L 39–84 (5th–95th percentile) with median 66; there is no true black and little white. Chroma is 4–17 everywhere. Four dabs on the palette (blue, yellow, black, red), about 0.4% of the area, are the only saturated colours. Absent: a cast shadow, one light direction, a horizon. Contours are mostly value jumps, not drawn lines.

**Man in a Café.** Black lines 4–8 px thick make a grid that runs through figure and setting. The vertical at x = 0.40 of the width continues from top edge through shoulder and hip; the horizontal at y = 0.26 of the height runs through the red panel's lower edge, the man's cheek and the window sill, unbroken across the canvas. Other cuts fall at x = 0.23, 0.55, 0.63, 0.71, 0.81, 0.84 and y = 0.09, 0.39, 0.45, 0.53. Between 0.55 and 0.81 they are nearly equal, about 0.085 W apart, so the figure is built on roughly a twelfth of the width. The face is a patchwork: full, profile and three-quarter parts abutted along the grid lines, so that eyes, nose and moustache do not meet. Cylinders (sleeves, hat) carry the shading: a sharp light edge L 65 fades to L 13 in 35 px (2.8% W). Angles: 39% of strong edges are within 3.5° of horizontal or vertical (23% vertical, 16% horizontal). The lower third is bare, pale canvas (L about 73–78) with legs and chair as black silhouettes; the paint stops in visible vertical strokes. Flat red stripes (`#5F2D1E`) and yellow lettering (PIC / AP) are the only strong colours. Letters sit at mid-left, cut by the frame of a cell.

**Violin and Checkerboard.** A pink-brown wallpaper top with a repeating lattice motif, violins and drapery below, a blue tilted board lower right. The board is one blue quadrilateral with sides at 57° and 147°, a true right angle. Over it sit two chequer lattices. One is upright (orange/black, strip at the bottom, cell 46 px = 6.3% W); one is tilted with the board, about 30°, and drawn as thin white lines only at the right. Measured pitches agree (about 45 px in each), so one lattice appears twice, once filled and once as outline. Where they overlap, cells are cut into kites and triangles: some pieces show the board's blue, the others the upright lattice's black or orange (it reads as an exchange of the two patterns; the exact rule was not decoded). A single violet cylinder interrupts the grid. Edges round every object have a black halo, strongest at the darkest side. Largest shares: orange-brown 31%, black and near-black 16%, blue 12%, green 11%, yellow 9%, orange accent small but at C 81.

**Violin and Glass.** A dusty violet wall (38%) holds a diamond of blue (28% across its two values L 45 and L 28), tilted about 45°. The violin appears as a wood-grain body, green ribs to either side, a violet scroll far off at the lower right, and strings on the wood. Wallpaper scraps in pink and red sit near the picture's edges (red cluster 5.6%), as fragments of the room. Note that music staves are printed on the blue with no change of plane, so the same blue is table, cloth and score. Strong edges: horizontal 11%, 99° 8%, vertical 7%, 133° and 173° 6% each; no single tilt dominates.

**The Table.** Canvas 1197 × 1575 px in the scan. The oval is 1038 × 1425 px (87% of the width, 90% of the height, 62% of the area), centred within 0.5% horizontally and 0.3% vertically. Outside it, flat blue-grey (`#6F717B`) with faint charcoal corners of a rectangular table. Inside, ochre papers: faux wood, striped wallpaper, newsprint, a page from a Fantômas novel. Two contradictory tables share one view: an upright oval and a receding rectangle whose corners poke out at the top right, left and bottom right. The bright white wedge with the key at lower right and the black wedges are the only value extremes. Directions: 36° 14%, vertical 11%, horizontal 9%, 56° 9%; the perpendiculars of the slopes are weak (126° 4%, 146° 2%), so these are parallel bundles, not rotated rectangles. Charcoal shading is soft and one-sided; across the wallpaper diamond the value falls from L 78 to 41 over about 50 px before a sharp edge. Text sizes step down sharply: LE JOU about 65 px tall, "LE VRAI ET LE FAUX" about 28 px, newsprint about 12 px (roughly 5 : 2 : 1), and the last is texture.

**Breakfast.** The pasted strip runs between verticals at x = 0.11 and 0.80 of the width (the right margin is twice the left); another vertical at 0.46 splits it, and its bottom edge lies at y = 0.82. Cups, saucers, coffee pot and spoon are drawn in charcoal and white directly on the papers; they alone are modelled as solids, while the papers stay flat. The same wood paper serves as table top, front and drawer, and as the turned legs, which stand detached on the blue at the bottom. A jug's spout pokes out of the strip at left, so the object continues across two grounds. The fragment "OURN / AZA / GRIS" sits at lower left, about 2.5% of the area, the only words. Blue (`#4B8593`, 21%) is the room; pale cream (`#F6EFC5`, 17%) is light. A complementary split: warm paper about 60%, blue about 20%, black 7%. Edges are scissors-straight; no curve is cut except in the drawn objects.

**The Glass of Beer.** The oval touches the frame: 97% of the width and 96% of the height, about 73% of the area, on a brown mottled ground. Inside it the paper's main edges lie at y = 0.25 and y = 0.75 and a vertical cut at x = 0.355. The glass is modelled as a solid (foam cloud, shaded body, white highlights) while the marbled paper behind it is cut to its own quadrilateral and ignores the glass. A small wine glass at right is half solid, half white line, with charcoal ghosts of it and other glasses shifted into the neighbouring planes. A black crescent at left (point L 9) balances the white plane at right (L 76). The Greek-key border (red on green) turns a square corner with no perspective, and is faintly continued outside its paper. Directions: horizontal 8%, vertical 8%, 158° 9%.

**The Sunblind.** Almost the whole picture is turned together: 60% of strong edges lie within 3.5° of 9° and another 9% of 99°. Both blinds use the same slat pitch, 51–53 px (0.043 of the height), in runs of 6 on the left and 9 on the right, though they are separate papers a third of the width apart. The glass is one flat teal shape (`#517B79`) for bowl, stem and foot; its left side is completed only by white chalk on black. The newspaper (LE SOCIALISTE) is the one strip at a steep angle, about 55°, and its masthead runs on past a break where the glass edge crosses it. Blue dabs (`#4896AE`, L 58) lie in three patches over table and ground, fading by density, the only soft texture. White chalk lines trace reflections.

**The Musician's Table.** A blue-grey ground (about 42%) is also the violin's body, the neck and the table's legs: figure and ground share one paper and only charcoal shading tells them apart. A black quadrilateral lies across it, its bottom apex at (0.49 W, 1.0 H); its long edges run at 49° and 139°, a true right angle (7% each, against 41% horizontal from wood grain and staves). The wood sheet starts at y = 0.333 of the height and stops on a clean horizontal edge. The bottle stands on that edge. Newspaper (top right, Gothic "Le M"), blank staves and the wood all read as real materials. The glass appears twice as a round top on a short body (top left and centre); a third round, charcoal only on the wood, repeats the circle of the bottle's label area at the same size (150–160 px, 12% W).

**Still Life with Checked Tablecloth.** One gingham lattice runs under the whole table, sides at 43° and 133°, square side about 70 px (5.5% W), in three tones (light, mid, dark). Its direction and pitch hold while its palette flips along a plane edge: grey-white/green/dark green above, cream/orange/maroon below, green again at the lower right. The overhanging cloth repeats it in green and grey with a different skew and dotted texture. The central axis is a compotier with a pale bowl, flanked by two sets of bottles. Round ends repeat: three ring "eyes" (diameter about 125, 137 and 150 px, i.e. 10–12% W) and four black half-discs with a white arc above them. Arcs and discs are placed on either side of the axis x = 0.51: the composition reads as a bull's head, and the "eau" label ends the word taureau (Met reading). Scratched, dotted and stippled surfaces differ by square; the Met conservation essay says the checker was drawn in pencil and each square painted, then scored in the wet paint, with no ground. White lines draw a guitar outline on a dark plane at left, so the instrument is drawn in line where the plane is dark and in shape where it is light. Directions: horizontal 12%, 112° 9%, vertical 6%, 69° 6%, 43° and 133° 5% each.

**Fantômas.** Two main right-angled frames, 16° and 106° (9% + 13%) and 24° and 114° (10% + 7%), and two slopes the other way, 151° (12%) and 163° (8%): six directions hold about 59% of the strong edges, against 24% by chance. The table plane is wood grain (34% of the area with mouldings, L 37–48). Across it the title LE JOURNAL climbs to the right, about 41% of the width, along an underline at 23.5°. It is white on wood for LE JO, green on grey-white for the U, black on yellow for part of the R, and green or teal on white for N, A, L: three changes of ink. The letters keep their size and path; only ink and ground change, and some letters split in mid-stroke. The pipe is black, solid and shaded in green; its stem restarts in teal on white. Outside the planes, bottle, glass and fruit continue as thin white lines (a continuous outline on the wood and a chalk one on black). A yellow disc, 95 px (7.4% W), sits inside a yellow-and-black rhythm: wedge, disc, stripes, at (0.40, 0.18). Black halos (L 5) lie on one side of most planes: profile across the maroon panel runs L 5 → 11 → 16 → 19 over about 50 px (4% W), hard at one edge, soft at the other. Absent: perspective, a light source, any object shown whole.

**Place Ravignan.** A dark surround (L 10, 27%) is cut open by two lit panels: a blue window panel, a tilted quadrilateral at 8° from vertical holding 17% of the area, and a table panel at 28°. Frames: 28° and 118° (9% + 14%), 8° and 98° (8% + 15%), plus one slope at 73° (8%): two rotated rectangles account for almost half. The trees and shutters of the square continue outside the window panel as dark blue-green outlines: inside they are solid light blue (L 55), outside they are scratchy lines on L 10, a step of 45 L at the same line. The word MEDOC, painted on a bottle, changes from dark blue-black letters to pink ones where the pane crosses; LE JOURNAL changes at least twice (dark on pale green, pink on dark, dark on pale). One red disc 8% W inside a cup outline at (0.49, 0.48). Blue runs at C 37–41 and the red at C 48, spread over one panel and a few small pieces.

**Chessboard, Glass, and Dish (1917).** All texture is gone. A dark ovoid (L 9–22, about 24%) holds cut-out planes. Vertical cuts fall at x = 0.285, 0.389, 0.613, 0.707 of the width: two pairs that mirror about the centre to within 1%. Horizontals fall at y = 0.12, 0.25, 0.37, 0.49. The chessboard is a green diamond at 47°, 11 dark ovals standing for the dark squares, and the board's edge is a thin white line at the lower left. Objects (glass, dish, pot) are white, grey and black silhouettes split across those verticals: a shape is white on one side and grey on the other. The board's ridge is a hard dark line and a 20 px feather. Frames: 13° and 103° (16% + 11%), 0° and 90° (10% + 8%), 47° and 137° (6% + 6%): three right-angled frames hold 57%. Colours are few and flat: black, rust, gold, white 3.5%, grey, olive, green.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **A few shared angles, not many** (strong in five or six works). Strong straight edges within 3.5° of the two best frames: Sunblind 69% (one frame), Place Ravignan 46%, Chessboard 1917 45%, Fantômas 39%; chance is 16%. In these works, and in the board of Violin and Checkerboard and the black square of The Musician's Table, both sides of the right angle are present, so they are rotated rectangles, shared by unrelated objects and tilted 8–30° or about 47°. In The Table (36°, 56°) and Portrait of Picasso (159°) the slope has no strong perpendicular: parallel bundles. Glass of Beer, Violin and Glass and Checked Tablecloth are weaker (about 25–30% for two) and do not prove the point alone.
- **The 0/90 frame is always there, first or second, except in three** (first in Man in a Café 39% and Breakfast about 22%; second in Chessboard 1917; nearly gone in Sunblind, Fantômas and Place Ravignan). The canvas edge is a frame like the others. The history runs from upright grid (1912) to all-tilted (1914–15) to upright and tilted interlocked (1917).
- **Plane boundary as the event** (Fantômas, Place Ravignan, Violin and Checkerboard, Checked Tablecloth). When a plane crosses an object the object does not move: it changes mode. Modes seen: solid to outline, positive to negative, one ink to another, texture to flat.
- **A word as the test case** (Fantômas: ink changes three times; Place Ravignan: MEDOC and LE JOURNAL; Man in a Café and Breakfast: one fragment). Where the word is whole it runs across the planes at nearly one size and only its colour answers the plane. The reader can read it, yet it is never one colour.
- **One-sided shading** (Picasso 56 → 34 → 86; Man in a Café 65 → 13 in 2.8% W; Fantômas halo 4% W; Chessboard 1917). Each edge is hard on one side and fades over about 3 to 9% of the width on the other. The shadow belongs to the edge, not to a lamp. The planes are flat apart from this.
- **Exact repeat at a fixed module, with rotated restatement** (Violin and Checkerboard 6.3% W, same pitch tilted about 30°; Picasso buttons 14% W; Sunblind slats 4.3% H in both blinds; Checked Tablecloth gingham 5.5% W in two palettes). The unit keeps its pitch across a plane edge while its state changes (filled to outline, green to orange).
- **Same material, several roles** (Musician's Table blue paper: wall, violin, legs; Breakfast wood: top, side, drawer; Violin and Glass blue: table, cloth, score). Colour holds across roles so the viewer reads relation before identity.
- **Two contradictory containers** (Table: oval 62% plus rectangle; Glass of Beer: oval 73%; Chessboard: ovoid). In The Table the corners of the rectangle poke out of the oval; in Chessboard 1917 gold planes cross the ovoid's rim, so neither container wins.
- **Lit centre, dark rim** (Place Ravignan: panel 17% at L 55 on L 10; Table: gold on blue-grey; Chessboard 1917: tan on black). The oval or panel is separated by a value step of about 22–48 L, not by a line.
- **Vertical symmetry at cut lines** (Chessboard 1917 only: 0.285/0.707 and 0.389/0.613; compare The Table's oval, centred within 0.5%, and the Musician's Table apex at 0.49 W; not found in Man in a Café or Breakfast). The underlying cuts pair around the centre while the objects over them do not.
- **Rhyme across unlike things** (Checked Tablecloth: rings 10–12% W on bottles, half-discs on cups and a compotier; Fantômas disc and wedge; Place Ravignan disc). One form is used for several objects.
- **Golden section: not established.** Camfield (1965) argued for it in Man in a Café; Fischler and Fischler (1980) show Gris's letter of 25 March 1921 saying the Section d'Or played no part in the recent works he names, and Golding's reading of a ruled drawing as verticals plus 45° diagonals. Cuts measured here (0.40, 0.63, 0.23, 0.81 in Man in a Café) sit 0.5–4% of the width off golden positions; Chessboard 1917's 0.389/0.613 pair is 0.5–0.7% off 0.382/0.618, but with some ten cuts a near miss is expected, so this proves nothing.
- **The geometric start, the free middle** (Kahnweiler, reported by Fischler: a proportion as springboard to a coloured architecture, then drawings destroyed). Fits the mix observed of ruled frames with hand-drawn lines that follow none.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Juan Gris.** *Fix two right-angled frames of direction before any content and cut the view into flat planes on them, one mode per plane; then lay the content across the plane edges, where it keeps its place, size and path and changes only its mode.*
Process:
1. Before any content, choose the frames: two (three at most), each a pair of directions at a right angle. One is the format's own level and upright; the other is turned by one angle between 8° and 30° (or about 45°) and never varied. Every plane edge takes a direction from these, so that 40–70% of all straight edges fall on two frames. One bundle of parallels without its perpendicular is allowed. With no tilt available, state the level frame as a grid of 4–7 unequal cuts per direction, closing to about a twelfth of the width where the content is densest.
2. Set the enclosure: one active field against a quiet rim, a value step of 22–48 L apart and no line between them. Either the field is centred within 1% and takes 60–75% of the area, or it is one panel of 15–20% placed off-centre. The central quarter carries 1.3–1.6 times the average edge density. If the content has two valid framings, overlay both containers and let one breach the other's rim at 2–3 points, so neither wins.
3. Cut the field into flat planes on the frames and give each plane one mode from a list of three or four (solid, outline only, inverted, patterned) and one view of the content. Colour is assigned to planes, not to things: one family at 30–70%, a second of the opposite temperature at 15–40%, the darkest value at 7–30%. One colour serves two or three unrelated roles (the ground of one thing, the body of another).
4. Place the content last, across the plane edges and not inside them: each main thing crosses at least one. At the edge it keeps its position, size and path and takes the mode of the plane it enters; nothing shifts or is redrawn. Where a plane does not carry the thing's colour, continue it as a thin line of one weight (0.15–0.3% of the width). Keep one or two things whole, in a single state: the ones that must be read exactly.
5. Run one word across 2–4 planes on one baseline at one size, about 40% of the width; only its ink changes, once per plane, and a letter may split in mid-stroke. Text has three sizes near 5 : 2 : 1, the smallest read as texture, and display text stays under 5% of the area.
6. Choose one repeated unit at a fixed pitch of 4–6% of the width, in runs of 6–9 (or 3–4 at about 14%). It keeps pitch and direction across every plane edge while its state flips (filled to outline, one palette to the other), and it is restated once on the other frame at the same pitch. Interrupt the run once, with one object. Give one simple form at 7–12% of the width to three or four unlike things on either side of the axis.
7. Shade edges, not things: where order must show, an edge is hard on one side and fades on the other over 3–9% of the width (a drop of 14–50 L). No cast shadow and no common light direction; the planes stay flat.
8. Budget the extremes: each saturated accent under 3% of the area, the most saturated the smallest (down to 0.4% in a low-chroma scheme); the lightest and darkest values meet at only a few edges.
Why it works:
- Grouping by likeness of direction: when unrelated things share four directions, the eye reads one structure before it reads the things, so a busy view holds together without boxes. Spread the angles evenly (16% on two frames by chance) and each edge is its own event.
- Good continuation against figure and ground: a thing that keeps its place, size and path across an edge is still one thing, and its change of mode proves the plane is there. Shift it at the edge and it becomes two things; leave it unchanged and the plane reads as a tint behind it.
- Reading survives a change of ink when baseline and size hold, so one word can measure every plane it crosses. Change its size or path as well and it is several labels.
- Depth from overlap, stated locally: a fade on one side of an edge says which plane is on top without a lamp, so flat colours stay comparable across the view. Give the whole view one light and the planes turn into objects with shadows.
- Salience by oddness: a unit at constant pitch makes the one interruption, and the one whole thing among split ones, the first things seen.
Colour relations: dominant family 30–70%, in two or three values; counter family of the opposite temperature 15–40%; darkest value 7–30%, used as a plane and as rule, never as a shadow under a thing; lightest value a few percent; accents under 3% each, highest in chroma and smallest in area. A thing has no colour of its own: it takes the plane's. Field and rim stand 22–48 L apart.
Space and line: two frames, the second turned 8–30° or about 45°; 4–7 cuts per direction; field 60–75% centred or 15–20% off-centre; central quarter 1.3–1.6 times denser; module 4–6% of the width; rhyme form 7–12%; two line weights (a rule at 0.3–0.6% of the width, a thin continuation line at 0.15–0.3%); one-sided fade 3–9%. In one work the cuts pair about the centre axis within 1% while the content over them stays asymmetric.
Type requirement: heavy, wide-stemmed capitals whose half-letters stay recognisable when the ink splits in mid-stroke; one size and one baseline for the crossing word; three sizes near 5 : 2 : 1; each ink chosen to hold contrast on its own plane. Running text stays on the level frame and inside one plane; only the crossing word may ride the turned frame, up to about 24°. Never crop a word that must be read, and give the full string to assistive technology once.
Motion and interaction: planes move and content stays. A plane edge sweeps along its frame (400–600ms, ease-out, no bounce) and each thing flips mode at the instant the edge passes it; the module keeps its pitch throughout. Selection brings a plane over the item; it adds no highlight to it. Several views are shown at once, side by side, never as a sequence. Targets keep their place under every mode, ≥44px. Reduced motion: cross-fade to the final arrangement.
Fails when: long text or tables must cross an edge (every line would split its ink); outline against solid already means disabled against enabled, so a change of mode is read as a change of state; colour must follow the object (status chips, series colours, brand marks); the things are too small to cross an edge (icon rows, form fields); the platform allows no second frame and no unequal grid; every item is a peer and nothing can stay whole.
Signature (do not carry together): a tipped-up table of bottle, glass and guitar in printed wood grain and newsprint · a newspaper masthead in fat capitals cut off mid-word · sharp tilted planes of saturated blue, ochre and black with chalk-white outlines.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Home energy screen, desktop 1280 × 800, pale ground with a brand violet, a rounded sans, light and friendly (the works are dark-rimmed, warm, matt and hand-cut).
1. *Frames.* Frame A is level and upright and carries all text and controls. Frame B is turned 12° (12° and 102°) and carries plane boundaries only. There is no third; every plane edge lies on A or B.
2. *Enclosure.* Light and dark are inverted: the rim is pale (L 94) and the active field is one mid-violet panel (L 58, a step of 36 L) cut on frame B, 18% of the screen, centred at (0.62, 0.40). No stroke round it. The rim holds navigation and labels only.
3. *Planes and modes.* Three planes: the rim (things appear as outline), the panel (things appear solid, light on violet), and a level peak-tariff band on frame A in the darkest value (things appear inverted). Shares: pale family 58%, violet 24%, darkest 14%, text and accent 4%. The same violet is the panel, the selected navigation bar and the dial's track: one colour, three roles.
4. *Content across edges.* The 15-day usage chart is 900px wide and placed so that the panel's left edge passes between day 6 and day 7 and the tariff band crosses the bar tops. Bars are 2px outlines on the rim, solid inside the panel and inverted under the band; baseline, width and place do not change. The bill total stays whole, in one state, on the rim, crossed by nothing.
5. *Word.* The period heading, 60px and 512px wide (40% of the width), sits level and crosses the panel's edge: dark ink on the rim, pale ink on the panel, one letter split by the 12° edge. Sizes are 60, 24 and 12px; the 12px tariff terms form a grey block. Display text is about 4% of the screen.
6. *Module and rhyme.* Bar pitch is 60px (4.7% of the width), in runs of 6 on the rim and 9 in the panel. The same 60px pitch is restated once on frame B as outline ticks along the panel's lower edge. A "today" disc, 96px, interrupts the run once. The disc form returns for the thermostat dial and the solar status (96–128px, 7.5–10% of the width).
7. *Edges.* The panel's left edge is hard; on the rim side the tone falls 16 L over 64px (5% of the width). No drop shadow anywhere.
8. *Extremes.* One accent from the brand's secondary hue fills the "today" disc, 0.6% of the screen, with its value printed beside it. The lightest and the darkest value touch only where the tariff band meets the rim.
- *Motion.* Changing the period sweeps the panel's edge along frame B over 500ms; the bars stay where they are and each flips mode as the edge passes. Reduced motion cross-fades.

Result: a pale, violet, rounded-sans energy screen with no table top, no pasted paper and no shards. What survived is two frames of direction fixed first, one mode per plane, content that holds its place and changes mode at an edge, a word that keeps its size while its ink changes, and a module that keeps its pitch.

## Space & composition
Depth is shallow: flat planes overlapped, with no vanishing point. Edge strength in the central quarter against the whole picture: Chessboard 1917 ×1.60, Checked Tablecloth ×1.59, Musician's Table ×1.58, Glass of Beer ×1.36, Fantômas ×1.35, Violin and Glass ×1.31, the rest ×1.03–1.23, Sunblind ×0.75. So the crowding is central and the rim quiet, except in the Sunblind, where the pattern is at the rim and the subject is the plainest shape. In The Table and Glass of Beer a centred oval takes 62–73% of the area, with a quiet rim; in Breakfast a strip takes 68% of the width. In 1915 the lit panel is smaller and off-centre (Place Ravignan 17%, upper centre-right). The frame is cut by planes at several points: corners go over the edge in The Table, Chessboard 1917 and Breakfast. Table tops are tipped up parallel to the picture.

## Color

Two phases. 1912: a cool grey-blue and warm grey with about 0.4% accents. 1913–15: a small number of flat, saturated hues with black between them. 1917: darker, few colours, large flat areas. Black or near-black (L < 15) covers 7–31% of ten of the thirteen works; it is about nil in Picasso, The Table (darkest L about 28) and Violin and Glass. Colour goes with planes more than with objects: an object takes a new colour when a plane crosses it. Hue families are few: in the 1914 collages one warm brown family (orange-brown plus yellow) takes 29–68% and one cool paper 16–42%; Fantômas and Violin and Checkerboard carry the widest set. ## Form, line & typography
Edges are straight and hard, cut as if by scissors. Curves occur in objects (bottle, guitar, disc, half-disc), some compass-like (discs, arcs), some freehand, against ruled planes. Line is of two kinds: black rule or grid (Man in a Café 4–8 px in 1280) and thin white chalk (Fantômas, Violin and Checkerboard, 2–4 px), which continues the object into a plane that does not carry its colour. Lettering is found type: newspaper mastheads (fat slab capitals for Le Journal and Le Socialiste, blackletter for "Le M…"), wine labels in script ("eau"), a ringed number (65). It is cut off on purpose: LE JOU, PIC / AP, OURN / GRIS, eau. Sizes in The Table step about 5 : 2 : 1. Text is small in area: about 2.5% of Breakfast (measured box), under 5% of Fantômas (estimate).

## Rhythm, motion & time
By eye, not measured: the eye goes from the lit or brightest patch (a white paper, a yellow disc), along the one diagonal axis (the newspaper title, the pipe, the table edge) to the edge. Repeats are regular (slats, buttons, checks) and then interrupted once by an object. No motion is depicted, and objects do not look as if they move: the several views are simultaneous.

## UI translations
*For strict studies and homage. A kit takes "Process (derived)" and its own surface.*

1. **Two frames, declared.** Take every panel edge from the level frame or from one frame turned 8–30°; no third angle, and the turn never varies within a product.
2. **A field and a quiet rim.** One active field 22–48 L from its rim with no border: 60–75% of the view when centred, 15–20% when it is an off-centre panel.
3. **One mode per plane.** Each plane shows what lies on it in one mode (solid, outline, inverted, patterned); three or four modes in the whole kit.
4. **Cross the edge, keep the place.** A chart, illustration or control group that spans two planes keeps position, size and path and only changes mode at the edge.
5. **A thin line where the colour stops.** Continue a thing into a plane that does not carry its colour as a line of one weight, 0.15–0.3% of the width.
6. **A heading that changes ink.** One heading crosses 2–4 planes at one size on one baseline, about 40% of the width; each plane gives it its own ink, at full contrast.
7. **Three text sizes, 5 : 2 : 1.** The smallest is a grey block of texture; display text stays under 5% of the view.
8. **A module that keeps its pitch.** One repeated unit at 4–6% of the width runs across plane edges, changes state there, is restated once on the other frame and is interrupted once.
9. **One colour, several roles.** Assign colour to planes; let the same colour be a ground in one place and a body in another, from one dominant family (30–70%), one counter family (15–40%) and the darkest value (7–30%).
10. **Edge shading in place of shadow.** A hard edge with a fade of 3–9% of the width on one side (14–50 L); no drop shadow and no common light.
11. **One form, several things.** A single simple form at 7–12% of the width stands for three or four unlike things.
12. **One thing whole.** One or two items stay in a single state, crossed by no edge: the ones that must be read exactly.
13. **Accents under 3%.** Each saturated accent under 3% of the view, the most saturated the smallest.
14. **The edge moves, the content stays.** Transitions sweep a plane edge along its frame (400–600ms, ease-out); items flip mode as it passes; reduced motion cross-fades.

## What this artist would reject
Evidence-based, from what is absent in all works checked:
- a lamp or single light direction: each edge shades itself;
- a vanishing point or a horizon line;
- a hue used for decoration across unrelated planes;
- a continuous contour around an object;
- a main object given in one single state (the newspaper and the grapes of Checked Tablecloth are the deliberate exceptions, whole and legible among split things);
- evenly distributed angles: few angles, shared.

## Signature prompt seed
Before placing anything, fix two right-angled frames of direction, the format's own and one turned by a single small angle, and take every edge from them. Set one active field a clear value step from a quiet rim, with no border. Cut it into flat planes and give each plane one mode (solid, outline, inverted, patterned) and a colour that belongs to the plane, not to what lies on it. Then lay the content across the plane edges: each thing keeps its place, size and path and changes only its mode at the edge. Run one heading across several planes at one size and let only its ink change. Repeat one unit at a constant pitch across the edges and interrupt it once. Shade one side of an edge and never cast a shadow. Keep one thing whole, and keep every saturated accent small.
