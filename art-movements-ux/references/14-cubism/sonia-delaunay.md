# Sonia Delaunay — Cubism

## Essence
Delaunay takes the Cubist faceted plane away from the object and gives it to colour: a surface is cut into patches, and each patch changes colour wherever it crosses an axis, an arc or a seam, so the eye never rests and the whole sheet is read at once. The same procedure runs, unchanged, through a quilt, a dress, a 2 m poem, a 2.5 m canvas and a printed silk. Where there are words, colour does not illustrate them or sit behind them: it occupies exactly the space the lines leave empty, and runs down beside them at their own pace.

## Detailed observations

**La Prose du Transsibérien (1913).** The sheet is ≈5.6 times as tall as it is wide. A vertical fold splits it at 49.5 % of the width, so the painting column and the text column are equal to within 1 %. The painting never crosses the fold and the poem never crosses it the other way: arcs and ovals on the left are cut dead straight at the fold (see the big black and violet oval at the top and the rainbow arcs two thirds of the way down). Folds every ≈9.4 cm give 11 rows × 2 = 22 sections, and the four sheets join about every 50 cm. At two of the joins the painting simply starts again, with no attempt to match colours. The sheets read as four compositions butted end to end, inside one continuous chain of curves.
*Head and foot.* Both columns open with a quiet area: at the left a typographic title block about 5 % of the sheet's height, at the right a one-colour railway map about 2.5 % high, and only then do colour and poem begin. At the foot, the painting ends in its one recognisable image, a red Eiffel Tower passing through an orange ring (the Wheel), in the last 6–7 % of the height; the poem's last line names the same tower and wheel, at the same level. So the route runs from a map (top right) to a destination (bottom left), and word and image meet only at the end.
*How colour meets text.* The right column is not a coloured background with text over it. Patches of wash fill the space where lines are short: to the right of left-aligned stanzas, to the left of right-aligned stanzas, in the indent between two blocks. Their edges follow the line ends as serrated teeth, one tooth per line, with a thin halo of bare paper left round the letters. Washes cover ≈24 % of the column at a strict threshold (ΔE > 18 from the paper) and ≈32 % at a loose one (ΔE > 12). Only ≈3 % of the ink lies on strong wash and ≈7 % on any wash. If colour were placed at random the figures would be ≈24 % and ≈32 %: the wash avoids the text by a factor of about 4.5 to 8.5. (Automated estimate on an aged sheet; ink on the darker washes is under-detected. The very pale peach tints below ΔE 12, which do pass under some lines, are not counted.)
*Two loudness levels.* Left column: median L 63, 10th–90th percentile L 26–89, 20 % of the area above C 50, 15 % darker than L 30. Right column: median L 90 (L 73–96), 5 % above C 50, nothing darker than L 30 except ink. Same hues, two volumes: the painting is dense, the washes are diluted.
*Alternation.* Stanzas swing between flush left and flush right (or a deep indent) about 20 times down the sheet, by eye; the wash swings to the opposite side each time, so a zigzag of colour descends the text column. Measured in 22 equal bands, wash cover goes 48, 14, 8, 4, 55, 42, 41, 37, 7, 40, 42, 17, 8, 15, 36, 22, 27, 7, 27, 43, 24, 49 %: dense and almost empty passages alternate, and the first and last bands are near the maximum. Dominant wash colour also moves: green-teal (bands 1, 9–10, 20), peach and gold (2–8, 12–14, 16–19, 21), blue-grey (11, 15, 22).
*Type.* The letterpress is set in many faces (30, as counted by Kitty Maryatt, cited by the Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco) and four inks. In the scan ≈40 % of the ink is red or orange and the rest blue-black or green-grey, changing by stanza and not by word. Size varies little (body lines at ≈4 mm pitch). Changes are in weight, width, slant and case. The refrain "Blaise, dis, sommes-nous bien loin de Montmartre ?" returns six times in six different faces (bold extended sans; condensed bold; red italic serif; larger bold red italic; small condensed; red small capitals), at 41, 43.5, 47.7, 49.8, 54.5 and 59 % of the height. The line is the same each time and never looks the same. Lines stay horizontal; there is no shaped or slanted text.
*Edges.* Pochoir shapes have three edge types side by side: a hard stencil edge, a torn or toothed edge, and a dry-brush fade. A thin sliver of bare paper is often left between neighbours instead of a drawn outline. No black contour is used anywhere; black appears as a colour area (15 % darker than L 30).
*Age.* Both originals and facsimile are on yellowed paper (paper reference L 97, C 18). The 2018 re-creation, with fresh gouache on white paper, shows how much bluer and cleaner the teals and blues were meant to be.

**Prismes électriques (1914).** A square with two main discs, centred at ≈(0.51, 0.19) and ≈(0.65, 0.49) of the width and height: both right of centre or on it, above the middle, on a diagonal. Arcs of a third system sweep in at bottom right and bottom left. Each disc is cut by a vertical and a horizontal through its centre, and those axes run on beyond it as the edges of other patches. Rings keep the same radius across the four quadrants (agreement to within about 8 % of the radius), and colour changes at each axis. Ring boundaries on the upper disc fall at about 60, 76, 107, 153, 183, 225 and 264 px of a 1440 px canvas, then ≈360 and ≈470: widths of ≈16, 31, 46, 30, 42, 39, then ≈95 and ≈110. Rings are irregular near the centre and jump wider outside.
One ring, four quadrants (radius ≈110–150 px, upper disc): pink `#CA949C` L 66, grey-blue `#7A89A8` L 56, navy `#0A2460` L 16, mauve `#9D5D78` L 47. In the next-but-one ring (≈185–225) the lower half is yellow, L 75–84, C 75–78, and the upper half dark green and brown, L 29–30: a value flip of ≈45–55 L exactly at the horizontal axis. Centres are always the palest spot (cream or pale pink, L 81–88).
Edge statistics (blurred scan, ΔE > 25 across an edge): median lightness step 19 L, quarter-to-quarter range 10–31; 39 % of edges have under 15 L of step, so they are made by hue alone. Median hue difference is 57°; 33 % of chromatic edges are over 90° apart and 16 % over 120°. So "simultaneous contrast" here is mostly middle-sized hue steps, with a minority of true complementaries. Palette: 52 % warm, 31 % cool, 16 % near-neutral; 14 % of the area above C 50; 24 % darker than L 30.
Two dark, figure-like silhouettes stand in the lower middle (x ≈ 0.5 and 0.7); they are built of the same quarter arcs and dissolve. At left, ≈0.6 % of the canvas area is a small text block (≈0.14 of the width by ≈0.04 of the height): about a dozen words hand-lettered in four rows of little coloured bricks, each brick a different colour with letters in a contrasting colour, and the poet's surname the largest, black on orange. It is smaller than any ring and sits on the same patch grid. The museum reads it as the prospectus of La Prose.
Paint is scrubbed on a coarse canvas so that the weave shows at every edge and no outline is drawn.

**Prismes électriques, study (Wellesley, 1913–14).** Two discs, one sliced by the top edge, one whole at about (0.77, 0.45), plus a left half of straight-sided panes. The same quadrant switching, but brushed wet so that patches fade from a colour to near-white inside a single pane. 16 % of the area above C 50, 52 % warm, 29 % cool, 20 % neutral.

**Le Bal Bullier (1913).** A band about 4:1. Three lamp halos (concentric rings, pale centres) sit along the top edge between 0.55 and 0.80 of the width; couples below are chains of S-curves (hip, shoulder and arm arcs). Verticals cut the band into panes of unequal width, about 12 to 15 across, so the dancers are seen as if by successive slices. The central, most legible upright figure (dark, at 0.35) and two or three couples give the figures at intervals of roughly 0.10–0.15 of the width. 21 % of the area is near-black (`#34342D`), the bright vermilion (`#D34F2A`) ≈4 %, in a few vertical flares. Warm 48 %, cool 27 %, neutral 25 %, 10 % above C 50. The museum suggests one couple shown at several moments.

**Contrastes simultanés (Thyssen, 1913).** Small, 46 × 55 cm. A grid of three main verticals at ≈0.16, 0.37 and 0.68–0.87 of the width and horizontals at ≈0.36, 0.61–0.70 and 0.93 of the height. Two long arcs cross it from the top centre to the right edge. Wherever an arc crosses a grid line, colour changes on one side of it: the same arc is violet plus vermilion, then violet plus orange, then blue plus vermilion. The lightest passages are up and left, the black rectangle (≈0.31 × 0.22 of the canvas) lies low at centre, under a red-orange half-disc, the strongest value-plus-chroma jump (`#E14C24`, L 53 C 73, against near-black, L 24). Many patches fade to a pale tone of themselves at one edge. 48 % of edges have under 15 L of step. Vermilion 4.7 % and orange 3.8 % of the area do all the shouting.

**Couverture de berceau (1911).** About 60 to 65 pieces (by eye) in an irregular rectangular patchwork, 1 : 1.35. Sides are never quite parallel and corners never meet four at a time; seams stagger like brickwork. One seam, at ≈0.44 of the width, runs nearly the full height and is the only long line. Pieces range from ≈1 % to ≈5 % of the area. Some 15 pieces are cut on a diagonal and 5 have one curved side; these triangles turn corners and couple two rectangles into one shape. Black is a full colour: 17–18 % of the area, in ≈11 pieces spread over the whole field, five of them on the side edges. Pale cream/pink 19 %, gold-tan 12 %, crimson 8 %, olive 12 %. There is almost no blue (one slate patch at the right edge). Value does the work here: median step 36 L across seams, only 5 % of edges under 15 L, and 58 % of edges touch a near-neutral patch. Texture (velvet, satin, wool, towelling) is the second variable: neighbours close in colour differ in sheen.

**Robe simultanée (1913).** About 35 visible pieces on the front (by eye). A centre-front seam splits the bodice; on one side small bright patches, on the other one large dark one. Small patches gather at chest, shoulder and waist; long blades run down the skirt, so scale grows downward (the largest dark blade is ≈0.4 of the dress's height). Nearly half of the dress is darker than L 25 (≈47 %) and only ≈12 % is above C 40; light areas ≈9 %. The two reds, ultramarine and gold are small and placed at sleeve, chest and thigh, which are the parts that move. Fabrics change as well as colours: fur, velvet, satin, taffeta, plush. Curved seams follow the body where they can, and diagonals cut across it.

**Marché au Minho (1916) and Portuguese Market (1915).** Everything turns into discs: bull's head, faces, pots, melons, an umbrella, an aqueduct's arches. I count about 12 to 15 disc systems in the Pompidou canvas, with diameters from ≈0.04 to ≈0.25 of the width, packed lower right and spaced upper left, so scale and density give depth while perspective is absent. A straight diagonal (a staff) and a stretch of arched viaduct are the only non-circular devices. In the MoMA canvas one disc dominates: a striped melon, ≈0.28 of the width, just below and left of centre, with rings spreading from it into the background. Both photographs are dim gallery shots: measured chroma (10th–90th percentile 4–31) understates the wax colour, so only structure is used.

**Stockholm cover (1916).** Open spread. The right half is a self-portrait made of two ring systems, face included; the left half carries the words. Letters are painted one colour per letter or per group, in yellow, blue, red, green, black, grey, in spaced capitals of uneven size. Words run straight in the top pane, then on arcs that continue the ring geometry below the disc, the smallest line last. Text covers roughly 12 % of the spread. 17 % of the area above C 50; bare paper, as white, about a quarter.

**Dress fabrics (designed 1925–27, printed 1967).** O66980 repeats every 295 px across and ≈497 px down on the 1400 px photo, a repeat of about 1 : 1.68, divided into three equal columns (≈98 px), each a stack of 3–6 blocks of different heights. Colours: black 31 %, white 19 %, pale blue 19 %, red 17 %, yellow 9 %, plus overlaps. Solid blocks alternate with striped ones (two black bars on white, three yellow bars on white): stripes are a third value between the solids. No block touches another of its own colour along a full side, apart from corner contacts. The same drawing exists in a blue-teal-rust colourway (O267148 group), the interlocked letter-like drawing in green and purple and in yellow and black, the wave drawing in two pastel sets: the line-work stays, colours are reassigned by role (dark figure, bright, pale ground). On sheer organza a fold lays the print over itself, and overlaps give third and fourth colours that were never printed: the pattern's colour changes with the movement of the cloth.

**Gloria Swanson coat (c. 1924).** Stepped, nested U-bands in five values from cream through rose and red to brown and black. Band width is nearly constant; shapes lock like a key pattern; they are large on the back and break into smaller steps at hem and cuffs. A plain black hem closes the form. Red-family 29 of 69 parts (≈42 % of the coat), pinks ≈20 %, black ≈19 %, brown ≈19 %. No blue, no green.

**Portfolio plates (c. 1925).** Figures and swatches take about 8–12 % of the sheet, in a row, on bare paper with a small centred signature line at bottom right. The fierce pattern is shown small and isolated; only the textile itself is all-over.

**Rhythm (1945), Triptych (1963).** Same devices with fewer, flatter patches: split discs, arcs whose colour changes at a straight cut, a stripe fan. Pale ground areas grow to ≈40 % and red is ≈13 %. Useful as a check that the quadrant switch and the ring survive a change of palette and finish.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Axis or arc recolours the patch** (Prismes, study, Contrastes, Bal Bullier, Stockholm, Rhythm, Triptych). Geometry continues and colour does not. A ring keeps its radius across an axis (within ≈8 %) and takes a new hue or value in each quadrant. A single ring in Prismes spans L 16 to L 66 in its four quarters, and a line crossing an arc splits both.
- **Contrast is neighbour by neighbour, and mostly moderate** (all). In Prismes the median hue step is 57° and the median lightness step 19 L. Contrastes: 49° and 15 L, with 48 % of edges under 15 L. Only 7–16 % of chromatic edges exceed 120° in the two paintings of 1913–14 (5 % in the Prose column, 22 % on the Stockholm cover). Strong complementaries are rationed: 4–5 % vermilion in Contrastes and Bal Bullier.
- **Two ways to make an edge** (hue or value, per medium). Paintings lean on hue (39–48 % of edges near equal lightness). The quilt leans on value (median 36 L, 5 % near equal). La Prose's painted column is between (24 L, 30 %).
- **Black as a colour area, never as a line** (quilt 17 %, dress ≈47 % with dark green and fur, Prismes 24 % darker than L 30, Bal Bullier 21 %, Prose left 15 %, O66980 31 %). Contours are made by the meeting of two patches or by a sliver of bare ground. No drawn outline appears in the 1911–1927 works examined.
- **Colour fills the space the words leave** (Prose). Washes cover 24–32 % of the text column, and the ink lies on them only 3–7 % of the time. Wash edges copy line ends tooth by tooth, and the wash swaps sides when the alignment swaps.
- **Two equal columns at two volumes** (Prose). Widths 49.5 : 50.5; painting median L 63 and 20 % above C 50, text column median L 90 and 5 % above C 50. Same hues at the two intensities.
- **Start quiet, end on the one image** (Prose). About 5 % title, map 2.5 %, then continuous; the only figurative sign is in the last 6–7 %, level with the poem's last line.
- **Repetition with a changed coat** (Prose refrain six times in six faces; textiles, one drawing in two to three colourways; Bal Bullier, one couple perhaps at several moments). What repeats is the structure; what varies is the dressing.
- **Type varies by weight, width, slant, case and ink, seldom by size** (Prose: thirty faces, four inks, lines of similar size, ≈40 % of the ink in red/orange; Stockholm: one colour per letter group).
- **Text is another patch** (Prismes: words in coloured bricks, ≈0.6 % of the canvas; Stockholm: letters on the arcs). Where words enter a painting, they take the grid's own cells.
- **Off-centre discs, pale centres, growing rings** (Prismes, study, Bal Bullier, Minho). Disc centres at (0.51, 0.19) and (0.65, 0.49); ring widths irregular, 16–46 px near the centre, then ≈95–110 outside; the centre is the palest spot (L 81–88). Discs are cropped by the frame or by each other.
- **Scale and crowding give depth; perspective is absent** (Minho: discs 0.04–0.25 of the width, dense lower right; Prismes: discs over arcs; quilt: pieces of 1–5 %).
- **Irregular modules, shared axes** (quilt: ≈60–65 pieces, corners staggered, one full-height seam; Contrastes: three verticals and three horizontals; O66980: three equal columns with unequal blocks). Columns agree and rows do not.
- **Material carries the same contrast as colour** (quilt, dress). Velvet, satin, fur and towelling set neighbours of close hue apart.
- **Transparency makes extra colours** (organza; Prose washes). Overlap and dilution produce tones the palette did not list.
- **Warm leads, about half** (Prismes 52 : 31, Contrastes 48 : 22, Bal 48 : 27, Prose left 53 : 17, quilt 68 : 0, with black and cream; the 1945 canvas reverses to 21 : 59). The remainder is cool and neutral, with 10–20 % of the area at high chroma (above C 50) in the oils, the stencilled column and the cover.
- **Same method, every support** (quilt 1911, dress 1913, Prose 1913, Prismes 1914, car and matching coat of 1925 in a photograph, fabrics 1925–27). The pattern was made to be worn, folded, read or walked past, not framed.
- **The generative rule:** divide a surface by a few shared axes; run arcs and edges across them; at each crossing change the colour and keep the geometry, so that every patch is defined by its neighbours alone; let no outline in; keep a third of neighbours nearly equal in lightness and a few far apart; let black be an area; leave one quiet opening and one figurative ending. If there are words, give them their own equal column, set them in changing weights, and put the colour where they are not.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Sonia Delaunay.** *Divide the surface by a few shared axes, run the content's long shapes across them, and at each crossing change the fill while the geometry runs on, so every patch is set by its neighbours and no outline is needed; ration the contrast edge by edge; give words an equal column of their own and put colour only where the lines leave space.*
Process:
1. Fix the axes before any content: about three per direction at unequal intervals, run from edge to edge with no inner margin. Columns agree and rows do not: blocks in one column are 3–6 of unequal height, corners never meet four at a time, and one axis at most runs the full length. In a dense field a module takes 1–5% of the area.
2. Lay each continuous thing (a route, a series, a band, a curve) across the axes whole. At every axis it keeps its path and width (within about 8%) and takes a new fill; one shape may pass through up to four states. An axis that has split one shape runs on as the edge of the next.
3. Draw no outline and no divider. An edge is the meeting of two fills or a sliver of bare ground. Then budget the edges: 30–50% of them under 15 L, made by hue alone (a middle-sized hue step, about 50–60°); the median edge 15–25 L; under a sixth of them a far hue step (over 120°); and one largest jump of value and chroma together, used once. With few hues, move the whole budget to value: median about 35 L, and over half the edges touch a neutral. An edge the user must tell apart always gets the value step; hue-alone edges only group.
4. Set the shares after the edges: the darkest tone is an area of 15–30%, in several pieces spread over the field, never a line; high chroma takes 10–20% of the area and the loudest hue 4–5%, in a few places; one temperature leads at about half, the opposite takes a quarter to a third, near-neutrals the rest.
5. Where the view has words and a field, give them two equal parallel columns (within 1%) along the reading direction; neither crosses the divide and no rule marks it. Set the text column in the field's own hues at a second volume: about 25–30 L lighter at the median, a quarter of the high-chroma share, nothing dark but the ink.
6. Inside the text column, colour goes where the lines are short: beside the ragged end, in the indent, between two blocks. Its edge steps line by line with the line ends and leaves a halo of bare ground round the letters. Fills cover 24–32% of the column and under 7% of the text lies on any of them. When the alignment changes side, the fill changes to the other.
7. Pace by density: per band (about a twentieth of the length), fill cover swings between about 5% and 55%, and the first and last bands are near the maximum. Open with a quiet block (2.5–5% of the length) and keep the one literal picture for the last 6–7%, level with the line of text that names it.
8. Repeat the structure and change the dressing: a line that recurs keeps its words and size and returns in another weight, width, slant, case or ink (up to six times, at irregular gaps); a pattern that recurs keeps its drawing and has its colours reassigned by role (dark figure, bright, pale ground).
Why it works:
- The eye continues a path across a cut (grouping by continuation), so a new fill on the same path reads as one thing in a new state. Break the geometry at the axis as well and it becomes two things; keep the fill and the axis disappears. Because the path holds the patches together, no outline is needed.
- A fill is judged against its neighbours (simultaneous contrast). An outline isolates each patch and cancels that. With every edge strong, nothing leads and the field is a chequerboard; with a third to a half of the edges nearly equal in lightness, the field holds together as one tone and the few value jumps win by oddness.
- Colour in the space the lines leave keeps every letter on bare ground at full contrast, and the stepped edge states the shape of the text, so the two are figure and counter-figure. A tint under the text lowers its contrast and turns colour into background; a picture above it turns colour into illustration.
- The same hues at two volumes hold the two columns together by likeness, while the gap in value says which is scanned in one sweep and which is read line by line.
- A repeat in the same dress is skipped (adaptation). The same words in a new dress are recognised and looked at again. One literal picture at the end is salient because it is the only one.
Colour relations: every fill is chosen against its neighbours, never by category; lead temperature about 50%, opposite 25–33%, near-neutral the rest; darkest tone an area of 15–30%; high chroma 10–20% of the area, the loudest hue 4–5%; 30–50% of edges near equal lightness; the second volume is the same hues diluted (median 25–30 L lighter, a quarter of the chroma share); overlap of two fills may make a third that the palette does not list.
Space and line: a few shared axes at unequal intervals; columns agree, rows stagger; shapes run to the frame and are cut by it; no line of any weight, the gap between neighbours being bare ground; words and field in equal columns; depth by scale and crowding only (units from 0.04 to 0.25 of the width, packed toward one corner), no perspective and no shadow.
Type requirement: one family or a small set with a wide range of weight, width, slant and case at nearly one size; the voice changes by block, never by word; up to four inks, changed by block; lines level, flush left or flush right with a ragged side, never justified, so that the line ends leave the space the colour takes. Words placed inside the field are small (under 1% of it, about 12% on a cover) and sit in the field's own cells, one fill per cell, the largest word a name.
Motion and interaction: change by state and not by displacement. When a shape crosses an axis (on scroll, or as data arrive) its fill switches in place, as a cut or a cross-fade of 150ms or less; nothing slides, spins or blurs. The two columns scroll as one sheet, and each fold-length section is one legible unit. Selection is the rationed strong edge: a value step of 30 L or more against the neighbours, never hue alone. Focus is a sliver of bare ground round the target plus a ring at 3:1 or more. Overlapping layers show their mix only while they overlap. Reduced motion: switches become cuts.
Fails when: hue must mean a category everywhere (status colours, chart legends), because here hue belongs to position and neighbours; every cell must be equally legible (tables, long forms); the text fills its measure or is justified, leaving no space for colour; the screen is too narrow for two equal columns of readable measure (stack them, and keep the fills in the line-end space only); a recurring label must be recognised by its form (status and navigation labels keep one dress).
Signature (do not carry together): concentric rings cut into quarters of unlike rainbow colour · a patchwork of bright scraps with black pieces · a tall folded sheet of stencilled colour beside verse in many mixed typefaces.

Release notes page for a database product, desktop 1280 wide and long, cool greys with one brand cobalt and one amber, sober and technical (the works are warm, hand-made and festive).

## Space & composition
- **Split by the fold.** La Prose: two 49.5/50.5 columns, 199 × 35.5 cm (5.6 : 1), 11 rows of ≈9.4 cm, sheet joins at ≈50 cm. The fold is respected by both media; only the page as a whole unites them.
- **Serrated interlock.** Wash to text share ≈24–32 % : ≈68–76 %, with bare paper, not a rule, as the gap. Text flush changes side ≈20 times down the sheet.
- **Pacing by density.** Wash cover per twenty-second band swings between 4 % and 55 % of the column, high at both ends (48 and 49 %).
- **Discs on a rectilinear skeleton.** Prismes: two discs on a diagonal, centre quadrants, axes that run on into panes. Contrastes: three verticals and three horizontals, with arcs.
- **Frieze.** Bal Bullier: ≈4 : 1, ≈12–15 panes, figures every 0.10–0.15 of the width, halos only in the top quarter at 0.55–0.80.
- **Nothing is empty in the paintings; a lot is empty on the plates.** Portfolio figures ≈8–12 % of the sheet, textiles 100 %.
- **Module hierarchy.** Fabric O66980: repeat 1 : 1.68, three equal columns, unequal blocks in each, one row of full-bleed solid alternating with a striped one.
- **No frame and no margin inside the painting;** patches run to the edge and are cut by it.

## Form, line & typography
- **No line.** Form is the meeting of two fills. A drawn contour appears only in her pre-Cubist work (Yellow Nude, 1908, has dark contours) and again as black bands after 1940, where it is an area.
- **Three edges in one work:** hard (stencil or seam), toothed, and dry-brushed fade. La Prose uses all three.
- **Curve over straight:** arcs are quarter or half circles of a common centre; straights are the axes and pane edges. Curves break at straights, not the reverse.
- **Letterforms, La Prose:** a heterogeneous case of job types, roman, bold, condensed, extended, italic, small capitals and slab, mixed per stanza; similar sizes; four inks; lines horizontal; flush left or flush right, never justified as a block; the last line centred. Titles in bold italic and bold old-style.
- **Letterforms, painted:** freehand capitals of uneven height, each filled in a different colour, with no outline; spaced; on straight lines or on arcs of the composition.
- **Words in a painting are small:** ≈0.6 % of Prismes; the biggest word is a name.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- **Downward reading in two tempos.** The eye can fall the length of the colour column in one sweep (continuous curves), while the poem is read line by line. The two meet at the foot. A fold every 9.4 cm makes each section a legible unit, and the panels are unfolded in order.
- **Pulse:** wash density swings between 4 % and 55 % from band to band; alignment flips ≈20 times; hue families change about every three to four bands.
- **Refrain with changing voice:** one sentence at 41, 43.5, 47.7, 49.8, 54.5 and 59 % of the height, in six faces. Gaps of 2.5, 4.2, 2.1, 4.7, 4.5 %: irregular, clustered in the middle, absent in the first 40 % and the last 40 %.
- **Motion by colour rather than by displacement** (Prismes, Bal Bullier): the same arc or ring changes state as the eye crosses an axis. The contemporary title of Bal Bullier, "Mouvement, couleur, profondeur, danse", names movement first.
- **Spin without speed:** discs are cut in quadrants of unlike value, so a ring looks to turn even though nothing is blurred.
- **Cloth in motion:** transparency and fur, satin and velvet change with the body; the pattern's third colour exists only where fabric overlaps. The V&A notes her theory linked hue combinations to how the body's movement alters a garment.

## UI translations
*For strict studies and homage. A kit takes "Process (derived)" and its own surface.*

1. **Recolour at the axis.** Cut each ring, band or arc by the layout's verticals and horizontals; keep its radius and width across the cut and give each segment a different fill.
2. **No borders.** Separate blocks by the meeting of two fills or by a sliver of bare ground; never by a stroke.
3. **Edge budget.** About 40% of adjacent blocks near equal in lightness and different in hue, the median pair 15–25 L apart, and one pair per view with the full jump of value and chroma.
4. **Black as a block.** The darkest tone fills 15–30% of the view as several separate pieces; it never outlines.
5. **Loud colour small.** High chroma on 10–20% of the area; the hottest hue on 4–5%, in a few flares.
6. **Colour column, text column.** Split a long page down the middle: a dense colour field on one side, the text on the other, neither crossing the fold.
7. **Tints in the rag.** In the text column, put pale tints of the field's hues in the space beyond the line ends, stepped line by line with a halo of ground; under 7% of the text on any tint. Alternate flush left and flush right and move the tint to the other side.
8. **Density pulse.** Let tint cover swing between about 5% and 55% from one section to the next, and make the first and last sections the fullest.
9. **One picture, last.** Open with a quiet title block; hold the only literal image for the final 6–7% of the page, level with the line that names it.
10. **Refrain in new dress.** Set a recurring line in a different weight, width, slant, case or ink each time, at one size.
11. **Colourways by role.** Offer one pattern drawing in two or three colour sets by reassigning dark figure, bright and pale ground.
12. **Off-centre systems with pale centres.** Place concentric systems off-centre and cropped by the frame, palest at the centre, with narrow inner bands and outer bands two to three times wider.
13. **Staggered blocks.** In a repeat, keep the columns equal and vary block heights; alternate solid blocks with striped ones as a third value.
14. **State, not travel.** Animate by switching a segment's fill as it crosses an axis; overlapping sheer layers show a third colour.

## What this artist would reject
- Outlining shapes to separate them, or giving every block the same border.
- A background tint laid under the text, or an image as a band above it: in La Prose colour and words share a sheet without overlapping.
- One flat colour per category, repeated everywhere: in her work hue belongs to position and neighbours.
- A palette of evenly loud saturated colours: her high chroma is ≈10–20 % of the area, with pale, dark and greyed patches around it.
- Symmetry and centred single focal points: discs are off-centre, cropped, and in groups.
- A repeat that is merely a grid of identical tiles: columns agree and rows stagger.
- One typeface, one weight, one voice for a text whose tone changes.
- Illustrating the text: the only image in La Prose arrives at the last line.

## Signature prompt seed
Divide the view by a few shared axes at unequal intervals, with equal columns and staggered rows. Run each continuous shape across the axes whole, and at every crossing keep its path and change its fill. Draw no outline: an edge is two fills meeting or a sliver of bare ground. Keep a third to a half of the edges nearly equal in lightness, most of the rest a moderate step, and one jump at full strength. Make the darkest tone an area, keep loud colour to a small share and the loudest to a twentieth. Give the words an equal column of their own in the same hues diluted, and put colour only in the space the lines leave, stepped to the line ends. Let the density swing from section to section, open quietly, and hold the one picture for the end. When a line recurs, keep its words and size and change its dress. Animate by change of state in place, never by travel.
