# Georges Braque — Cubism

## Essence
Braque makes the space between things as solid as the things, then loosens the parts of a picture from one another: outline, tone, colour, texture and lettering each keep their own shape and place and meet only by overlapping. A subject is known from a few exact tokens (a scroll, an f-hole, a word, a strip of wood grain) set in one continuous field of small planes that are hard on one edge and open on the other. From 1912 the local colour is a separate flat piece laid on the sheet, and the drawing runs across it without following its edge.

## Key works examined
- **Houses at L'Estaque (Maisons à l'Estaque), 1908, oil on canvas, 73 × 59.5 cm, Kunstmuseum Bern (Rupf Foundation).** Teaches stacking instead of recession, and leaking one plane into the next. *(visually checked: WikiArt scan 835 × 1040, looks darkened; palette, edge angles, L profiles across facets, crop of the middle)*
- **Violin and Palette, 1909, oil on canvas, 91.7 × 42.8 cm, Guggenheim 54.1412.** Teaches tokens left whole inside shattered objects, and one illusionistic nail as a foil. *(visually checked: Guggenheim museum image 578 × 1280, close to 1 : 2.2; palette, edge angles, crop of the nail)*
- **Violin and Candlestick (Nature morte, Violon et compotier), 1910, oil on canvas, 61.0 × 50.3 cm, SFMOMA 89.78.** Teaches the off-axis scaffold of small rectangles and objects the same colour as their air. *(visually checked: SFMOMA museum image 847 × 1024; palette, edge angles, centre/border density)*
- **Violin and Pitcher, 1909–10, oil on canvas, 116.8 × 73.2 cm, Kunstmuseum Basel.** Teaches facet size falling toward the subject. *(visually checked for structure only: WikiArt scan 724 × 1200, badly over-saturated red, so its colour is not used)*
- **The Portuguese (Le Portugais), 1911–12, oil on canvas, 116.7 × 81.5 cm (sources vary by a few mm), Kunstmuseum Basel.** Teaches flat stencilled letters as a fixed datum on a moving surface. *(visually checked: WikiArt 824 × 1206 and Univ. at Buffalo 1074 × 1605; palette, letter heights, row/column lightness, crop of top third. Both scans are yellow, the second more so)*
- **Clarinet and Bottle of Rum on a Mantelpiece, 1911, oil on canvas, Tate T02318.** Teaches the near-grey palette at its true chroma, and words surfacing and sinking. *(visually checked: Tate image 574 × 768 with frame; palette, angles)*
- **Homage to J. S. Bach, winter 1911–12, oil on canvas, ≈54 × 73 cm, MoMA.** Teaches one combed wood-grain patch and one stencilled name as the two opaque facts in a translucent field. *(visually checked: WikiArt 750 × 559; palette, angles, letter size, crop)*
- **Fruit Dish and Glass, Sorgues, autumn 1912, charcoal, cut and pasted printed wallpaper and gouache on paper, 62.9 × 45.7 cm, Met 2016.237.33.** First papier collé: colour leaves the outline. *(visually checked: WikiArt 787 × 1074; strip polygons, chroma mask, charcoal fade profiles, letter sizes, crop)*
- **Still Life with Tenora (The Clarinet), 1913, pasted papers, charcoal, chalk and pencil on gessoed canvas, ≈95 × 120 cm, MoMA.** Teaches one long plank as a datum and a short deck of overlaps. *(visually checked: WikiArt scan of an old colour plate, greenish whites; piece areas, angles, crop)*
- **Violin and Pipe (formerly Le Quotidien), winter 1913–14, charcoal, graphite, chalk and pasted papers on paper on card, 74 × 106 cm, Centre Pompidou AM 3405 D.** Teaches five kinds of material, each with one role, inside an oval. *(visually checked: WikiArt 1134 × 768; piece areas, angles)*
- **Aria de Bach, 1913, black and wood-grain papers, charcoal and white chalk on paper, ≈62 × 46 cm, NGA 1982.55.2.** Teaches line that changes colour where the ground changes. *(visually checked: WikiArt 759 × 1010; piece areas, angles)*
- **Checkerboard: Tivoli-Cinéma, 1913, pasted papers (incl. a whole handbill) and charcoal.** Teaches the ready-made page as one plane. *(visually checked: WikiArt 750 × 539, dark scan; piece areas. Collection not confirmed)*
- **Woman with a Guitar (Femme à la guitare), 1913, oil and charcoal on canvas, 130 × 73 cm, Centre Pompidou.** Teaches the collage logic painted by hand. *(visually checked: WikiArt 691 × 1235, yellow-green cast; areas, angles, crop of the guitar)*
- **The Musician's Table, 1913, oil and charcoal on canvas, 65 × 92 cm, Kunstmuseum Basel.** Teaches a see-through deck of tilted sheets over an oval. *(visually checked for structure: WikiArt 1600 × 1118, over-warm scan, so its hexes are not used)*
- **Still Life on a Table (with "Gillette"), 1914, charcoal, gouache and pasted papers on paper, 48 × 62 cm (Pompidou, Nature morte sur la table; identity with this image likely, not proved).** Teaches a tilt of the whole material layer. *(visually checked: WikiArt 1056 × 804; piece areas, angles)*
- **Pedestal Table, 1911; Duo pour flûte, 1913–14 (Met, Lauder); Man with a Guitar, 1914 (Pompidou).** *(not obtained: Wikimedia returned 429 on every request, Met and MoMA pages blocked automated reading)*

## Detailed observations

**Houses at L'Estaque, 1908.** A stack of about a dozen prisms fills the canvas from bottom edge to top edge. There is no sky, no horizon, no door and no window. Recession is replaced by height: the farther house is simply higher, overlapped by the nearer one, and not smaller by any consistent rule. The tree forms a bent diagonal from the bottom centre to the upper left, and a second sapling leans the other way at right, so two dark-edged diagonals bracket the pile like parentheses. Edges cluster on two diagonals: the 15° bins at 45° and 60° hold 30% of strong edge energy and those at 120° and 135° hold 19%, against 5% within 8° of horizontal. Light has no single source. On the central house the wall reads L 62–68 at its outer left edge, drops to 35 at the internal corner, then climbs back to 62 by the right: every plane is darkest where it meets its neighbour and lightest in its own body. The next gable does the same (L 73 in the body, 41 at the fold, 69 beyond it, 25 at the far edge). Passage is visible in the crop: the ridge of one roof has no left edge and slides as an ochre tongue into the trees, and the foliage has a crisp edge on one side (blue-green against ochre) and smears on the other. Colour is two families: ochre to brown (C > 28, 44% of the area, hue ≈78°) and green-grey (hue > 95°, 33%). Grey-blue trunks are the only coolish accent. Outlines are dark, short and broken, with bare canvas hairlines left between planes. Absent: sky, figures, windows, cast shadows, smoke, texture difference between wall and hill.

**Violin and Palette, 1909.** A very tall slot, 1 : 2.14. Three objects stack vertically with no table between them: violin (lower half), open score (a stepped fan at 0.17–0.48 of height), palette hung from a nail (top 0.20). A green curtain strip falls at the top right. The violin is broken into some 15–20 facets, but its scroll, both f-holes, strings and tailgut are drawn whole, at full legibility. Facets get small around the violin (roughly 60–120 px in a 578 px width) and large at the rim. The two f-holes are unequal in size and height and the strings break and restart offset in three groups. The nail at the top is painted in plain illusion with a cast shadow falling left; it is the only object with a consistent shadow. Colour: 62% of the area sits at C ≤ 12; warm brown (L 38–56, C 21–22) covers 18% and belongs to the violin; green is a low-chroma tint in the shadows. Median chroma 10. Vertical edges 22%, horizontal 5%: the field is tall, leaning and shardlike. Corners differ: top left L-grey 164, bottom left 53.

**Violin and Pitcher, 1909–10 (structure only).** Again ≈1 : 1.65 and stacked: score sheet with a turned-up corner at the top, pitcher at 0.22–0.58, violin at 0.42–1.0. The upper third holds wide calm panels, the middle and bottom are shards. 25% of edge energy is vertical, 5% horizontal, the rest slanted around 60–75° and 105–120°. The scroll and the f-holes stay whole again. A dado moulding with three hard horizontal steps at right gives the one architectural truth.

**Violin and Candlestick, 1910.** The shards have settled into a loose grid. Verticals (23%) and horizontals (13%) now both exceed chance (9% each), with a handful of diagonals: the knife at lower right, the violin neck, the folded paper. The outlined objects occupy a central island from roughly 0.17 to 0.82 of the width; around them the ground is a patchwork of larger rectangles of horizontal dabs. Edge energy in the centre is 1.59 times the border. The candlestick foot is tipped up into a near circle while its cylinder is seen from the side. Contours are dark blue-grey, thick, and open: a curve is inked on its shadow side for half its length, then stops, and the colour of the object carries on into the ground. Colour: seven clusters all between L 31 and 69, C 4 to 29, hue 77–101°; half the area is C ≤ 17. Hue says object or air only faintly: the same ochre is wall, table and violin. Contrast is kept in the mid-tone, 5th percentile L 29, 95th 72, no black, no white.

**The Portuguese, 1911–12.** A seated player with a guitar, built as one tall pyramid, broad and dark below, narrow and light above. Mean lightness by eighths from top to bottom: 55, 45, 45, 41, 36, 34, 32, 28, a drop of 27 L in a near-steady ramp. By columns from left to right: 52, 40, 30, 37, 36, 37, 38, 47, so the sides are lighter by 10–20 L and dissolve to thinly covered canvas. Corners at the top are near bare ground (L 71–79). Inside: a strict vertical spine at 0.49 of the width, upper torso planes at 70–80° leaning to it, a counter diagonal at ≈30° in the guitar arm. Dabs are short, horizontal, brick-like, 10–25 px long in an 824 px width, laid in patches whose direction never changes, so tone flickers inside every plane. Tokens: 4 or 5 parallel strings, a sound hole, a moustache-like pair of curves, buttons or eyelets as two heavy commas, a rope and bollard, a bottle at top. Letters: "D BAL" at top right, cap height 5.1% of the picture height, width 33% of the picture width; "D CO" at left cropped by the edge; "&"; "10,40" at 2.2%. They are stencilled, so each letter carries stencil bridges and reads as perfectly frontal, and they do not tilt, shrink or foreshorten with the planes. Yet they are not uniform ink: B is L 23, the D beside it L 50. Planes slide over part of a word and the letters fade with the paint behind them. They ring the figure in the pale outer zone and never enter the dark core. Colour: median chroma reads 25 in this scan, which is varnish and print cast; compare Tate, C 8.

**Clarinet and Bottle of Rum, 1911.** The same process at true chroma: seven clusters, all C 4–14, hue 82–101°, L 33–73. In effect a warm grey painting with 5.7% of soft tan at C 14. Diagonal bracing beams run from the corners to a centre bulge; the clarinet is a level cylinder at 0.42 of height. Word fragments "(V)ALSE", "RH" (RHUM) and what looks like "PARI" sit at different places, each cut by a plane. A pin with a small shadow is drawn near the clarinet. The periphery is bare light ground; corners at the top read grey 175, at the bottom 102–143. Painted f-hole-like scrolls repeat as a motif five or six times through the picture, at different sizes, belonging now to a bracket, now to an instrument.

**Homage to J. S. Bach, 1911–12.** Landscape format, almost orthogonal: vertical edges 33%, horizontal 16%, the rest at small leaning angles. Centre edge energy 1.61 of the border. The stencilled "BACH" (cap height 7.2% of the height, width 15% of the width) with "J S" on a second line is the darkest, sharpest, smallest area of high contrast, at (0.56, 0.66) of the width and height, below and right of centre. The wood-grain patch at lower left (0–0.07 across, 0.70–1.0 down; a small second one above it) is done with comb-like wavy lines; it stops dead on its right edge and is the one warm saturated note (≈C 26 against a median of 13). Violin tokens (two f-holes, unequal; one scroll curl) float at two heights and are not joined by a body. A table corner with a fringe is drawn in plain perspective at the bottom. Nothing is outlined all the way round.

**Fruit Dish and Glass, 1912.** Three pieces of the same printed oak-panelled wallpaper on white paper: a tall block at left (≈17% of the sheet), a column at upper right (≈5.5%) and a low block at the bottom (≈5%), about 3 : 1.1 : 1. Together they are 27% by polygon, 23% by chroma mask. All three are placed on the edge of the drawn still life, not on it: two touch the sheet's edge (top left, bottom), the third stops short. They are upright, ±1–2°, and their own printed double black rules give strong verticals. The drawing is charcoal, tonal and grey, centred: grapes, dish, foot, glass, with a blank light paper core of L 87–93 in the dish. Charcoal marks cross the paper edges: a curve, a shade, the B of "BAR" (half on wood, half on white). The wood never fills a drawn contour. It says wood (table, panelling, drawer, whose knob is a drawn circle) without being a shape of it. Charcoal planes are graded: L goes 90 to 16 within about 8 px (0.7% of the height) at the hard edge, then back to 84 over the next 40 px (3.7%): a ratio of about 1 : 5. The gradients mostly start at the top and fade down. Letters: "BAR" cap height 8.4% of the height, at the right edge, level; "ALE" 6.5%, at left, its E rotated about 30° and dropped. Each word is a fragment (beer, café, "A" shared). Chroma: wood C 47–54, everything else C 6–14.

**Violin and Pipe, 1913–14.** Pasted pieces make 29% of the sheet: wood 10.8%, newspaper 12.9% in two pieces (9.7 and 3.2), black 4.2%, a strip of patterned border 1.3%, plus a small paper pipe. The bundle is one tight stack around the centre (edge energy centre to border 2.55); the sheet around it is blank at L 87–91 for 67% of the area. A light ellipse drawn in charcoal spans 65% of the width and is cut by the top and the bottom. Outside it, soft combed charcoal shading on left and right makes the air. Pieces lean a few degrees each way: wood 5° off vertical, left headline 15° counterclockwise, right news sheet about 10°. The long news sheet is pasted with its columns upside down and is grey texture; the headline piece is upright and readable ("Le Quo", cut by the plank). Black silhouette is a double lobe, half a violin, with a white chalk f-hole. The violin is therefore said four times, partly: wood plank, black half, f-holes in dark and in white, a drawn scroll at top.

**Still Life with Tenora, 1913.** A plank of wood-grain (13.2% of the paper, 6.6 : 1 in shape, 81% of the paper's width) runs across at −5.7°, the longest straight thing in the picture. Black pieces stick out above and below it (1.4, 1.9 and 2.2%), a newspaper scrap (2.9%) at +9.5°, a blank pinkish card (3.1%). Total pieces ≈25%. Stacking order is legible: paper, black, wood, news, card, with the drawn clarinet lying under the plank, showing faintly through it and emerging below. The right-hand edge of the black at lower right is a step cut, not a curve. "L'ECHO D'A" is cut by the pink card with no completion. Edges 34% horizontal, 30% vertical, so 64% orthogonal: the most architectural work in the set, set off by the small plank tilt. Centre to border 3.89.

**Aria de Bach, 1913.** Three big pieces, 34% of the sheet: wood wedge 13.1%, black 8.4%, black 12.3%. The wedge is wider at the top and points down at the title. The guitar strings are charcoal on white paper, then white chalk when they cross the black, then charcoal again below: one line, inverted by its ground. The glass at left is drawn on black in chalk. The guitar's outline is a contour on the right only, the black rectangle stands for the body, offset about half a body to the left. Lettering is drawn, calm and level (tilted with its sheet by 12° or so), the only literal caption.

**Checkerboard, 1913.** Poster 12.8%, three brown strips 5.1, 5.0, 3.6%: 26.5% of the sheet. The handbill keeps its own typography, read in full except where a strip cuts its right margin. "JOURN" is drawn on a tilted sheet and stops, so a whole printed text and an abbreviated drawn one sit side by side. Black 2 × 3 checks (at about 0.63, 0.7) are the darkest area.

**Woman with a Guitar, 1913.** No paper is pasted: all is oil and charcoal imitating paper. A wood-grain trapezoid (6.8%, its top edge 0.81 of its bottom edge) is the guitar; a second flat wood rectangle (2%) at the top; flat black sickles at left (3.3%), right (3.5%) and lower left (0.6%) that stand for hair, sleeve or shadow and each end in points. A grey-blue bar down the middle at 0.39–0.54 of the width runs from the hat through the face to the body and down to a fading bottom edge, ignoring all the outlines. Guitar strings are drawn on the wood and run on past its right edge on to a white plane, continuing unbroken. "LE REVE… / ORGANE" and "SO…ATE" (Sonate) are lettered on tilted sheets and cut. The face is one large plain shape with two-way features: a small frontal mouth and a displaced eye.

**The Musician's Table, 1913 (structure only).** A big ellipse spans about 0.02–0.93 of the width, broken in four places by sheets that pass over or through it. White and black sheets sit at +35° and −60°; "VAL", "HIST" and a spade are cut or complete. Planes are translucent: the dark strip at the left shows the line behind it. Dark blots and sawtooth fringe at left, bottom and right edges repeat the same comb-like mark three times.

**Still Life with Gillette, 1914.** Wood 16% in two pieces, black 10.5%, cut newsprint 6.7%, dotted paper 2.5%: 36% pasted, 56% of the sheet bare at L 86–90. The plank is tilted 47°, the other 9°. The black shape has straight and curved edges and reads as the shadow of everything. One commercial label (Gillette razor packet) is whole, upright on its own tilt, inside a torn and cut newspaper. Edge directions spread evenly (horizontal 10%, vertical 12%): here the whole deck is rotated against the sheet.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Colour is a separate layer, parked beside the form** (Fruit Dish and Glass, Aria de Bach, Tenora, Violin and Pipe, Gillette). Wood paper covers 11–23% of the sheet (27% by outline in Fruit Dish), never fits a contour, and its edges run at 0–8° off the axis (47° once). The drawing has no local colour of its own (charcoal, C ≤ 14); the paper has C 44–54.
- **Drawing runs across layer edges, and changes polarity where it must** (Fruit Dish: "BAR" and shading over wood; Woman with a Guitar: strings past the wood edge; Aria: strings charcoal, then white chalk on black, then charcoal). The line keeps its path; only its value flips.
- **Pasted or flat material is about a quarter to a third of the surface, never more than half** (Fruit Dish 23–27%, Tenora 25%, Checkerboard 26%, Violin and Pipe 29%, Aria 34%, Gillette 36%, Woman with a Guitar 16%). The rest is one light ground (L 80–90, 54–72% of the area).
- **Few pieces, one family** (Fruit Dish 3 pieces of one paper; Aria 3; Checkerboard 4; Violin and Pipe 6 or 7). Three or so material roles: wood (C 44–54), black (L 7–15, 4–21%), print (L 39–58, 3–13%), patterned paper (1–3%).
- **The deck is shallow and its order is legible** (Tenora: paper, black, wood, news, card). Each piece is cut flat, overlaps one or two neighbours only, and is never shadowed.
- **Hard on one side, open on the other** (Houses, Violin and Candlestick, Fruit Dish). A plane has one crisp dark edge and fades over 5 times that width to the ground (Fruit Dish: 90 to 16 in ≈0.7% of the height, back over ≈3.7%). In Houses each plane is L 62–73 in its body and 35–41 where it meets the next, from either side, so shadows mark joints, not a light source.
- **Passage: an object leaks into its neighbour on purpose** (Houses, roof ridge open at the left; Violin and Candlestick, contour stops halfway; Portuguese, sides fade to bare ground). By eye, objects are closed on about half their outline.
- **Whole tokens, broken bodies** (Violin and Palette, Violin and Pitcher, Bach, Fruit Dish). Scroll, f-hole, strings, mouth, nail and grapes stay whole, exact and small; the large body is broken. Two f-holes are always unequal in size or height.
- **One object, several descriptions, at different places** (Violin and Pipe: plank, black half-body, two f-holes, scroll; Aria: strings, black rectangle, wedge, contour). No two agree on outline.
- **Two viewing angles for one thing, tipped toward the viewer** (Candlestick's foot tilted to a near circle, body seen from the side; table corner in Bach; oval table tops). The plan and the elevation of an object are both shown.
- **One plain-illusion object per picture as the measure of the rest** (nail with cast shadow in Violin and Palette; pin in Clarinet; table corner with fringe in Bach). It is the only thing with a consistent light. *Museum texts confirm the nail, the Met dates it as his first such motif.*
- **Letters are a level datum on the surface** (Portuguese, Bach, Fruit Dish, Clarinet). Stencilled or drawn, frontal, cap height 2.2–8.4% of picture height, 5–8% for the main word; they do not foreshorten. Their ink varies (L 23 to 50 in one word, Portuguese).
- **Words are cut** (D BAL, D CO, L'ECHO D'A, JOURN, VAL, HIST, Le Quo, SONATE). At least one word per work after 1911 is clipped by an edge or a plane, two or three letters missing, still recognisable.
- **Print as texture and print as word** (Violin and Pipe: upside-down column sheet is grey texture, headline is read; Checkerboard: whole handbill beside a drawn "JOURN"). Texture print stays at L 50–60; readable print is a large heavy word, by eye 4–10 times bigger.
- **Value pyramid, light outer ring** (Portuguese: top to bottom L 55 to 28, sides lighter by 10–20; Clarinet in weaker form: grey 175 at the top corners, 102–143 at the bottom). Density and darkness go to the core or the base; edges are nearly empty.
- **Density gradient: centre 1.2 to 3.9 times the border** (edge energy; Violin and Palette 1.2, Candlestick 1.6, Bach 1.6, Fruit Dish 1.4, Gillette 2.5, Violin and Pipe 2.6, Tenora 3.9). Pieces cluster, facets grow toward the rim, and the rim is bare or a dab field.
- **Almost orthogonal, then broken by one leaning thing** (vertical edges are 21–44% of strong edge energy where chance is 9%; horizontals 5–34%; Tenora's 64% orthogonal has the plank at 6°). From 1910 the shards settle to a grid; the diagonal minority carries the life.
- **Narrow hue, mid contrast (analytic), then whole range (synthetic).** 1910–11: C 4–29, hue 77–101°, L 5th to 95th percentile 29–72 (Candlestick), 32–74 (Clarinet). 1912–14: ground L 80–90, blacks L 7–15, wood C 44–54. Broad range with almost one hue: ochre to brown.
- **Material made by a trade, not by the brush** (comb-drawn grain in Bach; printed grain in Fruit Dish; stencilled letters; sand in pigment per Pompidou). Texture is a fact with its own tool, flat, and placed in only one or two spots.
- **The generative rule, as observed:** set the ground light and let it stay 55–70% open; draw the subject in grey as a centred cluster with open edges; keep each named detail whole and small; place 3–6 flat material pieces, 25–35% in all, in one grain family plus black plus print, at small tilts, beside and not inside the outlines; let the drawing cross them, flipping light to dark; set one level word or fragment, 5–8% of the height; add one plainly rendered thing. Cluster the dark in the core or base and let the sides fade.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Georges Braque.** *Give line, colour and lettering each a layer with its own shape and place, joined only by overlap; name each thing by a few small details kept whole while its body stays open, and hold one level word as the fixed measure of the surface.*
Process:
1. Fix one ground value and keep 55–70% of the view uncovered. Gather the subject into one cluster with a bare rim: detail 1.5–4 times denser in the core than at the border, the heavier values in the core or at the base.
2. Draw all the content in a neutral layer of line and tone, at the lowest chroma in the view, on a nearly orthogonal scaffold. Close each region on about half its outline: one hard edge, then a fade about five times as wide back to the ground. The darkest tone goes where two regions meet, never under a thing as its shadow.
3. Name each thing by 2–4 diagnostic details kept whole, exact and small, a quarter to a third of the view apart and never adjacent. Leave the body between them open.
4. When a thing needs two views (from above and from the side, the whole and the part), set both frontally on the same plane, tied by a detail from step 3. Depth is a deck of 3–5 flat sheets in a readable order; nothing recedes.
5. Add colour as a separate layer: 3–6 flat pieces, 25–35% of the view in all, in at most three roles (one chromatic family, the darkest value, a mid-value texture), the largest about three times each of the others. Park each beside the thing it qualifies, overlapping it but fitting no outline, 2–10° or a part-width out of register, touching one or two neighbours, unshadowed.
6. Run the line of step 2 across every piece edge on one path; it only flips value where the ground under it flips.
7. Set one word frontal and level in the open zone beside the cluster, cap height 5–8% of the view's height, with one second size about a third of that. It never leans with the pieces. A piece may cover two or three of its letters if the full text is stated elsewhere. Text used as texture stays at mid value, 4–10 times smaller.
8. Give the widest value step to the smallest area (that word or one detail, 1–3% of the view), and render exactly one small thing literally, with the only cast shadow, as the measure of the rest.
Why it works:
- Closure: an outline closed on half its length is still completed by the eye, while its open half shares tone with the neighbour, so many things read as one surface. Close it fully and each thing is a sticker on a background; open it fully and the details have no body to belong to.
- Recognition by diagnostic parts: a thing is known from a few details, not its silhouette, so the body can be open, shared or shown twice. Break the details too and nothing can be named.
- Grouping by proximity, not containment: colour that overlaps a form without fitting it still belongs to it, which frees line to carry structure and colour to carry kind. Fit the colour to the contour and two channels say one thing.
- Scale reference: one frontal, level word states where the surface is, so every lean and overlap reads as a departure from it. Tilt the word and nothing is at rest.
Colour relations: ground the largest share, one value; line and tone lowest in chroma, carrying all the modelling; one chromatic family as flat pieces (10–25%), highest in chroma and never modelled; darkest value 4–20%; mid texture 3–13%; a second hue only as a faint tint, never an accent. Either every value sits inside the middle 40–45 L, or ground and darkest stand 65–80 L apart; in both the sharpest contrast has the smallest area.
Space and line: one cluster, core to rim 1.5–4 : 1; details 1/4–1/3 of the view apart; outlines half closed; hard edge to fade 1 : 5; pieces 2–10° off axis, one to about 15°; deck of 3–5; parallel lines in sets of 3–5.
Type requirement: sturdy capitals that stay recognisable when partly covered and when their ink varies; two sizes about 3 : 1 plus a texture size; loose spacing; always frontal and level, at the edge of the cluster. Never cover a value, a label or an action.
Motion and interaction: the layers move separately and the level word never moves. The line layer appears first, the pieces arrive in deck order and settle out of register (400–600ms, ease-out, no bounce); on scroll the colour layer drifts slightly against the line, which flips as an edge passes under it. Selection closes a thing's open side; nothing gains a shadow. Hit areas follow the line layer, ≥44px. Reduced motion: cross-fade to the final state.
Fails when: the view is dense (tables, long text), so the ground cannot stay half open; things have no diagnostic detail (uniform rows, abstract metrics); many equal peers each need a cluster; colour must fit a shape to mean something (status chips, chart fills); several accent hues are required.
Signature (do not carry together): strips of printed wood grain or newsprint pasted beside a charcoal drawing · stencilled café and music words cut off mid-word · an ochre-grey field of small leaning facets with violin scrolls and f-holes.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Airline trip screen, phone 390 × 844, deep ink ground with a brand magenta, a rounded geometric sans, crisp and digital (the works are warm, matt, hand-cut and near-monochrome).
1. *Ground and cluster.* The ink ground (L 14) stays 61% uncovered. The trip is one cluster about 300px wide around (0.5, 0.45); the outer 36px rim holds only the level word. Rules and labels are 8px apart in the core and 24px at the edge of the cluster.
2. *Neutral layer.* Departure, flight and arrival are drawn in one grey a step off the ground's own cast, 1.5px lines on the axes. Each region has a hard rule on its left (tone drops 30 L within 6px) and fades back to the ground over 30px on the right; there is no right border. The tone is deepest where two regions meet.
3. *Details.* Four things stay whole and exact: gate, seat, boarding time, the scannable code. They sit 100–130px apart (1/4–1/3 of the width). Fare rules, baggage and terms get no outline at all.
4. *Two views.* The seat is shown twice on the same plane: the row from the side on the time rail and the cabin from above beside it, both frontal, both carrying the same seat number.
5. *Colour layer.* Three flat pieces, 29% of the screen: a magenta strip (17%) for the boarding state, a near-black piece (6%), and the fare conditions set at 9px as a mid-value texture block (6%). The magenta strip stands beside the time rail, overlaps its left 24px, fits no outline and leans 4°. No shadows.
6. *Crossing.* The route line runs over the magenta strip and the near-black piece on one path: grey on the ground, ink on magenta, white on near-black.
7. *Level word.* The destination in capitals, 52px (6.2% of the height), level, at the upper right of the cluster in the open zone; the magenta strip covers its last two letters. The full name is in the departure line and in the accessible label. Times are 18px. Nothing else is set between those sizes.
8. *Contrast and the literal thing.* White on near-black appears once, on the gate, about 1.5% of the screen. The "show code" button is the only element with a real cast shadow and a lit edge.
- *Motion.* The grey drawing appears first; the three pieces arrive in deck order over 500ms and settle out of register; on scroll the magenta strip drifts 6px against the rail and the route line flips as its edge passes. Reduced motion cross-fades.

Result: a dark, magenta, rounded-sans travel screen with no paper, no grain and no facets. What survived is line, colour and lettering as separate layers that only overlap, a few whole details in an open body, two frontal views of one thing, a level word as the measure, and the strongest contrast on the smallest area.

## Space & composition
- **Formats:** tall 1 : 1.4 to 1 : 2.1 for figure and violin stacks; landscape for table views and papier collés; oval table drawn inside a rectangle (65–90% of width). The oval is cut by the sheet and by pieces.
- **Stack, don't recede:** things overlap upward (Houses, Violin and Palette); no horizon, no vanishing point. Depth is an ordered deck of about 3 to 5 sheets.
- **Centre cluster, bare rim:** density ratio 1.2–3.9 (above); top corners L 70–80 in painted works, 87–91 on paper.
- **Spine:** a vertical near 0.39–0.54 (Woman with a Guitar bar; Portuguese at 0.49), slightly left or right of centre.
- **Tilt:** main pieces 2–10° off axis, one piece 15° (headline), maximum 47° (Gillette plank); the sheet itself is never turned.
- **Solid air:** the gaps between objects get the same dabs, planes and hues as the objects (Candlestick: object and ground clusters overlap in L and hue).
- **Repeats:** oval, scroll, comb-fringe, f-hole or grape repeated 2–6 times, at unequal sizes.

## Color
The measured hexes are for strict studies only. The relations that hold: one hue (ochre to brown, 70–95°) over a full value scale, plus black and paper white; chroma kept to C ≤ 15 for drawing and air, and to C 44–54 for the one material; cool tones appear as a greenish grey tint and never as an accent. Colour does not model form: tone (charcoal) models, colour sits flat.

Notes on scans: the Portuguese and Woman with a Guitar scans have a yellow or green cast (median C 25–26; Tate's museum image of a sister work reads C 8). Papers are aged: newsprint is tan (C 23–40) that was once grey; blank papers are cream (C 10–20); in Tenora whites are greenish from a colour plate. Houses is darkened by varnish or print.

## Form, line & typography
- **Contour:** heavy, dark, on the shadow side of about half the form; light or absent on the other side. Line weight changes within one stroke; the same arc may be dark, then white.
- **Edge inventory:** cut (pasted paper, knife-hard), charcoal hard-to-soft, painted dabs (soft patch), comb or printed grain, stencil bridge.
- **Dab:** short horizontal brick strokes (10–25 px in an 824 px width, ≈1–3% of the width), patch by patch, always the same way; they keep the surface one fabric.
- **Letters:** stencil serif capitals with bridges (Portuguese, Bach) at 5–8% of picture height, second size 2.2%; drawn capitals in charcoal (BAR, ALE); found newspaper type in heavy slab or fat-face, several times its small text (by eye 4–10). Spacing loose and uneven (B, A, L at unequal gaps), baseline level while the rest tilts; after 1912 one letter may drop or turn (E of ALE by about 30°). Words sit at the edges or just off centre, never in the dark core, and are cut by a plane or the page in most works.
- **Text/image share:** words cover 0.5–5% of the surface (Portuguese ≈2–3% by eye; Bach ≈1%), newsprint as texture 3–13%.
- **Type requirement (observed traits only):** frontal capitals, high stroke contrast or slab, with stencil gaps; a second role of tiny printed text.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- **Reading path:** the eye enters at the sharpest, smallest dark mark (nail, "BACH", scroll, black paper), steps to the next whole token, and fills the body between. Tokens are spaced roughly 1/4 to 1/3 of the picture apart, never adjacent.
- **Time:** several views appear at once, side by side, not in a sequence; a thing is drawn, then crossed by paper, then drawn over: traces of order remain visible (Tenora's clarinet under and over the plank).
- **Rhythm:** parallel strings, stave lines, string-like strokes in sets of 3–5 with equal spacing inside a set and unequal spacing between sets; steps in the cut black edge; grain waves.
- **Pace:** quick small facets in the core, slow plain zones at the rim; 1.2–3.9 to 1.

## UI translations
*For strict studies and homage. A kit takes "Process (derived)" and its own surface.*

1. **Colour beside, not inside.** Give a card or illustration its colour as a flat piece that overlaps the drawn content by a part of its width and fits no outline; all pieces together cover 25–35% of the view.
2. **Three material roles.** One chromatic family, one darkest value, one mid-value texture; 3–6 pieces in all, the largest about three times the others.
3. **Half-closed regions.** Group with one hard edge and a fade about five times as wide on the other side; no full border, no card outline.
4. **Tone at the joint.** Darken both sides of the seam between two regions by 20–30 L instead of dropping a shadow under either.
5. **Whole details, open body.** Keep 2–4 identifying details of an item crisp and complete (a numeral, a glyph, a control) a quarter to a third of the view apart, and leave the rest unboxed.
6. **Two frontal views.** Show the plan and the side of one thing on one plane, sharing a detail, instead of a perspective render or a carousel.
7. **A line that inverts.** Let a rule, route or underline run across layer edges on one path, dark on light and light on dark.
8. **A level word as datum.** One heading in sturdy capitals at 5–8% of the view's height, frontal and level at the edge of the cluster; a second size about a third of it; it never tilts with the pieces.
9. **Text as texture and text as word.** Fine print at mid value and a tenth to a quarter of the heading size reads as a grey plane; only the heading is meant to be read at a glance.
10. **Bare rim.** Keep 55–70% of the ground open and the core 1.5–4 times denser than the border.
11. **Small lean.** Pieces sit 2–10° off axis, one piece up to about 15°; text and the drawn scaffold stay on the axes.
12. **One literal thing.** Exactly one small element is rendered with a real light and cast shadow; everything else is flat.
13. **Deck order in motion.** Layers enter in stacking order and settle out of register (400–600ms, ease-out); reduced motion cross-fades.

## What this artist would reject
- Local colour filling an outline, or a hue per object.
- A closed contour all the way round a thing, with a shadow below it.
- One vanishing point, a horizon, a single light.
- Type that is tilted, foreshortened or scaled to act as decoration, or type that fills the middle.
- Full coverage: no bare ground at the rim and no blank sheet inside the subject.
- Ornamental texture all over; the grain is used once or twice and it means wood.
- A perfectly centred, symmetrical stack with no leaning thing.

## Signature prompt seed
Keep line, colour and lettering as three layers that only overlap. Leave 55–70% of one ground open and gather the subject into a single cluster, densest at its core, with a bare rim. Draw everything in a neutral line and tone, each region closed on half its outline and fading on the other side, tone deepest at the joints. Keep two to four identifying details of each thing whole and small, and leave its body open. Lay three to six flat pieces of colour (25–35% in all, one chromatic family, the darkest value, a mid texture) beside the drawing, a few degrees out of register, and let the line cross them and invert. Set one level word in sturdy capitals at the edge of the cluster. Give the strongest contrast to the smallest area and render exactly one small thing literally.
