# Pablo Picasso — Cubism

## Essence
Picasso takes one known thing (a seated player, a bottle, a guitar), breaks it into parts seen from different positions, and sets the parts back in order on a shallow, mostly upright scaffold. The thing stays the subject, and it is found again through a few small, fully legible signs (a moustache, a watch chain, strings, a label, a truncated word). In the analytic works (1909–12) the parts are small shaded planes, hard on one edge and open on the other, in one brown-grey family. In the synthetic works (1912–21) they are a few large, flat, cut pieces, each of one material or one colour, and each piece can stand for more than one thing.

## Key works examined
- **The Reservoir, Horta de Ebro, 1909 (oil on canvas, MoMA, New York; about 60 × 50 cm).** One mass of houses locked by shared edges. It teaches one line serving several objects, and planes with two possible depths. *(visually checked: WikiArt, 1667 × 2000; palette, edge angles, hinge line, edge asymmetry measured)*
- **Girl with a Mandolin (Fanny Tellier), early 1910 (oil on canvas, 100.3 × 73.6 cm, MoMA).** Graduated shallow facets on a figure still whole. It teaches one shadow-only edge per plane and parts turned square to the frame. *(visually checked: Wikimedia Commons / en.wikipedia scan 1280 × 1770, plus WikiArt; palette, hue spread, edge angles, edge asymmetry measured)*
- **Portrait of Ambroise Vollard, 1910 (oil on canvas, about 93 × 66 cm, Pushkin Museum, Moscow).** The same fracturing everywhere, but only the face is warm. It teaches focus by chroma, not by detail. *(visually checked: WikiArt 746 × 1088; palette of whole, face and body)*
- **Portrait of Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler, 1910 (oil on canvas, 100.4 × 72.4 cm, Art Institute of Chicago 1948.561).** A stepped grid from which hair waves, a nose, a watch chain and clasped hands surface. It teaches the recognisable sign as anchor. *(visually checked: WikiArt 780 × 1088 and a 551 × 768 copy; palette, sign sizes and positions, angles)*
- **The Accordionist, summer 1911 (oil on canvas, 130.2 × 89.5 cm, Guggenheim 37.537).** A tall triangle of overlapping planes, dense at the axis, fading to bare canvas at the rim. *(visually checked: Guggenheim web image 331 × 490 and WikiArt 744 × 1088; palette, edge angles)*
- **"Ma Jolie" (Woman with a Zither or Guitar), winter 1911–12 (oil on canvas, 100 × 64.5 cm, MoMA).** The near-unreadable figure with a two-word caption painted at the foot. It teaches flat letters as the fixed surface against which everything else becomes space. *(visually checked: WikiArt 809 × 1258, a greenish reproduction; letter sizes and edge angles; hues not trusted)*
- **Still Life with Chair Caning, May 1912 (oil and printed oilcloth on canvas edged with rope, 29 × 37 cm, Musée Picasso MP 36).** Printed imitation caning, painted shards, cut-off word JOU, rope rim. It teaches three levels of reality on one surface. *(visually checked: WikiArt 1043 × 796 and pablopicasso.org 900 × 701; ellipse ratio, area shares)*
- **Guitar, sheet metal and wire, 1912–14 (77.5 × 35 × 19.3 cm, MoMA 94.1971).** The body cut open, and the hole built as a projecting tube. It teaches void and solid changing places. *(visually checked: pablopicasso.org 700 × 1090; proportions, value range)*
- **Guitar, Sheet Music, and Glass, after 18 November 1912 (pasted papers, charcoal, gouache on paperboard, 47.9 × 36.5 cm, McNay Art Museum).** Seven pieces on a wallpaper ground; nobody draws the guitar. It teaches a whole implied by the placing of unlike parts. *(visually checked: WikiArt 790 × 1087; piece areas, alignments, palette)*
- **Glass and Bottle of Suze, 1912 (pasted papers, gouache, charcoal, about 65 × 50 cm, Kemper Art Museum, St. Louis).** A bottle assembled on an oval table inside a ring of newsprint. It teaches text as tone, one label as text. *(visually checked: WikiArt 815 × 1070; palette, piece areas)*
- **Bottle, Glass and Violin, 1912–13 (printed paper, charcoal and pencil on paper, 47 × 62 cm, Moderna Museet NM 6083).** Six pasted pieces on a mostly empty sheet, drawing laid over them. It teaches sparing material against a large quiet field. *(visually checked: Moderna Museet 1200 × 938; pasted-area share, palette)*
- **Head (Tête), 1913–14 (pasted papers, gouache, charcoal on paperboard, 43.5 × 33 cm, Scottish National Gallery of Modern Art).** A head from one triangle, one strip, one arc. It teaches the smallest set of signs that still reads. *(visually checked: Wikimedia 450 × 591 and WikiArt; palette, geometry)*
- **Card Player, winter 1913–14 (oil on canvas, 108 × 89.5 cm, MoMA).** Papier collé logic painted in oil. *(visually checked: Wikimedia 1280 × 1552; palette, edge angles)*
- **Student with a Newspaper, 1913–14 (plaster, oil, Conté, sand on canvas, 73 × 59.7 cm, Metropolitan Museum).** Dots, wood grain and stripes as three fillings for flat planes. *(visually checked: WikiArt 734 × 920; palette)*
- **Three Musicians, summer 1921 (oil on canvas, 200.7 × 222.9 cm, MoMA; second version 204.5 × 188.3 cm, Philadelphia).** Three full-size figures from interleaved flat colour. It teaches colour ranked from rim to centre, and one shape shared by two figures. *(visually checked: MoMA version, Wikimedia 2000 × 1800; Philadelphia version, museum IIIF 1200 × 1288; colour areas, components, positions, edge angles)*
- **Bottle of Vieux Marc, Glass, Guitar and Newspaper, 1913; Violin, 1912.** *(visually checked: WikiArt, supporting evidence only)*

## Detailed observations

**The Reservoir (1909).** Roughly two dozen house blocks climb from the bottom corners to one tower at about (0.54, 0.09) of the canvas: a pyramid whose base is the full width. Blocks overlap with no gap, so each block's edge is the neighbour's edge. The vertical between the tower's sunny and shaded faces (x ≈ 0.54) carries on down as the edge of three lower buildings; measured on the scan, the strongest vertical edge stays within x 0.545–0.55 from 25% to 40% of the height, after the tower's line at the same place. One edge, four objects, about a third of the height. Rubin adds that the sunny side of the tower is exactly as wide as the roof it touches. The light obeys no sun: one block has its ochre face on the left and grey on the right, the next one the reverse. Planes are one of two states, ochre (C ≈ 22+, 22% of the area) or neutral grey (C < 14, 60%), with a rust third state, and only one green patch (2.1%) in the pool. A plane that starts crisp at its ridge melts downward into the next (the sunlit wall of the middle house runs into the ground with no line). The roof in front of the tower widens away from the viewer, so it reads both as receding and as folding out. The sky is brushed in blocks, so it belongs to the same wall.

**Girl with a Mandolin (1910).** The figure fills the height, with head at 12% and hand at 78–85%. Two parts are still round: the right breast (a half-cylinder around (0.45, 0.56)) and the mandolin bowl. Everything else is a plate turned toward the picture plane. The face is a plain near-white trapezium leaning about 14° off vertical, with a scalloped edge of hair at the top and one almond eye; no mouth, hardly a nose. The grey plane right of the face is the shaded side of the same head, swung flat. The mandolin neck runs up right at about 59°, the single long diagonal, from the bowl to the raised hand at (0.93, 0.35). Behind, three stacked picture frames keep the verticals and horizontals and stop the space. Edges are one-sided: on the 3% strongest edges the step is 35 L, the dark side recovers 17 L within 4% of the canvas width, and the light side falls only 3 L. So every facet is a light plate with a soft shadow falling off it. Colour is nearly absent: mean C 8, 95th percentile 17, all hues pooled at about 83° (resultant 0.99); the sixth of the canvas above L 75 sits on face, shoulder, breast and forearm and leads the eye down that chain.

**Ambroise Vollard (1910).** A bulky seated figure, axis at centre. The forehead is a tall dome at (0.50, 0.12), about 27% of the width, and head plus beard take about 38% of the height. The mesh of facets is the same size in face, coat and background; nothing is smoother because it matters more. Focus is colour: the face carries C 19–39 (`#B5744F`, `#E1B585`) and the body and ground C 5–11 (`#584A3E`, `#857B69`). Warm skin is about 4% of the canvas. The second bright spot is a white wedge of shirt and folded paper below the chin at (0.50, 0.60), then a bottle top at upper left. Eyelids are down, one eye is a plain shadow. Long diagonals (from chin to lower left, from shoulder to lower right) all start and end on other facets; none is a contour of a body part from end to end.

**Daniel-Henry Kahnweiler (1910).** Plane fragments step in and out of a dark brown field with edges mostly horizontal (21%) and vertical (16%) and few diagonals (22%, against 33% by chance). Legible signs, each tiny: two sets of parallel wave lines for hair (top, x 0.48–0.78), a straight nose wedge, two eye notches, a short mouth arc, one watch chain of two mirrored hooks at (0.56, 0.66), about 0.3% of the canvas, and folded hands at (0.53, 0.91), 2.6%. They sit on one vertical axis at x ≈ 0.53–0.58, so the eye drops head, mouth, chain, hands and rebuilds a seated man. A bottle still life sits at lower left, a masked shape at upper left; both are drawn at the figure's own scale of facet. Face chroma reaches 29 in ochre facets, the rest is at 15 or so; hands measure L 58–73, among the lightest areas of the lower third. Edge of forehead: a kite rotated roughly 45°, its corner pointing up, over a cubic head turned another way: front and three-quarter views in one outline.

**The Accordionist (1911).** A tall triangle with its apex at the top edge (x ≈ 0.58), sides running down to the lower corners. Overlap and darkness are greatest along the axis and in the lower third; the margins are loose, warm, unmodelled dabs and bare canvas. Vertical edges 21%, diagonals 24%. Colour is C 13 on average, L 55, no hue outside one warm family (resultant 0.97). Two things are nearly clear: pleats of the accordion as a run of parallel bars around (0.55, 0.60), and the spiral ends of a chair at (0.28, 0.93) and (0.82, 0.78). Circular dots and short brackets (buttons, keys) are scattered as marks on the surface. The brush is squarish touches laid in rows, which makes tone flicker and lets neighbouring planes share paint.

**"Ma Jolie" (1911–12).** Planes are flat and frontal. The stack is a triangle: narrow column at the top centre (x 0.43–0.61, the head) widening to a base that spans x 0.13–0.75 at 90% of the height. Verticals 21%, diagonals 22%. Planes toward the centre are more frequent and more opaque; at upper sides the brush is long and thin. A dark bar of shadow on one side of each plane and a light on the other, flipped from neighbour to neighbour; edge step 37 L, dark side recovers 11 L, light falls 16 L. Readable bits: six verticals for strings at (0.49, 0.62), four fingertips at (0.82, 0.80), a treble clef on a stub of staff at (0.65, 0.91), bracket-like tuning marks. The caption: MA then JOLIE at the bottom, x 0.31–0.58 (27% of the width), caps about 4% of the canvas height and the last e lower case, baseline within 3% of the bottom edge, the two words not on one line (JOLIE sits a cap's third lower). Stencilled-looking black letters (L < 12) with no shading, ragged by the plane boundary that passes through M. They read flat on the surface, so the planes fall behind.

**Still Life with Chair Caning (1912).** A horizontal oval, rope outer ratio 1.30 (published 29 × 37: 1.28), framed by one continuous twisted rope (saturated orange-brown `#B05B0D`, L 48, C 60, the only strong hue). The oilcloth imitation caning fills lower left, about 30% of the oval, a strict repeat of an octagon mesh over black holes, and stops at hard, slightly slanted cuts; two dark diagonal bars and the shadow of the glass cross it, so the mechanical pattern gets overpainted like a tablecloth. The right half and top are hand-painted: glass seen as base, bowl and rim in different views, pipe stem, slice of lemon, knife, and a shell-like white fan. JOU at upper left, letters 120–130 px of a 690 px inner height (18%), three caps stepping down about 15 px to the right, the first on clear ground, the last blurred by a stroke. Everything is a partial view; the rope is at once a frame, a table moulding and a horizontal edge. Colour under the rope is C 5–20, so the printed pattern outweighs the painting in contrast.

**Guitar, sheet metal (1912–14).** Height to width 2.39 in the photo, 2.21 in the published size (camera foreshortening). Neck is a vertical gutter 27% of the body width and the front of the neck is missing: four wires stand for it. The back panel is an upright rectangle, the left body curve a projecting sheet, the right curve a wrap-over, and the front plate is one L-shaped cut that covers only the lower edge and right flank. The soundhole, 22% of the body's width, is a tube that comes forward from the depth, at the lower end of the neck, 58% of the height; its rim is the only curve with full closure. The hole is the solid and the body is the void. Material is single: every plane reads `#5A4739`–`#1F1813`, L 7–35, C 5–16. Depth is entirely by shadow. Edges are raw; the wires that stitch parts are visible as short stitches around the hole. Depth is 25% of height.

**Guitar, Sheet Music, and Glass (1912).** Ground: diamond lattice wallpaper with a rose repeat, a full-page pattern (about 50% of the sheet stays visible). Pieces, share of the sheet: blue trapezium 13% (9–10% visible), drawing on white paper 11%, sheet music 8%, newspaper strip 7%, faux-bois guitar flank 5.5%, black crescent 3.9%, white disc 1.8%. Total about 50%. No outline of a guitar; it is implied by the wood flank on the left, the blue plane as neck and front, the disc as sound hole, the crescent as bottom rim and the sheet's right edge as the far side. The pieces touch at shared joints: sheet music and drawing abut at y 0.49; the disc's centre (x 0.49, y 0.56) sits on the bottom edge of the blue plane; the crescent's top edge lines up under the wood piece. Each piece is one material and one fact: printed, drawn, imitated, plain. The glass at right is not cut: it is a charcoal cubist drawing pasted in as a scrap, so one drawn picture is inside a picture made from real paper. Text: LE JOU, the cap height about 5% of the sheet, cut at the right edge through the last letter, then LA BATAILLE S'EST ENGAGÉ, cropped. Music is printed at small size; its lyric is broken at "bel".

**Glass and Bottle of Suze (1912).** An oval blue table (x 0.16–0.73, y 0.27–0.71) is overlaid by a bottle: a tall white cut form with a dark half behind it (black 5.5%, cream 4.5%). The real label SUZE, rotated about 8° counter-clockwise, is on the bottle's belly at (0.55, 0.47); its caps are about 3% of the sheet height, larger and blacker than any newsprint. Newsprint surrounds on three sides, pieces cut as rough strips and turned to different angles and some inverted, so columns become hatching. Newsprint, kraft and wallpaper together cover more than half the surface (about 56% in the reproduction). A serrated black strip at (0.58, 0.85) and scallops at the bottle's and glass's inner edges repeat one cut contour, positive and negative: cut from paper on one side, left as ground on the other. The table's lower half is missing, its place taken by a brown panel with a semicircular notch. Wallpaper lattice sits only in the two lower corners. The palette is small: warm newsprint and kraft, one blue, black, cream, grey-green.

**Bottle, Glass and Violin (1912–13).** About 77–82% of the sheet is empty cream. Pasted area is 18%: glass panel 5.7%, violin upper 3.7%, bottle 3.4%, brown rectangle 2.3%, wood-grain flank 1.7%, plus the strip JOURNAL on an incline of about 14°. Newsprint columns in the bottle are turned 90° and read as grain. The violin's scroll and strings are drawn over the pasted paper; the body is bare outline. The wood-grain piece is the violin's right curve only: one cut contour with two lobes. Nothing overlaps except at the violin and glass. The empty left middle is the rest.

**Head (1913–14).** One white triangle (apex (0.50, 0.04), base about 75% of the height) divided by a slanting ground-coloured strip, so it is two white blades. A blue-grey strip with a charcoal line and one circle for the eye, at 15° from vertical, carries the face; a freehand arc joins the top of the strip to the right blade and reads as the back of a head. Base: one T of black paper, a piece of yellow. Ground is brown board, 50–67% of the area. Fewer than ten parts. Each part is either straight or one arc.

**Card Player (1913–14).** Painted as if cut: black and charcoal planes (L < 23) take 60%, yellow wood-grain 15%, blue 2.7%, red one patch at 0.4% and near the hands at (0.62, 0.65). Edges: vertical 29%, horizontal 17%, diagonals 12%. Face: one white plate, one dark plate, an I-shaped nose. Three white cards, JO letters, a Greek key frieze and panel moulding as surface fillings. Stippled edges show the shadow of a paper lifted.

**Student with a Newspaper (1913–14).** Face is a tilted rectangle filled with red-brown dots; hand or body is filled with violet dots; hat is one flat brown cloud; wood grain in one patch; newspaper at the base reads URNAL in five jumbled grey caps at slightly different baselines. 60% of the canvas is off-white (L 75–87). Each filling names a plane by texture and is bounded by a drawn edge.

**Three Musicians (MoMA).** A shallow box: brown floor, walls and ceiling in four brown values (L 12–28); horizon higher at left. Figures stand almost side by side across 90% of the width, overlapping toward the centre, masks at the same height band (0.08–0.30). Colour shares: black 19%, cream 8.6%, blue 8.2%, red 1.5%, yellow 1.1% (one piece, the guitar; the dull diamonds fall below the threshold) (the brown ground is the rest). Blue is ten patches from x 0.26 to 0.76 reused on three bodies, its biggest (4.2%) running without a break from Harlequin's masked face into Pierrot's torso. Blue is in front of Pierrot's white above the table and behind it below. Cream spreads more (x 0.17–0.78). Red and yellow are packed into Harlequin's column, x 0.46–0.67, with diamonds reversing each other. Black hits the far left (dog) and the right (monk). Hands are tiny, each about 3–4% of the canvas width. Edges: vertical 28%, horizontal 25%, diagonals 13%. Step 42 L at the edges, and dark sides recover only 2 L: flat paint. The Philadelphia version is cooler (blue-grey table glow and crosshatched olive wall, green `#656846` 13%, red 8%) and the table runs under all three; diamonds are again only on Harlequin.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **One known subject, kept.** Every work is a player, a portrait, or a table with bottle, glass and instrument. Rubin separates Picasso from Braque on this point: Braque starts with the spaces and the frame, Picasso with the thing itself. Titles and captions name it (Ma Jolie, JOU, SUZE).
- **Upright scaffold, diagonals rationed.** Verticals and horizontals (within 10°) together hold 27–38% of strong edges in 1909–11 works (Reservoir V 29, H 9; Girl V 15, H 12; Kahnweiler V 16, H 21; "Ma Jolie" V 21, H 14) against 22% by chance, and diagonals 22–28%, below the 33% of chance. In the synthetic works the scaffold hardens: 40–53% V+H and 12–17% diagonals in most (Suze 40/17; Head and Guitar, Sheet Music, and Glass stay at 32–36 with 22–24) (Bottle, Glass and Violin 45/15; Three Musicians 53/13; Card Player 46/12). One leading long diagonal carries the subject where needed (the mandolin neck at 59°).
- **Pyramid, dense at the axis and open at the rim.** Reservoir's houses, Girl's torso, Accordionist and "Ma Jolie" widen downward from a narrow top; overlap and opacity rise toward the centre and fade to bare ground. Three Musicians repeats it with the sizes of shapes: large brown at the rim, small busy overlap at the centre.
- **One-sided edge (passage).** Across six 1909–12 works the strongest edges have a 25–37 L step, then the dark side recovers 10–17 L within 4% of the width while the light side falls 3–16 L. Each plane is closed on one or two sides and left open on another, where it merges with its neighbour. In Reservoir one line is the edge of four objects. In Three Musicians the soft side is gone (2 L): shared contours do the passage instead, by overlap order that changes (blue in front, then behind).
- **Two states of light, no sun.** Reservoir ochre (22%) and grey (60%) alternate without a source; "Ma Jolie" and Accordionist give each plane its own dark side; light seems to come from inside (Rubin). In the metal Guitar the light is real and does the same job with one value range (L 7–35).
- **Several views put flat on one page.** Girl's face is frontal and shaded three-quarter at once; Kahnweiler's forehead kite and square head disagree in direction; Chair Caning's glass shows rim, bowl and base in different views; the metal Guitar shows back, side, top and hole at once. Views are turned toward parallel with the page (Girl: the shaded side of the head is swung into the plane).
- **Small legible signs on one axis.** Kahnweiler: hair waves, nose, mouth arc, chain (0.3%), hands (2.6%) along x ≈ 0.53–0.58. Accordionist: pleats and volutes. "Ma Jolie": strings, fingertips, clef, letters. Musicians: pipe, score, tiny hands. The reading is done by these, not by outlines.
- **Local colour restricted by kind.** Analytic works carry mean C 8–25 and a single hue pooled at resultant 0.89–0.99; there is no second hue. Vollard concentrates warm colour in 4% of the area. Synthetic works use few coloured papers (blue 9% in Guitar, Sheet Music, and Glass; blue 5% and black 5.5% in Suze; a red at 0.4% in Card Player).
- **Each piece is a material that says something.** Papers are used as themselves and as something else: newsprint is tone, grain, background, and also "newspaper"; wallpaper is wall and tablecloth; faux wood is a guitar flank; oilcloth is chair seat. Printed imitation and painted passage meet at a hard edge (Chair Caning), and the difference in kind of making is the event. Letters and pattern are the surface; modelled shards are the depth.
- **Text is cut, and lies flat.** JOU, LE JOU, URNAL, MA JOLIE, SUZE. Words are truncated by an edge, stepped on uneven baselines, stencilled or pasted, unshaded; the size step between a masthead or label and body text is wide (in Suze, caps about 3% of the sheet against type under 0.5%). Only one or two words per work can be read at a glance.
- **Void and solid swap.** The sound hole is a tube (metal Guitar), a disc (pasted Guitar, Sheet Music, and Glass); a missing fingerboard is given by strings; the table front is a notch (Suze); scallops of one cut are the counter-scallops of the next.
- **One shape, two owners.** The largest blue in Three Musicians is Harlequin's face and Pierrot's body in one shape; the crescent in Guitar, Sheet Music, and Glass is both rim of guitar and shadow; in MoMA's Head (1913, not viewed) Rubin reads one shape as both ear and sound hole.
- **Fading, not framing.** Margins thin (Accordionist, Girl); the oval (Chair Caning) and the oval table (Suze) remove corners; only Three Musicians ends at a wall box.
- **Sequence of work.** In the papiers collés the drawing comes first, then papers are cut for particular planes, and lines that got covered are drawn again on top (Rubin, Man with a Hat). Pieces stay few: 6 in Bottle, Glass and Violin, 7 in Guitar, Sheet Music, and Glass.
- **The generative rule:** take one named subject; split it into views or parts; turn each toward the page; lay them on a vertical-horizontal scaffold that narrows upward and fades at the rim; close each part on one side only and let it run into its neighbour on the other; keep the light on one family; place a handful of tiny exact signs on one axis so the whole can be found; then, in the later phase, swap the shaded parts for few large pieces, each a different kind of material, whose edges mean the thing.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Pablo Picasso.** *Start from one named thing: split it into its views and parts, turn each flat to the surface and butt them at shared edges on an upright stack that thickens toward one axis; string a few tiny exact signs down that axis so the whole can be found again, and let each part's kind of making, not an outline, say what it is.*
Process:
1. Name one subject per view and write the name where it is read first: one or two words, flat and unshaded, at the foot or the head, cap height 3–5% of the view's height. Everything else in the view is a part or a view of that subject. If a second subject is needed, it is drawn at the same grain and kept off the axis.
2. Split the subject into 6–10 parts or views (its front, its side, its inside, its state over time) and turn every one square to the surface. Nothing recedes, and there are 2–4 depth levels at most. Two views of the same part sit side by side as neighbours, not on separate screens.
3. Stack the parts on an upright scaffold: 40–55% of edges level or plumb, slanted edges under a sixth, plus at most one long diagonal (about 60°) for the subject's main direction. The stack is narrow at the top and wide at the base, with its axis 0.50–0.60 across. Overlap and opacity are highest on the axis and thin to bare ground at the rim; 50–80% of the view stays quiet.
4. Join without gutters: neighbours share an edge, and at least one edge runs on as the boundary of three or four parts over about a third of the height. Close each part on one or two sides with a value step of 25–40 L and leave another side open, returning 10–17 L toward the neighbour within 4% of the width. In a flat-fill system, replace the fade by a change of order: the same part passes in front at one joint and behind at the next. The dark side alternates from part to part; there is no common light.
5. Set 4–6 small exact signs on the axis, within a band about 5% of the width, in the subject's own order from top to bottom. Each takes 0.3–3% of the view and is the most literal thing near it. They do the recognising, so no outline is drawn round the whole.
6. Give each part one kind of making and one fact: set text, a drawn line, a regular pattern, a plain fill, a ready-made insert. Sizes spread about 1 : 7 (2% to 13% of the view). Two kinds meet at a hard edge, and marks drawn afterwards may run across that edge. Body text turned into a part is tone only.
7. Exchange solid and empty at least once: state one part by its absence (a notch of ground where it would be) and make one opening the only fully closed shape. Let the largest piece of the second colour belong to two neighbouring parts as one unbroken shape.
8. Budget contrast by position. One family carries the whole view by value, with the same grain of detail everywhere; the focus gets chroma, not detail: about 4% of the view at two to four times the chroma of the rest. With flat fills: the darkest value and the ground hold the rim, one second colour takes 8–10% as several patches reused across the parts, and the strongest colours take 1–3%, packed into the axis column (about a fifth of the width).
Why it works:
- Recognition by diagnostic parts, grouped by a common axis: a thing is known from a few exact details, and details in line are read as one chain in order. Scatter them and each is a separate puzzle; enlarge them and they become the picture; remove them and the stack is a pattern that cannot be walked back to a thing.
- Figure and ground at a shared edge: an edge that belongs to two parts lets neither sit in front, so several views hold together as one mass on one plane. Give every part its own closed outline and a gutter, and the views become a contact sheet of separate pictures.
- Salience by oddness: with the grain equal everywhere, a small change of chroma is the only odd thing, so the focus is found without size or border. Add detail at the focus as well and the rest reads as unfinished background.
- Grouping by likeness of texture: parts made in different ways separate with no line between them, and each way of making carries a claim about what kind of thing the part is. Render all parts the same way and outlines must come back to tell them apart.
- Scale reference: flat, unmodelled letters state where the surface is, so every value step behind them reads as depth. Shade or bend the word and nothing marks the surface.
Colour relations: one family at low chroma holds 90% or more, spread wide in value (three ranks: lit, middle, dark side); focus chroma 2–4 times the mean on about 4%. Or, in flat fills: ground the largest share, darkest value 20–60% at the rim, a light neutral about 9%, one second colour 8–10% in several patches, the strongest colours 1–3% on the axis. The colour most unlike the rest is always the smallest and the most central.
Space and line: one stack, apex at the top, axis 0.50–0.60 across; quiet share 50–80%; 6–10 parts sized 1 : 7; no gutters; one-sided closure, open side back 10–17 L within 4% of the width; level and plumb 40–55%, slants under a sixth, one long diagonal; signs 0.3–3% each on a 5% band; three line roles (heavy on the dark side, thin and carried past its part, parallel runs as texture).
Type requirement: heavy, plain capitals that hold as a flat silhouette at 3–5% of the height; at most two words per view meant to be read at a glance; the next size at least six times smaller and set tight enough to read as tone. The named words stay level, and a part edge may crop one only where the full name is stated elsewhere. Nothing that must be read (a value, a label, an action) is ever used as tone.
Motion and interaction: the views are simultaneous, so nothing pages or flips. The signs appear first, in axis order; parts then attach from the axis outward and the rim comes last (400–600ms, ease-out, no bounce). Selecting a part closes its open side and moves the focus chroma to it (the 4% moves, it does not grow); a part may change its order at a joint. The name never moves. Signs are read, not pressed: targets are whole parts, ≥44px. Reduced motion: the assembled state, cross-faded.
Fails when: the view holds several equal subjects (lists, dashboards of peers), each needing its own axis; the subject has no diagnostic detail (uniform rows, abstract totals); long text or a table must be read, so the quiet share and the two-word limit cannot hold; a shared shape or an open side would misstate data (chart fills, form fields, status chips); many category colours are required; parts cannot touch because each scrolls or resizes alone.
Signature (do not carry together): a brown-grey field of small shaded planes · pasted newsprint, wallpaper and imitation wood grain with stencilled words cut off mid-word · guitars, bottles and masked players.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Picasso's procedure → a home-battery status screen, phone 390 × 844, pale mint ground with one violet, a soft rounded sans, calm and domestic (the works are brown, dense and hand-cut).
1. *Subject.* One battery. Its name, "HOME BATTERY", is set flat at the foot, caps 32px (3.8% of the height), baseline 26px from the bottom edge. Nothing on the screen is about anything else.
2. *Split.* Seven parts, all frontal: charge now (front), today's flow in and out (the side, over time), cell temperatures (inside), grid link (state), solar input, tonight's forecast, the charging schedule. No rendered battery, no perspective, three depth levels.
3. *Stack.* The axis is at x = 211px (0.54). The top part is 120px wide; the stack widens to 340px at 90% of the height. Every edge is level or plumb except one line at about 60°, the charge trend. Overlap is confined to a 110px column round the axis. 56% of the screen is bare mint.
4. *Joints.* No gaps. The right edge of the charge column runs on downward as the left edge of the forecast and of the schedule, 280px in all (a third of the height). Each part has a hard top and left edge (30 L against its neighbour) and returns 12 L toward the next part over 16px on the right, with no rule. The darker side alternates from part to part.
5. *Signs.* Five marks between x = 202 and 221: a bolt, the charge numeral, a temperature dot, a plug, a clock tick, top to bottom in the order energy travels. Each is about 32 × 32px (0.3%); the numeral is 0.9%.
6. *Kinds.* Charge is a plain fill; flow is a drawn line; cells are a regular dot pattern; the schedule is 11px set text read as a grey panel (the same times are given as labelled values when the part is opened); solar is the live figure. Parts run from 2% (grid link) to 13% (flow) of the screen. The trend line is drawn last and crosses three part edges.
7. *Exchange.* The uncharged remainder is not a grey track: it is a notch of bare ground cut into the column. The grid link is a closed ring, the only fully closed shape. The largest violet patch is at once the top of the charge column and the head of the solar part.
8. *Budget.* The mint family runs from L 92 to L 38 and takes 96%; violet covers 4%, at about three times the chroma of anything else, all of it within the 80px axis column. The dot pattern and the line weights are equally fine in every part.
- *Motion.* On load the five signs appear down the axis, then the parts attach outward over 500ms. Tapping "cells" closes its open right side and moves the violet from the charge to the warmest cell. Reduced motion shows the assembled state.

Result: a pale, soft utility screen with no brown, no tilt and nothing pasted. What survived is one named subject, its views flat and touching on one upright stack, a chain of exact signs on the axis, parts told apart by how they are made, and chroma spent only at the centre.

## Space & composition
- Shallow. Analytic works place everything within a relief that starts at the surface and goes back a hand's depth; nothing has a far distance (Reservoir's sky is brushed as another wall). Synthetic works lay pieces at 2–4 visible depths.
- Centre of mass on the vertical axis, 0.50–0.60 across; apex at the top edge (Reservoir 0.54, Accordionist 0.58, "Ma Jolie" 0.52).
- Overlap toward the centre, loose rim. In the papiers collés the quiet share is 50% (wallpaper) to 80% (Bottle, Glass and Violin).
- Edges meet at shared joints rather than at gaps; no gutters. Few parts are separated (Bottle, Glass and Violin is the exception, three islands).
- Reserve: ground paper, bare canvas or plain board is part of the figure (white of Head's blades, table notch in Suze).
- Absent: single vanishing point, continuous outline, a lamp, deep space, decorative frame (the rope is an exception that is a thing), equal spacing.

## Color
*The hex table below is a reference sample for strict studies and homage only. For design, use the colour relations in "Process (derived)".*

Analytic: a brown-grey family with a narrow hue, all of it warm (hue angle 64–92°), L spread wide (sd 16–20), C mean 8–25 and usually under 20. Value does the modelling; hue appears only as a warmer face (Vollard) or an ochre plane (Kahnweiler). Synthetic: a paper or brown ground, black and white in broad shapes, then one cool (blue, 8–10%) and small patches of red/yellow (1–3%). The hot colours stay near the centre; black and brown hold the rim.

## Form, line & typography
- Form is a plate: trapezium, kite, triangle, arc, rectangle. Curves are single arcs, one per object (hip of a guitar, bowl of a glass, spiral of a chair). In the papiers collés contours are cut, not drawn, and a little uneven; the same scallop or tooth repeats as a motif within a work (Suze: serrated strip, scallop on bottle and glass).
- Line is of three kinds: heavy black edge on the dark side of a plane; thin construction line carried past the plane it defines; repeated parallel strokes as texture (hair, strings, pleats, staff).
- Letters: slab or Clarendon-like black caps, stencil edges, sizes from about 3% to 18% of the height; few words, cropped, set on slants of 8–14° or stepped baselines. Printed body text is used as tint (angles turned 90° or 180°). Handwriting appears rarely.
- Drawn over paper: strings, hooks, staff and rim lines pass across the piece edges.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- Simultaneity instead of sequence: the turn of a head or the walk around a guitar is one still page. The eye moves by a chain of light plates (Girl) or by the axis of signs (Kahnweiler).
- Rhythm comes from alternating dark side and light side (edge step about 30 L, reversed plane to plane), from runs of parallel marks (pleats, strings, staff) and from the zigzag contour; repeats are never exact.
- Reading time is the subject's: first the mass, then a sign, then the object; a word or a label is read immediately and delays the rest. In Three Musicians the tiny hands and the score are found late.
- Nothing is drawn as moving; no motion lines. The only temporal element is revision (a visible stitch, a line redrawn over glued paper).

## UI translations
*For strict studies and homage. A kit takes "Process (derived)" and its own surface.*

1. **One subject per view.** A detail page shows one thing; every panel is a view or a part of it, and its name is the only large text.
2. **Views side by side, flat.** Show front, side, inside and state together on one plane instead of a carousel, tabs or a perspective render.
3. **Stack that widens downward.** Narrow at the top, wide at the base, axis at 0.50–0.60 of the width; overlap only near the axis and 50–80% of the ground left quiet.
4. **Shared edges, no gutters.** Panels butt; let one edge run on as the boundary of three or four panels for about a third of the height.
5. **One-sided closure.** A panel has a hard edge of 25–40 L on one or two sides and returns 10–17 L toward its neighbour within 4% of the width on another; no full border.
6. **Signs on the axis.** Put 4–6 small literal glyphs or figures (0.3–3% of the view each) in a band 5% wide down the axis, in the subject's own order.
7. **Focus by chroma.** Keep the same density of detail everywhere and give the focus about 4% of the view at two to four times the chroma of the rest.
8. **A kind of making per panel.** Set text, drawn line, regular pattern, plain fill, ready-made insert: one kind and one fact each, sizes from 2% to 13%.
9. **Text as tone, one name as text.** Fine print at a sixth of the heading size or less reads as a grey panel; one or two words in flat capitals at 3–5% of the height are read at once.
10. **A notch for what is missing.** State the absent or remaining part by a cut of bare ground, and make one opening the only fully closed shape.
11. **One shape, two owners.** Let the largest patch of the second colour run unbroken across two neighbouring panels.
12. **Rim dark, centre hot.** With flat fills, the darkest value and the ground hold the edges; the strongest colours take 1–3% inside a column a fifth of the width.
13. **Line over the pieces.** Draw connecting lines and ticks last, across panel edges, in a thin weight that runs past the part it defines.
14. **Assemble from the axis.** Signs first, panels outward, rim last (400–600ms, ease-out); reduced motion shows the final state.

## What this artist would reject
- A closed contour all the way round a form.
- One viewpoint, one lamp, a vanishing point.
- Decoration with no object behind it; abstraction that cannot be walked back to a thing.
- Even spacing and equal cells (the pieces touch and overlap, sizes differ 1.8% to 13% within one sheet).
- A second hue for its own sake: colour is one family, or a few named papers.
- Whole sentences in the picture; every word fully legible.
- A single material for everything when the subject has several (in the papiers collés); in the Guitar, a mix of materials where one will do.

## Signature prompt seed
Give each view one named subject and set its name as one or two words in flat, heavy capitals. Split the subject into six to ten views and parts, turn each square to the surface and butt them with no gutters on an upright stack, narrow at the top, wide at the base, densest on an axis just right of centre, with half or more of the ground left quiet. Close each part on one or two sides and let another side run into its neighbour. Set four to six tiny literal signs down the axis in the subject's own order. Make each part in a different way (set text as tone, drawn line, pattern, plain fill) and draw no outline round the whole. State one part by a notch of bare ground. Keep one colour family, the same grain of detail everywhere, and spend chroma on about 4% at the centre.
