# Berthe Morisot — Impressionism

## Essence
Morisot treats finish as an amount to be spent, and spends it in very few places: a face, a hat, one hand, a black ribbon. Around that point the paint gets longer, thinner and more open until, at the edge of the cloth, it stops and the canvas is left showing. Figure and surroundings are made with one set of strokes and, after about 1875, at almost the same lightness, so the sitter is found inside the light and not set in front of a background.

## Key works examined
- **The Harbor at Lorient, 1869 (oil on canvas, 43 × 72 cm, National Gallery of Art, Washington).** A calm view with the sitter pushed to the right edge. Teaches the starting point: big even fields, one figure at the far side, closed edges. *(visually checked: NGA open-access file via Commons, 1920 px; palette, diagonal, band heights)*
- **The Cradle, 1872 (oil on canvas, 56 × 46 cm, Musée d'Orsay).** A mother looks through a veil at a sleeping child. Teaches a veil as a third value between two subjects, and one diagonal joining two heads. *(visually checked: Google Art Project file via Commons, 1280 px, colour trusted; a second, pinker Yorck Project scan was seen and its colour discarded)*
- **Reading, 1873 (oil on canvas, 46 × 71.8 cm, Cleveland Museum of Art 1950.89).** One pale dress in a green field. Teaches scatter: the same small marks carry the wildflowers and the dress pattern. *(visually checked: Cleveland CC0 file, 3400 px; palette, region L/C, gradients)*
- **Hide-and-Seek, 1873 (oil on canvas, 45 × 55 cm, private collection).** Two figures around one small tree. Teaches a screen placed in front of the subject. *(visually checked: Commons file, only 800 px of painting and dark; structure trusted, colour not)*
- **In a Villa at the Seaside, 1874 (oil on canvas, 50.2 × 61 cm, Norton Simon Art Foundation M.1979.21.P).** Terrace, railing, post, sea. Teaches the threshold: near dark, far pale, a rail between. *(visually checked: high-resolution Commons file reduced to 1920 px, palette and geometry; a visitor photo was also seen and is greyer, not used for colour)*
- **Eugène Manet on the Isle of Wight, 1875 (oil on canvas, 38 × 46 cm, Musée Marmottan Monet 6029).** Window, curtain, fence, harbour. Teaches stacked screens. *(visually checked: Commons scan, 1280 px; geometry trusted, colour too yellow-green to trust)*
- **Hanging the Laundry out to Dry, 1875 (oil on canvas, 33 × 40.6 cm, National Gallery of Art, Washington).** Teaches a repeated small white touch as the rhythm of a whole landscape, with a raw border. *(visually checked: NGA file via Commons, 1920 px; palette, gradients, edge share)*
- **Woman at Her Toilette, c. 1875–80 (oil on canvas, 60.3 × 80.4 cm, Art Institute of Chicago 1924.127).** Back view, mirror that refuses the face. Teaches dissolving at equal lightness. *(visually checked: museum file via Commons, 1920 px, plus edge crops; region L/C, gradients, edge share)*
- **Summer's Day, 1879 (oil on canvas, unlined, 45.7 × 75.2 cm, National Gallery, London NG3264; long loan to Hugh Lane, Dublin).** Two women in a boat. Teaches one zig-zag stroke for dress, water and leaves, and the bare margin. *(visually checked: Commons file at 3840 px, with face, right-edge and lower-left crops; palette, angles, stroke sizes)*
- **The Wet Nurse Angèle Feeding Julie Manet, 1879–80 (oil on canvas, size not confirmed, private collection).** Teaches how far the face can be left alone while the ground boils. *(visually checked: Commons print scan, 1088 px; relations only, yellow cast)*

## Detailed observations

**The Harbor at Lorient, 1869.** Width to height 1.71. Sky is the top half (horizon about 0.52), and one diagonal quay edge runs from (0.30, 1.0) to (0.82, 0.70), about 18°. The woman occupies only the right 20% of the width, cut slightly by the edge at her hem, head at (0.85, 0.42). Sky and water share one pale family (L 72–82, C 10–17, about 69% of the surface). The detail (boats, town) sits in one thin band between 0.40 and 0.58 of the height. Gradient there is 3–5 against 1.5–2 elsewhere. White dress is among the brightest things and has no outline on the light side. Cracks and darkened ground are visible; the sheet is yellowed. Edges are painted up to the border: the open margin has not arrived yet.

**The Cradle, 1872.** Height to width 1.22. Two heads: mother's face at (0.36, 0.44), child's at (0.70, 0.69). The line between them falls at about 50°. The pink-edged hem she pulls runs from (0.19, 0.77) to (0.59, 0.47), about 42°, across the same gap, so a gaze and a piece of cloth cross. The canopy is a tall triangle whose tip is at (0.77, 0.02) and whose foot is the full width. White is not one thing: window curtain L 68, C 10 (greyed), veil L 76, C 12, front cloth L 83, C 12. The dark gap between the two curtains is L 17 and reaches only about 12% of the surface; the jacket is L 34, C 1. The child's face is L 71 behind the veil with a lightness spread (sd) of 4.5; the mother's is L 68 with a spread of 10.8. So the same subject (a face) is painted at two levels of finish: the one behind the gauze gets less than half the contrast. Colour is nearly absent (mean C 11, 95th percentile 18); red-leaning pixels (a > 15) are 0.07%, all on the ribbon and the mouth. The brightest thing is a small knob at the tip. Left out: any floor, any room depth, any description of the crib.

**Reading, 1873.** Ratio 1.56. The figure is a triangle: hat at (0.41, 0.15), hem spread from 0.32 to 0.82 at the bottom, so it is about 50% of the width at its base and the head is at 0.42. Ground: dark green (L 36–45, about 56% of the surface, gradient 0.55–0.59 of the picture average). Dress L 75, C 2. Dress against grass is 39 L apart: here the figure is still cut out by value. The face is L 55, C 15, only 0.5% of the surface, with gradient 1.56 times the average: the tightest drawing in the picture, together with the black bow (about (0.42, 0.50)) and the hat. Marks: the sprigs on the dress are dots and commas of red, blue and green about 0.5–1% of the width. The same size of comma makes the weeds and wildflowers, and grass blades are painted over the hem at (0.79, 0.88), so ground and cloth interlock. Umbrella at the left edge, fan at (0.72, 0.65), cart at (0.81, 0.12): three satellite objects, increasingly vague. Cleveland reports X-ray evidence that the eyes were first painted looking out, then lowered.

**Hide-and-Seek, 1873.** Ratio about 1.21. A tree stands in the middle left (trunk (0.34, 0.70), crown reaching the top) and divides the canvas between a child at 0.31 and a woman at 0.53. The tree's crown overlaps the woman's outstretched arm. The turquoise open parasol (0.69, 0.77) is the only clear colour and also the only concave form. Horizon 0.25. The white dress is the only large light. Scan is dark (mean L 38): not used for colour.

**In a Villa at the Seaside, 1874.** Ratio 1.24. Horizon 0.37. A rail at 0.53–0.57 closes the near space; the post at 0.70–0.73 and the curtain at 0.86–0.94 cut the right side into three openings of decreasing width. Woman's figure spans 0.11–0.48. Sea: pale green L 81, C 14, smooth (gradient 0.56 of average); sky L 88, C 3, gradient 0.38; skirt L 14, C 2, gradient 1.72, made of broken white commas on black. Skirt against floor is 44 L apart. 26% of the surface is above L 85, 6% under L 20: the light is outdoors and the weight is indoors. Face: a smudge, L 59, C 8, with no eye. The red spots are 0.04% of the surface (a > 30): a toy boat at (0.48, 0.77) and a dot at (0.85, 0.40), both small, both at the far ends of the figure's gaze line. A glass or mug at (0.11, 0.92) stands alone on an empty floor. The folding stool is half bare strokes. Warm ground shows along the top and left edges.

**Eugène Manet on the Isle of Wight, 1875.** Ratio 1.18. Man cropped by the left edge at 0–0.31, head at (0.22, 0.27), looking right. Horizontal bars at 0.21 (sash) and 0.37 (fence) and a sill at 0.79–0.85; vertical curtain from 0.73. The bars cut the bodies outside: the sash cuts a woman at 0.67 so that her head shows above it and her dress below; the fence cuts both outside figures at the waist. Four screens lie between viewer and harbour: curtain, sash, flower pots, fence. The view through the glass is about 40% of the surface. Gradient is highest here, at centre (centre to border 1.46), and lowest in curtain and lower wall. Red geranium dots (about 0.55%, a > 18) are the single colour accent, spread over the middle band. Marmottan's note says her interest is the garden and waterfront through the glass more than the man.

**Hanging the Laundry out to Dry, 1875.** Ratio 1.23, small (33 × 40.6 cm). Horizon 0.33; field strip 0.40–0.47; washing plots 0.48–0.85; fence 0.87–0.97. Sky: L 81, C 6, gradient 0.34 of average; field: L 79, C 17, one long horizontal stroke (stroke direction 2°, coherence 0.52). Below, the stroke turns vertical: white tick, pole, tick, tick. I count around 40–50 separate white touches in the plots, each 1–3% of the width, every one a piece of linen. A fence of vertical strokes at the bottom repeats the pole rhythm at larger scale. Workers are 5–8 strokes each, about 8% of the height. Highest gradient cells are around (0.55, 0.58), the cart and linen (1.9 times the mean), and (0.8, 0.40), the house. The small reds, chimney pots, sit on the house. Outer border: the raw ground (L 78–82, C 11–15) shows around three sides, with the paint stopping in ragged strokes: pixels within ΔE 8 of the bare canvas are 28–40% of the outer 5% band and 18–28% of the interior (interior number is inflated by pale paint of the same colour).

**Woman at Her Toilette, c. 1875–80.** Ratio 1.33 (horizontal). The head is at (0.50, 0.17), almost dead centre, and turned away. The mirror frame is a diagonal from (0.08, 0.0) to (0.21, 0.84), about 9–12° off vertical, filling the left 20%. It reflects the flowers and the powder box, and never her face. The signature is painted along the mirror's lower frame. Figure L 69–73, C 2–8; bed and wall right L 65, C 1; wall left L 54, C 1; mirror L 44. Dress to bed wall is only 8 L apart, with the same chroma 1–2. Mean chroma of the whole is 7 and 74% of pixels are below C 10. It is a grey picture. Colour is one blue thread on the dress edge and ribbon (about 0.2%, b < −15), dark choker, pinkish ticks on the wallpaper. Skin back has the lowest gradient of the figure (0.58 of average); the dress hem breaks into feathers (grad 0.96) and at bottom left the strokes run out to the bare tan canvas (L 72, C 11, 15% of the outer band against 10% of the interior). Upper right corner: bare canvas with a few scumbled strokes. The black choker is the tightest edge in the picture: L under 20, width about 1.5% of the picture height.

**Summer's Day, 1879.** Ratio 1.65. Two figures: left one cropped by the left border, right one facing. Face centres (0.20, 0.30) and (0.53, 0.31): same height. The gunwale is one straight line falling at 16°, from (0.14, 0.56) to (0.99, 0.95), interrupted by the second woman's body; the left and right pieces line up (a line extended from the left piece meets the right piece within 1% of the height). Four registers: bank 0–0.14, far water 0.14–0.45, figures 0.1–1.0, boat floor from 0.55. Right dress L 63, C 3; left water L 68, C 5; right water L 67, C 16; grey dress to water is ≤ 5 L apart. The dress is told from the water by hue only (grey-violet against blue-green) and by stroke direction. Colour: mean C 18, 5% above C 40; strongest blue is the coat (L 26, C 24; 11% of pixels have b < −15), then hat bow and parasol. Warm skin, straw, boat (L 49–72, C 26–27) is about 17% of the surface. Stroke: zig-zag. In the lower-left skirt I count about 8–10 turns in a 20% patch, each leg about 4–6% of the width. On water, dashes are 2–4% of the width (1.5–3 cm) and lie horizontal (178°, coherence 0.31). Leaves, water and bodice use the same loaded dash, but in the right-hand dress the direction is all ways (coherence 0.03). Face: blended, gradient 0.49 (left) and 0.76 (right) of the picture average, eyes are two smudges, mouth one red touch. The right margin, 1–3% of the width, is unpainted warm canvas (L 76–82, C 35–37) with the dashes trailing off in broken dots; lower-left corner is bare, with a round stamp. The National Gallery notes the same mesh of strokes is used for figures, water and foliage, and that a reviewer asked in 1880 why she did not finish.

**The Wet Nurse Angèle, 1879–80.** Ratio 1.22. The woman is central (head (0.47, 0.43)), about 40% of the width at the base, symmetric. The garden around is the busiest field in any of these works: gradient 5–7 at the edges against 2.2–4 in the skirt; garden 1.23 of average, face 0.70, skirt 0.56. Finish drops inward here: the figure is smoother than its surroundings. Blouse L 58, garden L 39, 19 L apart: still visible by value, but scan is yellow. Face is a mask of mouth and two dashes of brow. The only red ((0.53, 0.57) average) is the baby's head and neckerchief. At the bottom edge the skirt dissolves into bare strokes and the grass passes over it.

**Young Woman Knitting, c. 1883.** Ratio 1.20 (canvas). Seated figure at (0.42, 0.45), facing right, face hidden by the hat: face L 67, C 21, gradient 0.84, one blended patch with an ear, no eye. Path edge runs up from (0.52, 0.49) to (0.98, 0.14), about 33°, dividing lawn from path; the lawn is L 53, C 28, path L 74, C 16. Jacket L 65, C 25 stands against path L 74 (9 L) and lawn 53 (12 L): the figure is a middle value between its two grounds. The second chair at the right (0.75–1.0) is cropped by the border and made of about 25–30 thin brown strokes on bare path; you can see the path through every gap. Red parasol (0.55, 0.71) and pink skirt are the red centre: a > 18 is 1.3%. The path is the smoothest area (gradient 0.53 of average), the jabot and hat the tightest.

**Self-Portrait, 1885.** Ratio 1.22. The head is at (0.52, 0.28), about 0.27 of the height. Bare warm ground (L 82, C 16) covers about 15–24% of the picture and 33–51% of the outer 5% band (ΔE 8 of two sampled spots). The ground is smooth (gradient 0.39–0.50 of average). The face has spread 18.7, with Cradle's jacket (18.8) the largest of any region I measured, gradient 1.26; hair is 1.44 with one clear direction (46°, coherence 0.61). **In the Dining Room, 1886.** Ratio 1.23 (height). The maid stands on the vertical axis (centre about 0.49), head (0.465, 0.225), feet 0.88. ## Process and relations (observed)
- **Finish is spent in one or two places.** Highest gradient is the face and bodice in Dining Room (1.23 and 1.58 of the mean), the face and hair in the Self-Portrait (1.26, 1.44), the face in Reading (1.56). In 1879, outdoors, the face is the opposite, the smoothest patch (0.49–0.76) inside busy strokes (Summer's Day, Wet Nurse). Either way, the face differs from its surroundings in touch, not in outline.
- **A ladder of care, never two levels but four or five.** Face and black accent: small, blended, high contrast. Hands and hat: brisk. Dress: long open strokes. Objects at the side: 25–40 strokes, see-through (chair in Knitting, palette in Self-Portrait, dog in Dining Room). Edge: bare ground. The steps can be read in gradient: 1.4–1.6, then 1.0, then 0.7–0.9, then 0.4–0.6.
- **The edge is left open, and it is thicker at the bottom and the far side.** Laundry, Summer's Day, Toilette, Self-Portrait, Dining Room. The bare colour takes 15–51% of the outer 5% band in Toilette, Dining Room, Laundry and Self-Portrait, and about a third to two-thirds less in the interior. The strokes stop in combed ends or broken dots; they do not fade by blur.
- **Figure and ground at nearly the same lightness, told apart by hue and stroke.** Dress versus surround: Reading 39 L apart, Villa 44, Cradle jacket 34 (1872–74); Toilette 8 against the bed wall, Summer's Day 5 against the water, Dining Room 4 (bodice on wall), Knitting 9 (jacket on path) (1875–86). The gap shrinks to about a quarter or less while strokes become freer. It is not lost all round: Dining Room's apron is 20 off the floor and bodice 22 off the window, Toilette's dress 19 off the left wall.
- **One stroke vocabulary across substances.** The comma of Reading sprigs is the comma of the weeds; the zig-zag of Summer's Day is water, coat, hem and bush; the vertical tick of Laundry is pole, cloth and fence board; the feather in Toilette is wallpaper and dress.
- **Direction belongs to a surface.** Floor 2°, water 178°, field 2°; cloth 40–60°; foliage and dress mixed (coherence under 0.1). Horizontal is level things, diagonal is cloth and glass, random is leaf and figure.
- **A tiny, dark, sharp mark anchors a pale picture.** Choker in Toilette and Self-Portrait, ribbon in Reading and Cradle, hat in Villa. Under 3% of the area, L 15–25, usually at the throat, between the least-finished body and the most-finished face.
- **A tiny red, away from the centre.** Roof in Dining Room (0.84, 0.13), toy boat in Villa (0.04% of area), chimneys in Laundry (about 0.03%), geraniums in Wight (0.5%), parasol in Knitting (1.3%). Never over about 1.3%.
- **A screen between viewer and subject.** Veil (Cradle), tree (Hide-and-Seek), rail and post (Villa), sash, curtain, pots and fence (Wight), door frame (Dining Room), mirror frame (Toilette), grass over hem (Reading). Depth comes from things cut, not from shading.
- **Threshold as subject.** Villa, Wight and Dining Room place a window or rail between inside and outside and let the far side be paler (Villa floor L 58 to sky L 88) or share the same lightness (Dining Room).
- **Faces withheld, and some detail transferred elsewhere.** The mirror does not show the face (Toilette), the hat hides it (Knitting), the veil halves its contrast (Cradle), the Villa face is a smudge. The attention goes to neck, hat, hands.
- **Crops on one side.** Left figure of Summer's Day, man in Wight, stool in Villa and chair in Knitting, table in Dining Room are cut by the frame; the cut thing is always the less finished one.
- **Ground as a colour.** Brown in Dining Room, warm cream in Self-Portrait and Laundry, tan in Summer's Day and Toilette: the ground is a middle value seen between strokes, not a white paper.
- **The generative rule:** choose one spot to finish and one tight dark mark near it; step down the care in four levels outward; keep figure and ground within 10 L of each other along at least one long side, and separate them there by hue and stroke direction; use one stroke unit for every substance in a region; put a screen in front of depth; let the outer 5% go bare, more at the bottom and the far edge; add one red under 1%.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Berthe Morisot.** *Treat finish as a budget: resolve one small place fully, set one tight dark mark beside it, step the detail down in four levels to an open edge, and hold subject and ground at nearly one lightness, told apart by hue and by the direction of their marks.*
Process:
1. Lay one tinted ground of middle value, never the blank white of the format, with more chroma than the pale fields that will sit on it. Whatever is not drawn is ground, and it shows between marks. Keep one third to one half of the view quiet, at 0.35–0.6 of the view's mean detail.
2. Plan value before detail: two value families for all surfaces, about 20 L apart, the whole view inside a span of about 40 L. Along at least one long side, keep the subject within 10 L of what it touches and separate the two there by hue and by mark direction only. On the opposite side leave a step of about 20 L, so the subject is lost on one side and found on the other.
3. Choose ONE place to finish: at most 1% of the area, high in the view (0.15–0.45 of the height), near the centre or at one side. Make it differ from its surroundings in grain, with no outline and no enlargement: 1.2–1.6 times the mean detail in a calm view, or 0.5–0.75 of it in a busy one.
4. Set one dark mark touching that place: the darkest value in the view, under 3% of the area, about 1.5% of the short side thick, with the hardest edge in the view. Dark as a mass stays under 1% everywhere else.
5. Step finish down outward in four levels, never two: detail at 1.4–1.6, then 1.0, then 0.7–0.9, then 0.4–0.6 of the mean, then bare ground. A thing on the lowest drawn level is made of 25–40 marks with the ground visible through every gap.
6. Use one mark unit for every kind of thing inside a region, sized to the thing (0.5–1%, 2–4%, 4–6% or 10–20% of the width). Give each kind of surface one direction: level things within 2° of horizontal, upright or hung planes at 40–60°, the subject and loose matter undirected. Repeat small marks 40–50 times at intervals that vary up to 2×, set by the content and never by a grid.
7. Put one to four plain bars or a frame strip in front of the subject, crossing it. Depth comes from what is cut, not from shading. A veiled thing keeps its sharp edges at under half its contrast. Let the frame cut one thing on one side: always the least finished.
8. Leave the edge open: in the outer 5% band, 15–50% is bare ground, and the interior holds a third to two thirds less; most of it lies at the bottom and on one side. Content ends by its marks getting fewer and shorter, never by a fade, a blur or a frame.
9. Add one hue of the highest chroma, at most 1.3% of the area, off-centre and away from the finished place.
Why it works:
- With grain graded in four or five levels, density itself points at the finished place, so it needs no size, border or colour. With two levels the eye sees a cut-out on a backdrop; with equal finish everywhere nothing leads.
- Things of nearly equal lightness group as one field, so the subject reads as part of its setting. Hue and direction are enough to tell them apart at close range, and the one side that keeps a value step lets the eye complete the rest.
- In a view that spans only 40 L, one small dark mark is the single extreme. It gives every pale value a scale and pins the focus. Enlarged, it becomes a mass and the narrow span reads as weak contrast.
- An edge that stops in fewer, shorter marks reads as "this continues", and it lets the ground into the view as a working colour. A closed frame turns the same content into a finished, separate object.
- Overlap by a cut states depth with no shading, so the planes stay flat and equal in value.
Colour relations: the ground is a tinted middle value, more chromatic than the pale fields on it, and takes one third to one half of the view plus the open edge. Surfaces form two value families about 20 L apart. Pale fields come in two or three temperatures within about 15 L of each other, and low-chroma colour covers 40–75% of a quiet view. A shadow is a cooler hue, not a darker neutral. The darkest value is one mark under 3%; the highest chroma is one touch under 1.3%, and it ranks first in saturation and last in area. Planes at one value are separated by temperature.
Space and line: focus at 0.15–0.45 of the height; one long straight line (9–42° off an axis, or axis-aligned bars) runs the full view, is interrupted by the subject and lines up on both sides within 1%; no continuous outline anywhere, only one or two firm dark strokes where a form must hold; nearest layer cut by the frame; no deep box.
Type requirement: one family with at least three steps of weight or width whose lightest still passes AA on the ground; tabular figures at the finished place; short lines set ragged, straight on the ground with no container; lower levels say less (fewer words, no unit, a name only) and never drop in contrast or sharpness.
Motion and interaction: a view builds coarse to fine in three stages (ground and lowest level, middle levels, the finished place and its dark mark last). Finish is a budget: focus or selection raises one item by one level and returns the previous one to its own level. A change of state turns the direction of a region's marks; nothing blurs or fades. Reduced motion: swap states at once.
Fails when: every item must be fully specified (tables, audits, legal and medical figures, forms); the view is mostly text, so dark ink exceeds the budget and the narrow span breaks; all items are peers with no single focus; a control's boundary would fall on the lost side (it needs 3:1); a narrow format has no margin to leave open or side to cut; a high-contrast mode is required.
Signature (do not carry together): feathered and zig-zag brushwork that runs out to bare canvas at the edges · a woman in a white dress, in a garden or at a window, in pearl greys · a black ribbon at the throat with one touch of red.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Incident console for an operations team, desktop 1440 × 900, dark slate with an orange brand hue, a monospaced family, terse and technical (the works are pale, small, domestic and hand-made).
1. *Ground.* One slate ground, L 24, with a visible blue cast; the surfaces set on it are nearer neutral. It is not a page colour: it shows between every mark. The right 30% of the screen and the bottom band stay quiet, about 45% of the area.
2. *Value plan.* Two surface families, L 30 and L 50; the darkest and lightest surfaces are 26 L apart. The active-incident panel (L 30) lies on the ground 6 L away along its left and bottom sides with no border, and is told from it by hue (neutral against blue) and by direction (its rows run level, the ground's timeline ticks stand upright). On its right it meets the service strip at L 50, a step of 20 L.
3. *Finished place.* The current error rate: one figure with unit, change and time stamp, 150 × 56px (0.65% of the screen) at (0.40, 0.24). The view is busy with events, so the figure is the calm spot: a 240px clear field with no rule, tick or icon, at about 0.6 of the mean detail.
4. *Dark mark.* Directly under the figure, one bar 14px high (1.5% of the height) and 300px wide at L 6, carrying the incident ID and severity word in light text: 0.3% of the screen. Nothing else is that dark.
5. *Ladder.* Level 1, the figure: value, unit, change, time. Level 2, three affected services: name and value. Level 3, nine related alerts: name only. Level 4, the upstream dependency sketch at the right: about 30 hairlines, outline only, ground through every gap. Then ground.
6. *Marks.* One unit, a 2 × 12px tick, is the event on the timeline, the bullet of an alert and the segment of the dependency sketch. Level surfaces (tables, timeline) run their marks horizontally; the unacknowledged region is hatched at 50°; the sketch is undirected. The timeline holds about 45 ticks spaced by real event time, so intervals vary.
7. *Screen and cut.* A time-window rail of two hairline bars crosses in front of the timeline and the panel. Events outside the window keep sharp edges at 45% of their contrast. The dependency sketch is cut by the right edge of the screen; no figure and no text is cut.
8. *Open edge.* No panel has a frame. The alert list ends in names only, then single ticks, then ground. The bottom 45px band is 40% ground and the right band 30%; the top and left are full.
9. *Signal.* Orange appears once: the dot and label of the one breaching service, 0.5% of the screen at (0.86, 0.14). The figure itself is not orange.
- *Motion.* The view builds in three stages of 120ms: ground and sketch, then alerts and services, then the figure and its bar. Focusing an alert raises it one level (its value appears) and the previous one returns to a name. Acknowledging turns the 50° hatch level. Reduced motion swaps states.
- *Type.* One monospaced family in two weights, nothing under 12px, every level at AA or better on its surface. Levels differ by how much is written.

Result: a dark, flat, sharp-edged console in monospace, with no brushwork, no softness and no pale light. What survived is finish spent in one place and stepped down in levels, a subject joined to its ground at nearly one value on one side and found on the other, one mark for every kind of thing, an edge left open, and one small signal off-centre.

## Space & composition
- **Head high and near centre, or at one side:** head centre at 0.15–0.45 of the height. Toilette 0.17, Dining Room 0.225, Self-Portrait 0.28, Summer's Day 0.31, Wet Nurse 0.43, Cradle 0.44. Horizontally 0.36–0.53 for single sitters, 0.85 in Lorient.
- **One long straight line carries the picture:** quay 18° (Lorient), hem 42° (Cradle), gunwale 16° (Summer's Day), path 33° (Knitting), mirror 9–12° off vertical (Toilette). Usually one such line only; rail and sash bars in the window pictures are axis-aligned instead.
- **Horizon:** 0.25 (Hide-and-Seek), 0.33 (Laundry), 0.37 (Villa), 0.52 (Lorient); the late interiors have none.
- **Formats are small:** 33 × 40.6 to 61.3 × 80.4 cm; ratios 1.18–1.71.
- **Empty side.** One third to one half of the area is quiet: sky and field (Laundry 0–0.47), path in Knitting, water in Lorient, ground in Self-Portrait. Gradient there 0.34–0.6 of mean.
- **No deep box.** Recession is stacking of layers, with the nearest layer cut by the frame.

## Form, line & typography
- Forms have no continuous outline. The edge is a change of hue, or of stroke direction, with one or two firm dark strokes where the form must hold (choker, hat brim, chin, choker bow, chair post).
- Line types: the calligraphic single stroke (chair posts, mirror bevel), the comma (sprigs, petals), the zig-zag (water, hem), the feather (cloth), and the long drawn highlight over finished paint (apron edge).
- Stroke is sized to the thing: 0.5–1% of width for sprigs, 2–4% for water dashes, 4–6% for hems, 10–20% for apron edges and coat sweeps.
- Letter forms: signature in brown or black script, 8–16% of the width, dropped into a corner as another mark and partly lost against the ground (Dining Room, Laundry). In Toilette it lies along the mirror frame. She writes into the picture, not onto a label; the 1885 self-portrait is unsigned. There is no text otherwise.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- Repeats are small and uneven: linen ticks (about 45 in Laundry), fence posts, balusters in Villa (roughly 5–6 shapes cut by plants), geranium dots in Wight. Interval varies by 2× and is never a grid.
- Time is a held gesture in mid-action: sewing, reading, doing up hair, pulling a veil, passing a hand along a gunwale.
- Speed is readable: loaded, dragged, dry and scrubbed areas show order. Thin base, then middle strokes, then thick impasto highlights last, over the hem and the hat (Dining Room, Toilette).
- Wind and light changes are shown by stroke direction shifting across a surface, not by blur: water is 178° far, curved and crossed near the boat.
- Sequence of works: 1869–74 value-cut figures and closed edges; 1875–79 zig-zag unity and open edges; 1883–86 open figures on coloured ground. The same subject types (mother, sitter at glass, woman in garden) come back in each phase; the Barnes sees landscape technique moved onto figures. Formal series are not shown by these works.

## UI translations
1. **A finish budget.** Give full specification (value, unit, change, time, state) to one item under 1% of the screen. Everything else says less, in four declared levels down to bare ground.
2. **A dark mark at the focus.** One near-black bar or rule, under 3% of the area and about 1.5% of the short side thick, touches the key item. No other dark mass on the screen exceeds 1%.
3. **Lost on one side, found on the other.** A primary surface sits within 10 L of the ground along one long side, with no border, and about 20 L from its neighbour on the opposite side.
4. **Hue and direction as the boundary.** Where two regions share a value, change temperature and the direction their repeated units run (rows against columns, level against a 40–60° hatch).
5. **One mark for every kind of thing in a region.** A tick is the event, the bullet and the chart mark; its size follows the thing (0.5–1% of the width for points, 2–4% for runs).
6. **See-through secondary objects.** A side panel or secondary chart is 25–40 thin lines on the ground with no fill, and the screen edge may cut it.
7. **A screen in front.** A pinned rail, bar or frame strip crosses the content; what lies behind a veil keeps sharp edges at under half its contrast. No shadows.
8. **Open edge.** No frames. Lists and charts end in fewer, shorter marks; the outer 5% band is 15–50% ground, most at the bottom and on one side. Never a gradient fade.
9. **A tinted ground that shows.** The ground is a middle value with more chroma than the pale surfaces on it, visible between marks and over a third to a half of the view.
10. **One small signal, off-centre.** The most saturated hue covers at most 1.3% of the screen, away from the key item, with a label.
11. **Build coarse to fine.** Load the ground and the lowest level first, the key item and its dark mark last; focus raises one item by one level.

## What this artist would reject
- Equal finish everywhere: every part described to the same degree.
- A background that is only a backdrop: untouched, blurred, or in different marks from the figure.
- Full-strength colour in large areas, or one accent used many times.
- A hard outline all the way round; a drop shadow to lift a figure off its ground.
- A clean frame around everything; a centred, complete, uncut composition of separate objects.
- Photographic faces and described features as the place of meaning.
- Evenly spaced repeats and ruled intervals.

## Signature prompt seed
Treat finish as a budget. Lay a tinted middle-value ground and let it show between everything. Give full detail to one place under 1% of the view, high on the screen, and set one thin near-black mark against it. Step the detail down outward in four levels until the content ends in a few short marks and bare ground, most at the bottom and on one side, with no frame and no fade. Keep surfaces within about 40 L; hold the main surface within 10 L of the ground on one side and tell them apart by hue and by the direction of their marks. Use one mark for every kind of thing in a region. Cross the subject with a plain bar or rail. Add one saturated touch under 1.3%, off-centre. Text stays sharp at every level and only says less.
