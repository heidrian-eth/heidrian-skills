# Edgar Degas — Impressionism

## Essence
Degas builds an "accidental" glimpse out of stored parts. Figures are drawn once, kept, then traced, mirrored, re-scaled and re-placed; the frame is the last decision, pushed until it cuts bodies and leaves a large tilted floor empty. The unposed moment (a yawn, a scratch, a tied shoe) is the result of many recorded revisions, and the same plate or composition is carried through states that change tone while position stays fixed.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **Figures are parts in a library** (both Dance Classes, Ballet Master, Nelson Rehearsal, both Cassatt prints and pastel, Dance Lesson). The ballet master stands in two paintings and, reversed, in two monotype images; the throat-holding girl, hands-on-hips girl and red shawl recur in both dance classes at heights within a few percent; the Cassatt back view runs from pastel to two plates. Each reuse may mirror (Ballet Master, Etruscan Gallery), move (x 0.76 to 0.89), or overlap with another part (Paintings Gallery).
- **Tracing and reversing is the engine** (Before the Race: traced, reversed, third interval widened, per Cleveland; Cassatt mirror). Reversal costs nothing, and gives a second picture.
- **One composition, several runs** (two Rehearsals; two pulls; four states). Position changes by about 0.02 or not at all, while medium, key, chroma, hidden foreground object or added figures change. The mean value and spread can stay the same (mean L 39, sd 16 in both Rehearsals).
- **States change tone, not drawing** (Paintings Gallery: landmarks fixed; mean L 58, 40, 49, 46; sd 13–20). Each state is a complete image rather than a draft.
- **Empty lower right, crowd to the opposite corner** (Dance Class Orsay 20%, Met 21%; Rehearsals 15–16%; Dance Lesson 34%; Concorde 27%; Before the Race lower right third). Empty ground is in one connected polygon, not distributed; it starts where the frame cuts it.
- **The near figure is a lever** (Orsay dancer 16%, back view; Met pair; Dance Lesson red bodice; Orchestra bassist; Absinthe matchstriker; Millinery hats). Near figures are turned away, cut or busy, and cause the far crowd to read as small. Scale jump 3:1 to 4:1 with few intermediate sizes.
- **Frame cuts at least two sides** (Races: right and bottom; Concorde: left, bottom, top; Dance Lesson: left, bottom and right; Orchestra: top and right; Absinthe: right; Millinery: top, right and bottom; Jockeys: right). What is cut is often the subject (dancers' heads), a hat, a horse, a hand. X-ray and text evidence: Orchestra canvas trimmed at sides and top; Concorde cropping the penultimate change.
- **A barrier stands in the way** (Jockeys post at x 0.64; Cassatt pillar 26–27%; Orchestra chair back; bass scroll; Absinthe tables; Met music stand). The obstacle covers part of the subject and is given full finish.
- **Accent is one per group, under 1%** (Dance Lesson red 0.5% at the start of a path and yellow at its end; Met reds 0.36% at four depths; Concorde red spots; Orsay sash colours). In high-chroma works (Millinery, Before the Race) the logic reverses: C over 40 covers 44% and 27%, and the accent is the cool hue.
- **Path by zigzag or by stepped diagonal** (Absinthe 35°, 30°, 13°; Dance Lesson clumps; Orchestra V of bassoon and bass; Orsay funnel). Perspective lines disagree (6° wainscot against diagonal floor).
- **Parallel echo of figure and ground** (La La: arm 28° to girder 34°, shins 35°; Orchestra bows against legs). The surrounding lines repeat the body's angle within several degrees (measured by eye).
- **Attention is divided** (Concorde, Absinthe, Cotton Office, Rehearsals, Cassatt prints). No gaze meets another or ours; unposed acts (yawn, scratch, read, slump) sit in the front row, the main action far away.
- **The result is not spontaneous** (a widely cited remark, primary source not located: "no art was ever less spontaneous than mine"). Millinery Shop (1879–86) and Orsay Dance Class (1873–76) were reworked over years; x-rays show moved heads, feet and mirror.
- **The generative rule:** keep a small stock of figures in unposed acts; place the same figure mirrored or moved; assign one near, turned-away, cut figure as the lever; let one connected empty field of 15–35% lie at the opposite lower corner; cut at least two edges through important things; put one barrier in front; keep the accent under 1% (or, in a hot field, the cool one); then vary tone in successive states while the drawing stays fixed.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Edgar Degas.** *Compose from a small stock of kept parts, re-placed, mirrored and overlapped; set the frame last so it cuts through things and leaves one connected empty field opposite the crowd; then lock every position and run the view through states that change tone alone.*
Process:
1. Keep a stock before any layout: a small set of parts, each drawn once and shown at work, not presenting itself. Every later use is the same part moved, mirrored or overlapped by another, never redrawn; its size holds within a few percent from view to view (0.39 against 0.40 of the height).
2. Allow two or three sizes and no ladder between them: the near part is 3–4 times the far ones. Appoint one near part as the lever: 15–30% of the view, a secondary item in the middle of its own task, not the headline.
3. Push the mass into one corner along a diagonal and leave ONE connected empty field of 15–35% at the opposite corner, touching the frame. Never distribute it as margins. Intervals between groups are unequal (one gap 2–4 times its neighbour).
4. Lead from the lever to the main item by a stepped path of 3–4 legs at different angles (35°, 30°, 13°), or by groups stepping down in size along the diagonal (1 : 0.6 : 0.3). The main item sits far and small at the end of the path, off-centre.
5. Put one barrier in front: a fixed object 5–27% of the width that covers part of what lies behind it and carries the fullest finish in the view.
6. Spend detail in at most three bands at three levels. The middle, working band is the tightest; the far band, where the nominal subject is, is the loosest.
7. Set the frame last. Move it until it cuts at least two sides through things that matter, never symmetrically; a cut item shows anything from 8% of itself upward. Then trim the format to what is left (strips of 2.3:1 and 2.9:1 are allowed), removing the part that only looks on.
8. Hold the field narrow in chroma and place the accent as spots: under 1% in total, either a chain of 2–4 that steps back in depth or one at the first and one at the last step of the path. If the field itself is high in chroma, the accent is the one hue of opposite temperature, at about 1%. Where value must stay flat (spread of 6 L), separate the subject by hue alone.
9. Lock the drawing (drift at most 0.02 of the width) and run the view through states. A state changes tone only: mean value across a range of about 18 L, the dark share from 5% to 25%, or the key from cool to warm with mean and spread unchanged. Every state is complete, and the states are kept side by side.
Why it works:
- Scale reference: one large near thing and many small far ones, with no sizes between, give depth at once and make the far crowd read as a crowd. Add the intermediate sizes and the jump becomes a gradient that carries no distance; centre the lever and it becomes the subject.
- Depth from overlap and the cut edge: a thing covered by a barrier or by the frame is completed by the eye behind it, so the view reads as a sample of something larger that goes on without the viewer. Show every item whole and the view becomes a display arranged for inspection.
- Visual weight: one connected empty field is a mass in its own right and balances the crowded corner; it also gives the small far items a measure. Split it into margins and it is only padding; fill it and nothing says how dense the crowd is.
- Adaptation to the previous state: with every position fixed, the only difference between two states is tone, so the eye reads the difference as the change in condition. Move one part between states and the change can no longer be attributed.
Colour relations: one low-chroma field family for 50–60% of the view in two neighbouring values; one dark mass (20–60%) that repeats across the crowd and groups it; accent spots under 1% in total, the highest chroma on the smallest area, repeated 2–4 times along depth; in a high-chroma field (over 40% of the area) the accent is the opposite temperature at about 1%; an equal-value mode where hue alone separates (value spread 6 L).
Space and line: void 15–35% in one polygon at the corner opposite the mass; lever 15–30%; size jump 3:1–4:1; barrier 5–27% of the width; at least two edges cut, asymmetrically; path of 3–4 legs at unequal angles; unequal intervals; subject off-centre (about a third in from two edges, or high at one side); earlier contours stay visible beside the corrected one.
Type requirement: text is a kept part too: each label is set once and reused at the same size wherever its part appears. Two or three sizes with a 3:1–4:1 jump and nothing between; one size per band of finish. Words recognisable by outline at the far size (mixed case, clear ascenders). Tabular figures, so numbers hold their position from state to state. A frame or barrier may cut a container, never a word or a figure: text re-seats inside the visible part.
Motion and interaction: a state change is a change of tone in place (250–400ms, linear, nothing moves); scrubbing through states keeps every position. The frame is what moves: panning brings a cut item into view while the layout stays still. A reused part may flip to its mirror in one step. A revised value keeps its earlier value beside it, thin. Reduced motion: states switch at once.
Fails when: every item must be seen whole and at once (forms, checkout, legal or audit tables); the cut or the barrier would hide a control, a value or an error; the screen is too small to give up 15% of its area; states differ in the number or order of items, so positions cannot be locked; there is only one item and no stock to re-place; one message must be read first and centred (an alarm).
Signature (do not carry together): a bare tilted floor with the crowd in a far corner · bodies seen from behind and cut by the frame or a post · chalky, reworked contours on performers and horses.

**Transfer test** (an illustration that the process survives new colours and content; never reuse its hues, layout or wording as a design answer):

Degas's procedure → a build-pipeline run view for a developer tool, desktop 1440 × 900, cold white with a brand violet, exact and terse (the works are warm, dim rooms full of people).
1. *Stock.* Six parts, each drawn once: job tile, stage header, log line, artefact chip, runner tag, duration figure. A job tile is the same part wherever it stands; fan-in jobs use its mirror, with the status edge on the other side.
2. *Sizes.* The job now running is the lever: 520 × 380px (15% of the screen). Every other job tile is 130 × 95px (1:4). There is no medium tile.
3. *Void.* Far tiles pack into the upper right; the lever sits lower left. One empty polygon of about 640 × 450px (22%) fills the lower right and touches the right and bottom edges. It holds only the run number, small. Gaps between stage groups: 96, 24 and 40px.
4. *Path.* Three connector legs run from the lever's output to the queued stage to the failing tile, at 35°, 30° and 13°. The failing tile, the thing the user came for, is far, small and at (0.78, 0.2).
5. *Barrier.* The commit rail, 84px wide (5.8%), stands at x 0.64 from top to bottom in front of the tiles, with full detail: hash, author, message. Tiles behind it are covered; their labels re-seat to the visible side.
6. *Finish.* Far tiles: name and a status glyph. Stage rows in the middle band: every step, with durations. The lever: its heading and the live step only.
7. *Frame.* The pipeline canvas is wider than the screen. The frame is set so the lever is cut by the left and bottom edges (88% showing) and the last stage by the right edge (8% showing). The stage that only reports results is trimmed off. No word is cut.
8. *Accent.* Violet is 0.6% of the screen in two spots: the failed-step marker on the lever, at the start of the path, and the failing tile at its end, each with a glyph and the word "failed". The field is two near-white values; dark text blocks group the crowd.
9. *States.* Runs 412 to 415 are four states of the same view. No tile moves. Tile tone alone steps with duration, the dark share rising from 5% to 20%. The four states sit in a strip of thumbnails; scrubbing changes tone in place over 300ms.
- *Type.* 13px on the far tiles, 44px on the lever, nothing between; tabular figures, so durations hold their columns through all four runs.

Result: a cold, violet, technical tool with no room, no people and no chalk. What survived is a stock of re-placed parts, one lever and a size jump, one connected void, a barrier, a frame that cuts, and tone as the only thing that changes between locked states.

## Space & composition
- **One connected void, opposite the crowd**: 15–34% of the area, touching the frame, mostly lower right (Dance Classes, Rehearsals, Dance Lesson). The mass sits on the other corner, joined along a diagonal.
- **High floor line**: floor fills 47–50% of height or more in the rehearsal rooms (wall–floor at y ≈ 0.50 in both Dance Classes, 0.22 in the Dance Lesson at the far end), tilted up.
- **Off-centre primary**: La La centre about (0.34, 0.34), absinthe pair's heads both at y 0.17, at x 0.52 and 0.87, Races carriage lower right, jockeys on the right of a post at x 0.64.
- **Formats follow the subject**: 2.3:1 and 2.9:1 strips (Dance Lesson, Frieze), 2.4:1 upright (Cassatt plate), 0.66:1 (La La). The La La study is 1 : 2.3 and the final 1 : 1.5: what was removed is the audience half.
- **Three handling planes** (Orchestra): loose far, tight middle, dark near; the far plane is cut at the top.
- **Frame as blade**: figures at edges show from 8% (Concorde bystander) to most of the body; the cut is never symmetrical.
- **Unequal intervals**: Frieze 0.16, 0.18, 0.29; Dance Lesson gaps 0.46 / 0.10 / 0.18 (by eye).

## Form, line & typography
Line is drawn, then kept: reworked contours stay visible (pastel Cassatt: trial marks beside the cuff and glove; Frieze: black contour corrections; Paintings Gallery: hatching, scraping, aquatint over line). Silhouettes do most of the naming: back views, hats, tutus, horses, instruments; faces are often hidden. Tutu edges are broken by scumbled, drier strokes while arms and shoes get firm contours. Finish is uneven by plan: tight heads, loose stage. Text inside pictures (a poster in the Met class reading GUIL..., newspaper in the Cotton Office and café) is treated as a grey block cut by the edge. Signature is written small on an object or the empty floor and often near the void.

## Rhythm, motion & time
Time is a sample of postures around the act (tying, yawning, scratching, waiting), not the act. The Frieze puts four views of one pose on one line; the Dance Lesson does it across depth. Interval and scale shrink toward the far corner, so the eye accelerates along the diagonal, then comes back by the bare floor. Sequences happen between works: states of a plate, pulls of a plate, reversed traces; tone and surroundings progress while position holds. Nothing is climactic; the decisive performance is cropped, small or over.

## UI translations
1. **A stock of parts, re-placed.** Draw each component once; every later appearance is the same part moved, mirrored or overlapped, at the same size within a few percent.
2. **Two sizes, far apart.** A near item at 3–4 times the far ones, with no medium size; the near one is a secondary item in the middle of its task, 15–30% of the view.
3. **One connected void.** Leave 15–35% of the view empty in one piece at the corner opposite the crowd, touching the frame; do not spread it as margins and do not fill it.
4. **Crop last, through two edges.** Lay out on a canvas larger than the viewport, then set the frame so it cuts containers on at least two sides, asymmetrically, with 8% or more of each cut item showing. Words and figures are never cut.
5. **One finished barrier.** One fixed element 5–27% of the width stands in front of the content, covers part of it and carries the fullest detail.
6. **A stepped path to a small subject.** Lead from the near item to the main one by 3–4 legs at unequal angles, or by groups that step down in size (1 : 0.6 : 0.3) along a diagonal.
7. **Three levels of finish.** Detail by band: fullest in the working middle, least at the far end; no blur, only fewer fields.
8. **Accent as a chain of spots.** Under 1% in total: 2–4 spots stepping back in depth, or one at each end of the path, each with a label or glyph. In a high-chroma theme the accent is the single opposite-temperature hue at about 1%.
9. **States change tone, not layout.** Versions, periods or conditions of one view keep every position (drift at most 2% of the width); only tone changes, across about 18 L of mean value or 5–25% dark share. Show the states side by side.
10. **Same mean, other key.** A second run of a view may shift from cool to warm while its mean value and spread stay the same, so the two remain comparable.
11. **Keep the correction.** A revised value shows its earlier value beside it as a thin mark.
12. **Equal-value mode.** Where contrast must stay low (value spread about 6 L), separate the subject by hue and by finish, and give text its own contrast.

## What this artist would reject
- A centred subject with even margins.
- A complete figure with nothing overlapping it.
- A crowd all looking at the same thing.
- A layout that fills the empty quarter with more content.
- Starting each picture from zero instead of re-using a figure.
- Making the accent large or giving every group its own.
- A single final version (tone states hidden, corrections erased).

## Signature prompt seed
Build the view from a small stock of parts, each drawn once and re-placed, mirrored or overlapped. Use two sizes 3–4 times apart: one near, secondary item at 15–30% of the view and a crowd of small ones packed toward the opposite corner, the main item far and small at the end of a stepped path. Leave one connected empty field of 15–35% touching the frame. Stand one fully detailed barrier in front. Set the frame last so it cuts containers on two sides, never text. Keep the accent under 1%, as a chain of spots. Then lock every position and show the view in several states that differ in tone alone.
