# Camille Pissarro — Impressionism

## Essence
Pissarro keeps the whole surface going at once: sky, road, wall, tree and crowd are laid in together, in the same small touch, and every part is corrected against every other until they agree. From one fixed window he then repeats the same scaffold and changes only the weather, the hour and the season, so the difference between two canvases is the subject. People are never picked out: they are one more size of touch in the same fabric.

## Detailed observations

**Boulevard Montmartre, the series (seven canvases compared).** All are landscape rectangles, 1.20–1.29 : 1. The scaffold is the same: a V of sky dropping between two walls of buildings to a point left of centre, a road widening from that point to fill the bottom, a row of trees on each side, a kiosk and tree at the lower left, a lamp post just off centre standing across the road, a pavement edge running to the lower right corner. Only the viewpoint turns a little and the crop rises or falls.

The mean lightness moves 40 points (24 to 64) and the mean chroma 14 (6 to 20) while the drawing hardly moves at all. The relations inside each canvas do shift with the condition: in full sun sky and road are 35 L apart, in winter 19, in mist 21; at night the order is reversed, the road (L35) is 16 L lighter than the sky (L19) because the wet street carries the light. Sky and road share a hue in mist, winter and spring (hue angles 86/92, 103/90, 99/83): the weather is one mixture spread over both. They part in the sunny and festive canvases (afternoon sky 124, road 79; Mardi Gras 67/47), where the ground is warmed separately. In the mist the far end is L75, only 7 below the sky, and the near roof L46–60; the depth is a lightness ramp, not a smaller drawing. In the afternoon the far end (L58) is not paler than the near façades (L54), so distance is told by size of touch alone.

**Winter Morning (1:1 crop).** A thick grey-violet ground is dragged in short, mostly horizontal and slightly diagonal ridges that catch light; the figures are put on over it in two or three touches each: a dark trunk, a skirt stroke, sometimes a pink head. A woman in the crowd has an apron that is one white stroke and a collar of orange. The carriage horse is five strokes of white and blue. Nothing has an outline; a cab's edge is where its dark stops. Small unmixed touches of red and yellow sit on almost every object (cab wheel yellow, kiosk red-brown, a red flash in the crowd), but together the saturated red is 0.1% of the canvas. Figure heights: ≈.11 of the canvas height at the near right, ≈.055 at the middle right, ≈.01 near the apex: a ladder of 11 : 5.5 : 1 with nothing else changing. By the raised ridges in the photograph the paint is thickest in the light sky and white cab roofs, thinner in the dark trees; but every zone is worked over, none left bare.

**Morning mist.** Near objects hold full dark (kiosk and trees L33–46) while the far third dissolves into two or three steps between L66 and L81. The carriages become soft-edged darks, the lamp post is drawn as a run of broken vertical dashes with the road showing through. The only warm colour is a red cab at .52, .65 and a little rust in the lower corners.

**Spring and end of day.** Green appears only as touches over bare branches: 4.0% and 4.2% of the canvas by mask (spring `#949760`, L61, C≈30), gathered in two masses at x ≈.10–.25 and x ≈.55–.95, right and left of the road, so the road's pale wedge is framed by them. Everything else keeps the greys of the winter canvases, two lightness steps up. At the end of the day the light lies on the road as a pale wedge of `#C3B499` (10%), and every shadow is a blue-violet grey.

**Mardi Gras.** The road and pavements become crowd: the lower 45% of the canvas is one fabric of dabs 1–2% of the width across, dark (L22 average) with single unmixed lights; a red, orange and green procession narrows from about x .26–.42 at y .80 to a point near .33,.48, with an open pink road below it (`#825E51`). Clear reds (a > 18) are 2.5% of the canvas. Where a group of people would be a drawing, here it is a change of density and colour.

**Boulevard at Night.** Lit areas are tiny: pixels lighter than L65 are 1.1% of the canvas, warm light of C > 40 is 0.8%, against 43% of blue. They are still the whole subject. The NG names three lights: cool white electric arc lamps with indigo halos, warm gas in shop windows, oil lamps on cabs. They are painted as three kinds of touch, not only three colours: round impasto blob with a dark ring (lamps), upright strokes of yellow-orange in the right-hand shop fronts, tiny red and white dabs in the cab line. The wet pavement repeats the orange as vertical smears. The sky is made of strokes that fan upward from the apex.

**Garden of the Tuileries (three, 1899).** Same terrace window, sky 45–50% of the height, towers at x ≈.38–.42 and lawn at lower centre in the two winter canvases; in the spring one the view is turned and the towers move to x ≈.66. Tonal readings: winter A sky `#ACAEA6` L71 C5, bare-tree band `#595E62` L40 C3, lawn `#7F8650` L54 C29, path `#988977` L55; winter B sky `#DBD3C4` L85 C9, tree band `#5A544B` L36 C6, path `#C5AD93` L69; spring sky `#C3C2C7` L79 C2, tree band `#9D9663` L62 C28, lawn `#8C984F` L60 C40, path `#D2B38E` L73 C24. Between two winter days the sky changes by 14 L and the path by 14 L, the trees by 4. Between winter and spring the trees change by 22–26 L and gain 22–25 chroma. The layers that move are different each time: weather moves sky and ground together, season moves foliage.

**Hoar Frost (1873).** The horizon sits at .39, the sky takes 39%. The field is two-part: an upper slope of frost and a lower right of ploughed furrows that converge toward upper left. Blue-grey shadow strokes (median `#637176`, L48, b below zero) cross the field in long bands. Those at the lower left (x .1–.4) run off the bottom edge and no tree inside the frame can be their caster, so their source lies outside the picture (read from the image; no source was found that says so). The blue strokes are only 2.6% of the mid-right patch I masked and the yellow-ochre strokes 10%, yet they set the temperature. Furrow paint at 1:1 is a mosaic of separated blunt strokes, orange next to grey-blue next to brown, all short and roughly horizontal; the frost above is the same stroke, paler. The walker at .23, .60 is the only upright, ≈ 20% of the height, and he is toned to the ground.

**Orchard in Bloom (1872).** A flat blue sky (`#ACCAE3`, L80, C16, sd 4) and a flat brown earth (`#918068`, sd 6) hold most of the canvas between them (the two blue sky clusters alone are 41%); the big blossoming tree occupies x .02–.55, y .17–.9 as a lace of white and green touches (L spread sd 18), the rest is quiet. Two small working figures sit at .12,.77 and .81,.75, each ≈ .12 of the height, both half the tone of the trunks. Lightness range 38–84 (5th–95th): no black anywhere.

**Jalais Hill (1867).** The base line for comparison. Large flat areas of single colour edged by shape: a huge dark mass fills the right half (L17, 24% of the canvas), with a second dark green at 21%; 6% of the pixels are below L12. Horizon at about .33, the fields a patchwork of flat oblongs. Value spread sd 28, the widest of any work measured. A road enters the foreground at the bottom centre. Everything after this compresses that range and breaks the flats into touches.

**The Côte des Bœufs (1877).** A tall canvas, 1.31 : 1. Seven or eight trunks run from bottom to top or leave the frame at the top; the gaps between them run from ≈.04 to ≈.14 of the width by eye, irregular. The white houses and one orange roof appear in the gaps at x .54–.98, y .46–.63, cut by trunks, so the biggest light mass in the picture is never seen whole. Detail: the trunks, ground and foliage are the same small multi-coloured strokes in different directions, roughly 5–10 mm long by pixel scale, layered into a mesh; a figure at .24,.64 with a white kerchief is four touches and a second face beside it is almost lost, as the gallery also notes. Colour range narrow: `#7B7D69`, `#958F6E`, `#767454`, `#5F6452` take 80%, sd of L only 12.

**Edge of the Woods (1879).** The closest range of all: L 22–53 for 90% of the pixels, mean L36, sd 10, and mean C19 across the whole canvas; five greens within 21 L. A pale house wall (`#78857A`) and a light tree trunk are the only departures. A lying man and a goat are laid into the same texture.

**Wooded Landscape, print.** The plate is a drawn version of the same screen: four or five trunks bending right, a path, a man with a pale shirt at left of centre, houses behind. Built by tone more than line: soft-ground strokes and grainy aquatint, no contour. Of the three final impressions, Met and NGA measure L≈67–68 (10–13% dark, 49–52% light), Cleveland's darker inking L≈51 (35% dark, 9% light): the same plate, printed 16 L apart. The Met and NGA papers are cream, Cleveland's grey, so some of the difference is paper and camera. The darkest fifth of the height is always the fourth (the lower middle), lighter at the base.

**Apple Harvest (1888).** Four workers and a leaning tree on a pale ochre field; horizon at .22, the curved tree line at .25, the trunk from .51,.18 to .56,.72. The shadow is a pool across y .72–.95 made of separate coloured touches. In the shadow patch I measured blue touches 18% (`#636377`), red-orange 19% (`#905D46`), green 27% (`#5A683A`); the lit field is yellow 82% of its chromatic pixels. Conservators describe red, blue, green, pink, lavender, orange and yellow dots that stay separate, and blue strengthened along the shadow's edge to sharpen it against the light. Figures are the same stroke, turned to follow the body. Conservators later removed an amber varnish that had made it look yellower; the file used may predate that, so treat its yellow as an upper bound.

**Place du Théâtre Français (LACMA, 1898).** A plan view from a high window: no sky at all. Three pale ground tones (`#C7AF85`, `#D2C2A0`, `#A08F70`, L60–79) make 73% of the frame; carriages, buses and people are scattered over it at irregular intervals, their sizes shrinking up the canvas. Two bare trees enter from the upper right and cross the top. The eye drifts, there is no central focus.

**Rue de l'Épicerie (1898).** Three towers stand at fixed heights in the upper half over a triangular lane of market. In the crowd crop each person is 2–5 strokes of unmixed colour: dark skirt, coloured blouse, dot of head, a red parasol. Sky `#B0B7C7` L74, the tower `#838384` (average hue almost none), the crowd average `#6F6862` L45: a bright crowd is grey at a distance.

## Process and relations (observed)
- **One scaffold, many conditions** (Boulevard Montmartre ×7, Tuileries ×3). Sky apex x .25–.37, lamp x .48–.58, right roof 12–15° in five of seven; mean L 24–64, mean C 6–20. Positions move by about .1 of the width as he turns or crops; the colour and value key is remade each time. He did not move his chair: in 1897 and 1898 he took a hotel room with the view he wanted (letters, 8 February and 15 December 1897), and he reports that varied effects completely transform a repeated motif (letter of 26 December 1891, on studies from his window).
- **Weather is one mixture laid over sky and ground together.** Sky and road hue angles agree within 16° in mist, winter and spring, and the lightness of the two rises together (winter sky 74 / road 55; spring 92 / 69; Tuileries winter A to B, both +14). In direct sun they part. Season acts on another layer: foliage (4% of the canvas) and nothing else.
- **Compress the range, then spend it** (Jalais 1867 sd 28; Hoar Frost 16; Côte des Bœufs 12; Edge of the Woods 10; Winter Morning 15). The 5th–95th percentile span shrinks from 81 L (Jalais 11–92) to 31 L (Edge of the Woods 22–53) and near-black (L < 12) from about 5% to zero. It returns only where the condition is a contrast: afternoon sun (sd 24, 5.4% near-black), Mardi Gras (20, 5.0%) and night (18% near-black); in mist, winter, spring and dusk it is 0–0.5%.
- **One touch for every substance, its direction changed** (Winter Morning, Côte des Bœufs, Rue de l'Épicerie, Apple Harvest). Horizontal on roads and sky, upright for figures and shop fronts, fanned for the night sky, following the limbs in Apple Harvest. The stroke, roughly 0.5–2.5% of the width (finest in the 1897 crowds, broadest in the 1873 furrows), is the only unit; objects end where the touch ends.
- **Figures at the size of the touch.** A near person is 2–5 strokes, a far one is a single dab: 11 : 5.5 : 1 height ladder (Winter Morning), 2–5 strokes in the crowd (Épicerie), four touches for a whole woman (Côte des Bœufs). No face, no gesture, no focus figure. In Mardi Gras the crowd is a change of density over 45% of the canvas.
- **The accent is scattered and small.** Saturated colour (C > 30) is 0–3.4% in six of the seven Boulevards. It is always spread over many objects (cab wheels, kiosk, blouses, parasol), never one spot. In the night the 0.8% of warm light (C>40) is the picture's subject because the other 99% is dark blue and grey.
- **The frame cuts; the source may lie outside.** Shadow bands from unseen trees (Hoar Frost), trunks leaving the top edge, houses cut by trunks (Côte des Bœufs), a near roof chopped at the right edge (all Boulevards), vehicles leaving the LACMA square. The scene is always larger than the picture.
- **Depth by lightness and touch, not line.** Mist: near L46, far L75, sky L82. Sun: same lightness all the way, only touch sizes fall. Only a few hard verticals (lamp, trunks) give measure.
- **Light is several colours at once.** Night (three lamp types, three touch shapes); Hoar Frost (blue shadow strokes 2.6%, yellow 10% of the patch); Apple Harvest (shadow of blue 18, red 19, green 27). A shadow or a lit area is never one mix: it is a set of separate hues that average to the wanted tone (the Épicerie crowd averages `#6F6862`).
- **Everything advances together.** The Le Bail advice, as recorded: work on sky, water, branches and ground at once, on an equal basis. The results measured here support it: the touch is of one family over sky, road and walls, and no area is left bare or polished. Corrections continue after the motif: in late April 1897 he is retouching the Boulevards before they go to the dealer; the fog version of the Place du Théâtre Français is said to have taken one day (Thyssen).
- **The state, not the picture** (1879 plate). Six states, then impressions of one plate 16 L apart by inking and wiping: the same drawing under different light, as the later series. In a letter of 4 March 1894 he tells Lucien to attend to the general values and to mix freely, not to let each colour dry inside its boundary.

## Process (derived)
*From observation to process (see SKILL.md §1). This is what transfers; the rest of the file is evidence.*

**Camille Pissarro.** *Fix one window and a few hard landmarks; build every zone and every item from one small unit, so direction and density do the drawing; give each layer its own clock and re-key only the layer whose clock has struck, inside a narrow value band; scatter a tiny accent over many items; keep the states as a bordered set.*
Process:
1. Fix the window before any content: one frame ratio and one raised viewpoint for every state. Set a far point where the lanes of the view converge at 0.25–0.37 of the width, never on the centre, and one hard upright landmark at 0.48–0.58, so the two stand 0.15–0.27 of the width apart. Add two to four more fixed landmarks (an edge line to a lower corner, a block cut by a side). Between states a landmark may drift by about 0.1 of the width and no more. The open top field takes 12–32% of the area and the working ground about half; seen from straight above, drop the top field and let two or three ground tones hold about three quarters of the frame.
2. Sort what the view holds into layers by how often each changes, and give each its clock: the ambient pair (top field and ground) by the hour, one small layer by the season, the population by the day, the structure hardly ever. A new state moves only the layers whose clock has struck: between two states of the same season the ambient pair moves about 14 L and the seasonal layer about 4; across a season that layer moves 22–26 L and gains as much chroma while the structure keeps its value.
3. Choose ONE unit, 0.5–2.5% of the width, and build every zone from it. Nothing is outlined: a thing ends where its units end. The unit changes direction with what it describes (flat for ground and top field, upright for standing things, radiating for a field that spreads from the far point) and never changes kind.
4. Key each state: one mean lightness and one lean of hue for the whole view. Across a set the mean may travel 40 L and the mean chroma three times over. In a quiet state hold surfaces to a span of 30–65 L (5th to 95th percentile) and let near-dark take 0.5% of the area at most. Open the range to 60–80 L, with 5–18% near-dark, only for a state whose condition is itself a contrast (hard light, a full house, night).
5. Treat the ambient pair as one mixture: in a quiet state top field and ground share a hue within 16° and rise and fall together, 19–23 L apart. In a hard state they part, about 35 L and 45° of hue. In a dark state the order reverses and the ground is about 16 L lighter than the top field. The order and width of this gap tell the condition before anything is read.
6. State distance one way per state. Either a lightness ramp, with the near structure about 30 L from the far and the far within 7 L of the top field; or, where the lightness is level from near to far, by unit count alone.
7. Make every item from the unit: 2–5 units near, one unit far, on a ladder of heights 11 : 5.5 : 1 (0.11, 0.055 and 0.01 of the frame). No item is larger, finished further or set apart. A group is a change of density and hue across the ground, up to 45% of the view, thinning toward the far point at uneven intervals.
8. Spread the accent: unmixed colour takes 0–3.5% of the area and lies on many items, one unit each, never gathered in one place. A seasonal layer arrives as about 4%, in two masses on either side of the open ground. In a dark state the lit marks are about 1% of the area and are what the state is about. Tell kinds of source by the shape and direction of their unit as well as by hue (a ringed point, an upright bar, a small dab).
9. Build a shaded or lit region from two or three separate hues of one lightness, in shares of about a fifth to a quarter each, that average to the tone wanted; never one darkened mixture and never a neutral dark. A minority temperature of 3–10% of a region is enough to set which way it leans.
10. Let the frame cut the structure, and allow a screen of fixed uprights at uneven gaps of 0.04–0.14 of the width across the largest light mass, so that it is never seen whole. Something outside the frame may show by its effect alone: a band that enters from an edge with its source unseen.
11. Advance every zone together, each at the same finish, and correct each against the others; nothing is left bare and nothing is polished. Keep each finished state and show them as a set of 3–7 on the one scaffold, each inside its own neutral light margin. A state may also be re-keyed whole, about 16 L up or down, with nothing redrawn.
Why it works:
- With the window and the landmarks fixed, the eye adapts to the scaffold and whatever differs between two states belongs to the condition. Because the layers keep different clocks, the difference also sorts itself: both ambient fields moving together reads as the hour, one small layer alone as the season, a change of density as the day.
- Things made of one unit group by likeness of texture into a single surface, so direction and density carry the difference and no border or outline is needed. An item drawn in another manner would be read first whatever its size, and the surface would turn into its background.
- Fine separate units fuse into a tone at a distance. Mixed hues fuse to a quiet middle value that still has a temperature, so a full ground stays calm; a single darkened mixture reads as a hole in the surface.
- In a narrow value band a small step is already an event. Holding the ends of the range in reserve keeps them for the one condition that is a contrast, and a set of states then differs in key as well as in hue.
- A unit's size against a few fixed uprights gives the scale, so distance and quantity are read without perspective lines or counters; an accent laid over many items says where the activity is without naming a winner.
Colour relations: muted mixtures hold most of the area and lean one way per state; the ambient pair is the largest share, one hue within 16° and 19–23 L apart in quiet states; unmixed colour 0–3.5%, scattered, the highest chroma and the smallest form; a seasonal hue about 4%, in two masses; shade and light are sets of two or three hues at one lightness; both ends of the lightness range stay unused except in a contrast state; each state stands in a neutral light margin so that its key is judged alone.
Space and line: one window and one ratio; far point at 0.25–0.37 across, a hard upright at 0.48–0.58, structure cut by the sides; top field 12–32%, ground about half and full edge to edge, with nothing emphasised; the eye travels by density; the few continuous lines are the landmarks and each of them is broken; optional screen of uprights at gaps of 0.04–0.14 of the width.
Type requirement: text is the crisp exception and is never built from the unit. One even-stroke face in one weight, so that a block of text reads as an even tone beside the units; two sizes, 2 : 1, for the near and middle rungs of the ladder, while far items carry no text until called up. Lines run with the direction of their zone. Ink may leave the surface band and reaches 4.5:1 on its surface. Labels anchor to the fixed landmarks, so they hold their place in every state. State name and date sit small in a lower corner, off the grid of the view.
Motion and interaction: on a change of state only the layer whose clock has struck moves, and the landmarks never do. The ambient pair re-keys as one, slowly. The population changes by density: units enter and leave in place, with no travel and no gesture. Pointing at an item raises it one rung (one unit to 2–5 units with its label in ink) and returns it. A strip of the bordered states steps through the series. Loading follows step 11: every zone coarse, then all refined together. No blur, no grain, no shimmer. Reduced motion: swap the state at once.
Fails when: one thing must win or an alarm must be found (the process has no hero and its accent is spread); each item must be identified at a glance (a far item is one unit and carries no name); the content is sparse (a handful of items makes no surface and no density); the view is mainly long text or a table; there is only one state; the layout must differ between states; the brand depends on black on white.
Signature (do not carry together): small separate dabs of unmixed paint over the whole canvas · a street seen from an upper window, narrowing to a far point left of centre · a crowd and its carriages as flecks in grey weather.

## Space & composition
- **An off-centre apex.** The road's vanishing point sits at .25–.37 of the width, the other weight (lamp, tree mass) beyond the centre. Right-hand wall rises 12–15° (25–29° when he turned the view).
- **A V of light.** The sky is a notch, 12–32% of the canvas, narrow at the bottom and wide at the top. The Place du Théâtre Français has none and reads as a plan.
- **Fixed verticals measure the drift.** One lamp post, one or two kiosks, a few trunks: the same objects in every canvas, so every other shift shows.
- **The ground is the event.** From the apex down, about half of the picture (by eye) is road, pavement or crowd, with nothing left empty and nothing emphasised; the eye travels by density.
- **Screens.** Trunks spaced at .04–.14 of the width stand between viewer and houses (Côte des Bœufs; 1879 print): a foreground grid that hides part of everything.
- **Frames he chose.** The letters record his fight for white frames (28 February 1883) and, when a committee refused them, oak with a white margin (17 March 1887): a neutral light border, so the picture's own harmony is not disturbed.

## Form, line & typography
There is almost no drawn line: edges are where touches of one value stop against touches of another. The only continuous lines are the lamp, the trunks, the pavement edge and the rooflines, and each is broken. The stroke has a size (about 0.5–2.5% of the width), a direction and a ridge, and does not vary in kind between a wall and a person. No type appears as design; shop lettering and poster words are scumbled to unreadable marks on kiosks and awnings. The signature with year is small, in a lower corner, and not aligned to the motif. Prints: soft ground gives broken crayon-like strokes, aquatint gives grain; tone replaces contour.

## Rhythm, motion & time
- **Time is the series.** A fixed frame, a sequence of states; the viewer compares. He writes from Rouen (19 October 1883; the OCR misreads the year) that motifs vanish overnight and must be realised "in a single session". In June 1891 he has three canvases going together.
- **The crowd moves by density.** Interval, not gesture: dabs 1–2% wide, thinning toward the apex. Carriages repeat at irregular intervals.
- **Slow finish.** Started on the spot, unified and retouched later in the studio; he valued the impression through memory for its unity (letter of 26 April 1892).
- **Seasonal increments.** Layers change on different clocks: sky and ground by the hour, leaves by the season, crowd by the day, buildings hardly at all.

## UI translations
1. **One window, fixed landmarks.** Freeze the frame, the far point (0.25–0.37 across) and one hard upright (0.48–0.58) for every state; allow about 0.1 of the width of drift and no re-layout.
2. **A clock per layer.** Name the layers (ambient pair, seasonal layer, population, structure) and let a state change only the layers whose clock has struck, so that the kind of change is legible.
3. **One unit for everything.** Build zones and items from one cell or tick of 0.5–2.5% of the width; change its direction per zone, never its kind; remove outlines and dividers.
4. **Narrow band, opened on purpose.** Quiet states hold surfaces to 30–65 L with no near-dark; only a state that is itself a contrast (outage, peak, night) opens to 60–80 L. Text ink is exempt and reaches 4.5:1.
5. **The ambient pair moves as one.** Header field and ground share a hue within 16° and sit 19–23 L apart in quiet states; they part by about 35 L in a hard state and swap order in a dark one.
6. **Items at the size of the unit.** Near items 2–5 units with text, far items one unit, on an 11 : 5.5 : 1 ladder; pointing raises an item one rung. No hero item.
7. **Quantity as density.** Show a crowd, a backlog or a load as a denser stretch of the ground, up to 45% of the view, with the exact figure in ink on a landmark.
8. **Scatter the accent.** Unmixed colour is 0–3.5% of the view, one unit on each of many items, never one banner; a new category arrives at about 4% in two masses.
9. **Kinds by shape as well as hue.** Give each kind of source its own unit shape and direction (ringed point, upright bar, small dab), so that the kinds survive without colour.
10. **Shade as a mix.** A shaded, paused or selected region takes two or three hues at its own lightness, about a fifth to a quarter of the units each; nothing is dimmed, greyed or overlaid.
11. **Cut and screen.** Let structure run off the frame, cross the largest light panel with a few fixed uprights at uneven gaps (0.04–0.14 of the width), and show off-screen causes as a labelled band entering from the edge.
12. **All zones together.** Load every zone coarse, then refine all at once; no skeleton beside a finished panel and no panel more polished than its neighbours.
13. **A bordered set.** Keep 3–7 states as small copies of the same frame, each in a neutral light margin, and offer a whole-view re-key of about 16 L as an alternative state.

## What this artist would reject
- A finished outline that is then coloured in.
- One hero figure or accent, with the rest as background.
- Polishing one part to completion while another is blank.
- Black shadow, or any single mixed colour for a shadow.
- A different layout for each condition: he moved the light, not the room.
- A mechanical dot kept for its own sake: in 1891 he wrote that he had abandoned it because it weakened the sensation.
- Motif for its own sake (a letter quoted by the Thyssen: motifs are secondary, "the atmosphere and the effects" are the interest).

## Signature prompt seed
Fix one window, an off-centre far point and a few hard landmarks, and keep them in every state. Build every zone and every item from one small crisp unit, changing only its direction and density; draw no outlines and name no hero. Sort the view into layers with their own clocks and let each state move only the layer whose clock has struck. Key each state inside a narrow value band with no neutral dark, opening the range only when the condition is itself a contrast. Spread a very small accent over many items, tell kinds apart by unit shape as well as hue, and build shade from two or three hues of one lightness. Set exact figures and labels in crisp ink on the landmarks. Show the states side by side, each in a neutral light margin.
